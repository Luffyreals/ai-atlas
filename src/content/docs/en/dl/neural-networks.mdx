## Intuition

### The staircase light

Your staircase has two switches: one at the bottom of the stairs, one at the top. Flipping either switch toggles the light: flip the bottom switch on your way up, then flip the top switch when you reach the landing. Recording each switch position as 0 or 1 so that both at 0 means the light is off gives this truth table:

| Bottom switch $x_1$ | Top switch $x_2$ | Light $y$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 0 (off) |
| 0 | 1 | 1 (on) |
| 1 | 0 | 1 (on) |
| 1 | 1 | 0 (off) |

The light turns on when **exactly one** switch is at 1. This logical operation is called **XOR** (exclusive or).

### Trying a "sum-and-threshold" rule

The simplest way for a machine to make a decision from two numbers $x_1, x_2$ is to assign each a weight, sum them up, and compare the total against a threshold. If the score meets the threshold, the light turns on. For several familiar logic gates, this works:

- **AND** (on when both are flipped): weights 1 and 1, threshold 1.5. Only $(1, 1)$ reaches a score of 2.
- **OR** (on when at least one is flipped): weights 1 and 1, threshold 0.5.

With XOR, no matter how you tune the numbers, it fails. To make $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ turn on, each weight alone must reach the threshold. To keep $(0, 0)$ off, the threshold must be positive. But then $(1, 1)$ gets a score equal to the sum of both weights — even larger — so it turns on too. Geometrically, if you plot the four points at the corners of a unit square, the two "on" points sit on one diagonal and the two "off" points sit on the other. A "sum-and-threshold" rule splits the plane with a **single straight line**, and no single line can separate the two diagonals of a square.

### Combining two rules

Stated in plain words, XOR is easy: the light is on when "**at least one** switch is on" **and** "**not both** switches are on". The first clause is OR; the second is "not AND". Each clause is a simple sum-and-threshold rule. Add a third rule that takes the outputs of the first two, and you have XOR. That is the core idea of a multilayer neural network: simple units arranged in layers, where each layer builds on the outputs of the layer before it.

The goal of this lesson is to **stack simple "sum-and-decide" neurons into multiple layers so the network can represent rules a single neuron cannot — like the staircase light — and write the entire computation cleanly in matrix form so it scales to real tasks like reading handwritten digits.**

## Theory

### The artificial neuron

A biological neuron receives signals from many other neurons. When the combined stimulation is strong enough, it fires an impulse down its axon. McCulloch and Pitts (1943) modeled this very crudely: take a weighted sum of the inputs and compare it to a threshold. Modern **neural networks** keep only that mathematical skeleton; they do not simulate the biological brain.

An **artificial neuron** takes a vector $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, \dots, x_n]^\top$ of $n$ inputs and performs two steps:

$$
z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j x_j + b, \qquad a = g(z)
$$

- $\mathbf{w}$ is the **weight** vector and $b$ is the **bias**. The bias plays the role of "minus the threshold": "score $\geq$ threshold $\theta$" is identical to "$z \geq 0$" with $b = -\theta$.
- $z$ is the weighted sum, also called the **pre-activation**.
- $g$ is the **activation function**, and $a$ is the neuron's output, called the **activation**.

You have already met this neuron. With $g(z) = z$, it is linear regression. With $g = \sigma$, it is logistic regression. The set of points where $z = 0$ is a line when $n = 2$ and a **hyperplane** in higher dimensions: that is the neuron's **decision boundary**.

### The perceptron and the perceptron learning rule

The **perceptron** (Rosenblatt, 1958) is a neuron whose activation function is the **step function**:

$$
\mathrm{step}(z) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } z \geq 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } z < 0 \end{cases}
\qquad\qquad
\hat{y} = \mathrm{step}(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b)
$$

Rosenblatt also gave a rule for learning $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ from labeled data $y \in \{0, 1\}$. Loop over each sample, compute $\hat{y}$, and update:

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \eta\,(y - \hat{y})\,\mathbf{x}, \qquad b \leftarrow b + \eta\,(y - \hat{y})
$$

Because $y - \hat{y}$ takes only three possible values, this rule breaks down into three cases:

- **Correct prediction** ($y - \hat{y} = 0$): change nothing.
- **Predicted 0 when true label is 1** ($y - \hat{y} = 1$): add $\eta\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{w}$ and $\eta$ to $b$. Recomputing $z$ on this same sample with the new parameters gives $(\mathbf{w} + \eta\mathbf{x})^\top\mathbf{x} + (b + \eta) = z + \eta\left(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2 + 1\right)$. Thus $z$ increases, pushing the sample toward "1".
- **Predicted 1 when true label is 0** ($y - \hat{y} = -1$): subtract instead, reducing $z$ by the same amount and pushing the sample toward "0".

For example, take AND with $\eta = 1$, starting from $\mathbf{w} = [0, 0]^\top$, $b = 0$. In the first epoch:

| Sample $(x_1, x_2)$ | $y$ | $z$ | $\hat{y}$ | Updated to |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| $(0, 0)$ | 0 | 0 | 1 | wrong: $\mathbf{w} = [0, 0]^\top$, $b = -1$ |
| $(0, 1)$ | 0 | $-1$ | 0 | right, unchanged |
| $(1, 0)$ | 0 | $-1$ | 0 | right, unchanged |
| $(1, 1)$ | 1 | $-1$ | 0 | wrong: $\mathbf{w} = [1, 1]^\top$, $b = 0$ |

This epoch makes 2 errors. Repeating until an entire epoch makes zero errors, the first code cell stops at epoch 6.

**When does the learning rule stop?** A dataset is **linearly separable** if there exists a hyperplane that classifies every sample of both classes correctly. The **perceptron convergence theorem** (Novikoff, 1962) states: if the data is linearly separable, the perceptron learning rule makes at most a finite number of updates and then stops, regardless of sample order. If the data is not linearly separable, it loops forever as the weights cycle back and forth.

### Why a single perceptron cannot solve XOR

The Intuition section gave the geometric picture; now let us prove it algebraically. Suppose there exist $w_1, w_2, b$ such that a perceptron classifies all four XOR samples correctly. With the step function, $\hat{y} = 1$ when $z \geq 0$ and $\hat{y} = 0$ when $z < 0$. The four samples impose four inequalities:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(0, 0) \to 0:&\quad b < 0 \\
(0, 1) \to 1:&\quad w_2 + b \geq 0 \\
(1, 0) \to 1:&\quad w_1 + b \geq 0 \\
(1, 1) \to 0:&\quad w_1 + w_2 + b < 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding the second and third inequalities gives $w_1 + w_2 + 2b \geq 0$. Moving one $b$ to the right-hand side yields $w_1 + w_2 + b \geq -b$. By the first inequality, $b < 0$, so $-b > 0$. Hence $w_1 + w_2 + b > 0$, which directly contradicts the fourth inequality. No parameters can satisfy all four: **XOR is not linearly separable**.

Replacing the step function with a sigmoid and predicting "class 1 when $\sigma(z) \geq 0.5$" does not save it either, because $\sigma(z) \geq 0.5$ holds precisely when $z \geq 0$: the decision boundary is still the straight line $z = 0$. In 1969, Minsky and Papert's book *Perceptrons* analyzed such limitations of single-layer perceptrons in detail, contributing to a decade-long chill in neural network research.

### Multilayer perceptrons

Let us return to "combining two rules". Use two perceptrons in the first layer and one perceptron in the second layer:

- $h_1 = \mathrm{step}(x_1 + x_2 - 0.5)$: equals 1 when at least one switch is on (OR).
- $h_2 = \mathrm{step}(x_1 + x_2 - 1.5)$: equals 1 when both switches are on (AND).
- $\hat{y} = \mathrm{step}(h_1 - h_2 - 0.5)$: equals 1 when $h_1 = 1$ and $h_2 = 0$.

Checking all four inputs:

| $(x_1, x_2)$ | $(h_1, h_2)$ | $h_1 - h_2 - 0.5$ | $\hat{y}$ | XOR |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| $(0, 0)$ | $(0, 0)$ | $-0.5$ | 0 | 0 |
| $(0, 1)$ | $(1, 0)$ | $0.5$ | 1 | 1 |
| $(1, 0)$ | $(1, 0)$ | $0.5$ | 1 | 1 |
| $(1, 1)$ | $(1, 1)$ | $-0.5$ | 0 | 0 |

Look at the $(h_1, h_2)$ column. The first layer has **transformed the representation** of the data: the two "on" inputs both map to the same point $(1, 0)$, while the two "off" inputs map to $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$. In the $(h_1, h_2)$ plane, these three points are linearly separable, and the output neuron simply draws a line between them. That is what a multilayer network can do that a single neuron cannot: early layers **learn new features**, and the final layer solves an easier problem in that feature space.

Such a network is called a **multilayer perceptron (MLP)**:

- The **input layer** simply holds the raw inputs $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and performs no computation.
- The **hidden layers** sit in between. They are called "hidden" because the training data does not specify what values their neurons should output.
- The **output layer** produces the prediction $\hat{y}$.

Every neuron receives inputs from **every** neuron in the immediately preceding layer, so each such layer is called a **fully connected layer** (or **dense layer**). Information flows strictly forward from input to output with no loops, so an MLP is also called a **feed-forward network**. The network above is written compactly as 2-2-1 (neurons per layer) and is called a **2-layer** network: by standard convention, we do not count the input layer.

The name "multilayer perceptron" is a historical holdover. Modern MLPs do not use step functions; they use smooth activation functions, for reasons explained in the Activation functions section below.

### Matrix form of forward propagation

With 2 inputs, writing out each neuron by hand is fine. The digit-recognition task in the third code cell has 64 inputs and 64 hidden neurons: we need a more compact notation.

**Notation.** Number the layers $l = 0, 1, \dots, N_L$, where layer 0 is the input layer and layer $N_L$ is the output layer. Layer $l$ has $n^{[l]}$ neurons; the input layer has $n^{[0]} = n$, the number of input features. A bracketed superscript $[l]$ indexes the layer, distinct from $(i)$ which indexes a sample.

**One layer.** The $k$-th neuron of layer $l$ has a weight vector $\mathbf{w}^{[l]}_k$ (of length $n^{[l-1]}$) and a bias $b^{[l]}_k$. It takes the activation vector $\mathbf{a}^{[l-1]}$ from the previous layer and computes $z^{[l]}_k = \left(\mathbf{w}^{[l]}_k\right)^\top\mathbf{a}^{[l-1]} + b^{[l]}_k$. Stack the row vectors $\left(\mathbf{w}^{[l]}_k\right)^\top$ as the **rows** of a matrix $\mathbf{W}^{[l]}$, and stack the biases $b^{[l]}_k$ into a column vector $\mathbf{b}^{[l]}$. Matrix-vector multiplication computes all dot products at once, one per row, so the entire layer fits in two lines:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{z}^{[l]} &= \mathbf{W}^{[l]}\mathbf{a}^{[l-1]} + \mathbf{b}^{[l]} \\
\mathbf{a}^{[l]} &= g^{[l]}\!\left(\mathbf{z}^{[l]}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

The activation function $g^{[l]}$ is applied **elementwise** to $\mathbf{z}^{[l]}$. Hidden layers usually share the same function $g$; the output layer often uses a different one (see Output layers below).

**Shape check.** $\mathbf{W}^{[l]}$ has $n^{[l]}$ rows (one per neuron in layer $l$) and $n^{[l-1]}$ columns (one per input from layer $l-1$):

$$
\underbrace{\mathbf{z}^{[l]}}_{n^{[l]} \times 1} = \underbrace{\mathbf{W}^{[l]}}_{n^{[l]} \times n^{[l-1]}}\ \underbrace{\mathbf{a}^{[l-1]}}_{n^{[l-1]} \times 1} + \underbrace{\mathbf{b}^{[l]}}_{n^{[l]} \times 1}
$$

**The whole network.** Start with $\mathbf{a}^{[0]} = \mathbf{x}$, apply the two equations above for $l = 1, 2, \dots, N_L$, and read off the prediction $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{a}^{[N_L]}$. Computing layer by layer from input to output is called **forward propagation** (or the **forward pass**).

Our XOR network writes out as:

$$
\mathbf{W}^{[1]} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{b}^{[1]} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 \\ -1.5 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{W}^{[2]} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{b}^{[2]} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 \end{bmatrix}
$$

For $\mathbf{x} = [1, 0]^\top$: $\mathbf{z}^{[1]} = [1 - 0.5,\ 1 - 1.5]^\top = [0.5, -0.5]^\top$, which passes through the step function to give $\mathbf{a}^{[1]} = [1, 0]^\top$. Next, $z^{[2]} = 1 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 0 - 0.5 = 0.5$, giving $\hat{y} = 1$ — matching the third row of our table.

**Batching multiple samples at once.** Stack $m$ samples as the $m$ **columns** of a matrix $\mathbf{A}^{[0]} = \left[\mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{x}^{(m)}\right]$ of shape $n \times m$. The $i$-th column of $\mathbf{W}^{[l]}\mathbf{A}^{[l-1]}$ is $\mathbf{W}^{[l]}$ times the $i$-th column of $\mathbf{A}^{[l-1]}$, so a single matrix multiplication runs forward propagation for all $m$ samples simultaneously:

$$
\mathbf{Z}^{[l]} = \mathbf{W}^{[l]}\mathbf{A}^{[l-1]} + \mathbf{b}^{[l]}, \qquad \mathbf{A}^{[l]} = g^{[l]}\!\left(\mathbf{Z}^{[l]}\right)
$$

Here $\mathbf{b}^{[l]}$ is added to **every column** via NumPy broadcasting. The second code cell implements this exact calculation. Computers multiply matrices extraordinarily fast — especially on GPUs — so matrix notation is not just tidy algebra; it is how neural networks are actually executed.

**Parameter count.** Layer $l$ has $n^{[l]} \times n^{[l-1]}$ weights and $n^{[l]}$ biases, for a total of $n^{[l]}\left(n^{[l-1]} + 1\right)$ parameters. The 64-64-10 MLP in the third code cell has $64 \times 65 + 10 \times 65 = 4160 + 650 = 4810$ parameters.

### Activation functions

**Why they must be nonlinear.** Suppose we drop the activation function in the hidden layer, setting $g(z) = z$. Substituting $\mathbf{a}^{[1]} = \mathbf{W}^{[1]}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{[1]}$ into the second layer gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{z}^{[2]} &= \mathbf{W}^{[2]}\left(\mathbf{W}^{[1]}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{[1]}\right) + \mathbf{b}^{[2]} \\
&= \left(\mathbf{W}^{[2]}\mathbf{W}^{[1]}\right)\mathbf{x} + \left(\mathbf{W}^{[2]}\mathbf{b}^{[1]} + \mathbf{b}^{[2]}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line distributes $\mathbf{W}^{[2]}$ and regroups terms. Setting $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}^{[2]}\mathbf{W}^{[1]}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{W}^{[2]}\mathbf{b}^{[1]} + \mathbf{b}^{[2]}$, the two layers collapse into a **single** linear layer $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$. By induction, stacking any number of linear layers is no more expressive than a single linear layer, and still cannot solve XOR. Nonlinear activation functions are what allow hidden layers to fold and warp the input space — just as the XOR hidden layer merged the two "on" corners onto a single point.

**Common activation functions.** Here are their values at several sample points (Leaky ReLU shown with $\alpha = 0.01$):

| Function | $z = -2$ | $z = -0.5$ | $z = 0$ | $z = 0.5$ | $z = 2$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Step | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Sigmoid $\sigma$ | 0.119 | 0.378 | 0.5 | 0.622 | 0.881 |
| tanh | −0.964 | −0.462 | 0 | 0.462 | 0.964 |
| ReLU | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0.5 | 2 |
| Leaky ReLU | −0.02 | −0.005 | 0 | 0.5 | 2 |
| GELU | −0.046 | −0.154 | 0 | 0.346 | 1.954 |

- **Step function.** Flat everywhere except at the jump at 0, so its derivative is 0 everywhere it is differentiable. Gradient descent needs a nonzero slope to know which way to nudge parameters, so a multilayer network with step functions cannot be trained by gradient descent. The perceptron learning rule only works for a single layer because only the output layer has ground-truth labels to compare against.
- **Sigmoid** $\sigma(z) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$, with values in $(0, 1)$. Smooth, but it **saturates** when $\lvert z\rvert$ is large: the curve flattens out and its derivative approaches 0. Today sigmoid is mainly used at the output layer of binary classification and inside the gates of GRUs and LSTMs.
- **tanh** $\tanh(z) = \dfrac{e^{z} - e^{-z}}{e^{z} + e^{-z}} = 2\sigma(2z) - 1$, with values in $(-1, 1)$. A rescaled, shifted sigmoid symmetric around 0, so its outputs stay centered near 0. It still saturates in both tails. Often used in recurrent networks.
- **ReLU** (rectified linear unit) $\mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$. Cheap to compute, does not saturate for positive values, and has been the default choice for hidden layers since the early 2010s (Nair & Hinton, 2010; Glorot et al., 2011). Weakness: for $z < 0$, both its output and derivative are 0. If a neuron gets pushed into $z < 0$ for every training sample, its weights never receive a gradient again — a phenomenon called **dying ReLU**.
- **Leaky ReLU** $\max(\alpha z, z)$ with a small $\alpha$, typically $0.01$ (Maas et al., 2013). Keeps a small nonzero slope on the negative side so neurons cannot die completely.
- **GELU** (Gaussian error linear unit, Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2016) $\mathrm{GELU}(z) = z\,\Phi(z)$, where $\Phi$ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution (the probability that a standard normal variable is at most $z$). For large positive $z$, $\Phi(z) \approx 1$ so GELU behaves like $z$; for large negative $z$, $\Phi(z) \approx 0$ so GELU approaches 0. Near 0, the curve bends smoothly and dips slightly negative, reaching about $-0.17$ at $z \approx -0.75$. GELU is the standard activation in BERT and GPT; you will meet it again inside the Transformer MLP block in [Build Your First LLM](/en/llm/build-first-llm/) and [The LLaMA Family](/en/llm/llama-family/).

No single activation function wins on every task. A safe starting point: **ReLU for hidden layers** in MLPs and CNNs, GELU for Transformers, and tanh/sigmoid inside recurrent gates. [Training Neural Networks](/en/dl/training-neural-networks/) explains why sigmoid saturation makes deep networks hard to train.

### Output layers and loss functions

Hidden layers learn features; the output layer turns those features into the exact type of prediction your task requires. The three standard pairings are:

| Task | Output layer | Loss function |
|---|---|---|
| Regression (predict a real number) | 1 neuron, $g(z) = z$ | MSE |
| Binary classification | 1 neuron, sigmoid | Binary cross-entropy |
| $K$-class classification | $K$ neurons, softmax | Cross-entropy |

**Softmax.** The output layer has $K$ neurons producing $K$ logits $z_1, \dots, z_K$. Softmax converts them into a probability distribution:

$$
\hat{y}_k = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}}
$$

Exponentiation makes every number positive, dividing by the sum normalizes the total to 1, and larger logits receive larger probabilities. Unlike the elementwise activations above, softmax is **not** computed independently per neuron: each $\hat{y}_k$ depends on all $K$ logits. For example, $\mathbf{z} = [2.0, 1.0, 0.1]^\top$ gives $\hat{\mathbf{y}} \approx [0.659, 0.242, 0.099]^\top$.

**Cross-entropy.** For a sample whose true class is $c$, the loss is $-\ln$ of the probability the network assigned to class $c$. Representing the label as a **one-hot vector** $\mathbf{y}$ (1 at index $c$, 0 elsewhere), the average loss over $m$ samples is:

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{K} y^{(i)}_k \ln \hat{y}^{(i)}_k
$$

In the inner sum over $k$, only the true class term survives (all other $y^{(i)}_k$ are 0), so each sample contributes $-\ln \hat{y}^{(i)}_{c}$. In the example above, if the true class is class 1, the loss is $-\ln 0.659 \approx 0.417$; if the true class is class 3, the loss is $-\ln 0.099 \approx 2.317$. Confident correct predictions give loss near 0; confident wrong predictions incur a huge penalty. When $K = 2$ with a single sigmoid output $\hat{y} = P(y = 1)$, this reduces to binary cross-entropy: $-\frac{1}{m}\sum_i\left[y^{(i)}\ln\hat{y}^{(i)} + \left(1 - y^{(i)}\right)\ln\left(1 - \hat{y}^{(i)}\right)\right]$.

Stepping back to view the whole network: a softmax output layer paired with cross-entropy **is literally softmax regression**, except its input is no longer the raw vector $\mathbf{x}$, but $\mathbf{a}^{[N_L - 1]}$ — the features learned by the hidden layers.

### The universal approximation theorem

The XOR network needed just one hidden layer. How far can a single hidden layer go? The **universal approximation theorem** answers: with a suitable nonlinear activation function, an MLP with **one hidden layer** and **enough neurons** can approximate any continuous function on a closed and bounded domain to any desired accuracy. Cybenko (1989) proved this for sigmoid; Hornik, Stinchcombe, and White (1989) extended it to a broader class of activations; and Leshno et al. (1993) showed that any continuous non-polynomial activation works — including ReLU.

We will not prove the theorem formally, but the intuition with ReLU is easy to see. A single ReLU neuron is a hinge that bends at one point. The difference $\mathrm{ReLU}(z) - \mathrm{ReLU}(z - 1)$ forms a ramp that rises from 0 to 1 and then stays flat. By adding many such ramps placed at different locations and scaled to different heights, you can stitch together a piecewise linear curve that hugs any continuous curve as closely as you like. More neurons mean more segments and a tighter fit.

Pay close attention to what the theorem **does not** say:

- **It does not say how many neurons are needed.** For many functions, a single hidden layer requires an exponentially large number of neurons. In practice, **deep** networks represent the same functions with far fewer parameters than wide, shallow networks.
- **It does not say how to find the weights.** The theorem only guarantees that a good set of weights **exists**. Whether gradient descent can find them is a separate question.
- **It says nothing about unseen data.** Fitting the training set arbitrarily well can easily result in [overfitting](/en/ml/overfitting-underfitting/).

### What is still missing: learning the weights

We set the XOR weights by hand. The digit classifier has 4810 parameters, and modern networks have millions to billions: setting weights by hand is impossible. The roadmap forward is clear: pick a loss function $\mathcal{L}$ (cross-entropy) and use [gradient descent](/en/ml/gradient-descent/) to minimize it. That requires $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{W}^{[l]}$ and $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{b}^{[l]}$ for **every** layer, where each parameter influences $\mathcal{L}$ through all subsequent layers. The algorithm that computes all of these gradients efficiently is **backpropagation**, the subject of [Training Neural Networks](/en/dl/training-neural-networks/).

Furthermore, an MLP treats every input feature as an independent slot: it has no built-in notion that two pixels sit next to each other in an image or that two words appear adjacent in a sentence. [Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)](/en/dl/cnn/) and [Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN)](/en/dl/rnn/) build spatial and sequential structure directly into the network architecture.

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Forgetting the activation function between linear layers]
`nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.Linear(64, 10))` looks like a two-layer network, but as we proved in the Activation functions section, it collapses into a **single** linear layer — softmax regression with redundant parameters. The code runs without complaint and the loss still goes down, raising no error. Always place a nonlinear activation like `nn.ReLU()` between consecutive `nn.Linear` layers (except after the final output layer — see the next box).
:::

:::caution[Adding softmax before `nn.CrossEntropyLoss`]
PyTorch's `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` expects raw **logits** and applies log-softmax internally for numerical stability. If your network already ends with `nn.Softmax`, softmax gets applied twice. The second softmax receives probabilities restricted to $[0, 1]$, so even when the network is 100% confident, the highest probability after the second softmax is at most $e/(e + 9) \approx 0.232$ for 10 classes, and the loss cannot drop below about 1.46. Gradients also shrink, slowing training dramatically. Return raw logits from your final layer; call `torch.softmax(logits, dim=1)` separately only when you need human-readable probabilities, or take `argmax` directly on the logits to get the predicted class.
:::

:::caution[Reading the universal approximation theorem as "one hidden layer is all you need"]
The theorem says such a network **exists**, not that it is small, not that gradient descent will find it, and not that it will generalize to new data. Widening a single hidden layer also does nothing to encode the spatial grid of an image or the sequential order of a sentence. That is why practitioners use deep networks, CNNs for vision, and Transformers for sequences, even though a single hidden layer is theoretically "universal".
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- An artificial neuron computes $z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$ and $a = g(z)$. The perceptron uses a step function and the update rule $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \eta(y - \hat{y})\mathbf{x}$; this rule converges only on linearly separable data, so it cannot learn XOR.
- An MLP stacks neurons into fully connected layers. Forward propagation starts at $\mathbf{a}^{[0]} = \mathbf{x}$ and iterates $\mathbf{z}^{[l]} = \mathbf{W}^{[l]}\mathbf{a}^{[l-1]} + \mathbf{b}^{[l]}$, $\mathbf{a}^{[l]} = g^{[l]}(\mathbf{z}^{[l]})$, where $\mathbf{W}^{[l]}$ has shape $n^{[l]} \times n^{[l-1]}$. Hidden layers learn new representations where the task becomes linearly separable.
- Activation functions must be nonlinear, or the entire network collapses into a single linear layer. ReLU is the default for hidden layers; sigmoid and tanh saturate in the tails; Leaky ReLU avoids dying neurons; GELU is standard in Transformers.
- $K$-class classification pairs $K$ output neurons with softmax and cross-entropy loss $-\ln\hat{y}_c$. The universal approximation theorem guarantees that a sufficiently wide one-hidden-layer MLP can approximate any continuous function, but leaves finding the weights to backpropagation in the next lesson.
