## Intuition

### Three bottlenecks of reading word by word

Read the sentence below and answer: what does "it" refer to?

> The cat that our neighbor adopted last winter climbed onto the roof because **it** was afraid of the dog.

You know immediately that "it" is the cat, even though more than ten words sit between them. Your eyes jump straight from "it" back to "cat." An LSTM cannot jump. It has to march through every word in between, hitting three bottlenecks:

1. **Sequential computation.** To compute $\mathbf{h}_{10}$, you must first finish $\mathbf{h}_9$. A 1,000-token passage requires 1,000 sequential steps, leaving thousands of GPU cores waiting idly.
2. **Long path length.** Information about "cat" has to survive more than ten state updates to reach "it," and backward gradients must traverse the same chain in reverse. Between two tokens separated by $L$ steps, the path length is $O(L)$.
3. **One fixed-size vector for the entire past.** Everything read so far must be squeezed into a single vector $\mathbf{h}_t$ of fixed dimension. The longer the sentence, the more crowded that vector becomes.

The gates of GRUs and LSTMs ease the second bottleneck, but they cannot remove the first or the third.

### Soft lookup

Now try a radical alternative: let **every word look directly at every other word** in the sentence in a single step. The word "it" broadcasts a question: "who in this sentence is an animate subject that can feel afraid?" Every word holds up a tag describing itself: "cat: animate noun, subject," "roof: inanimate object, location." Whichever tag matches the question best gets the largest share of attention.

Think of this as a **soft dictionary lookup**. A Python dictionary `d["cat"]` performs a hard lookup: the key must match exactly, and you retrieve a single value. A soft lookup computes a **compatibility score** between the query and every key, then returns a **weighted average** of all values—the better a key matches, the larger its weight. Because the entire operation is built out of matrix multiplications and softmax, it is fully differentiable and end-to-end trainable by backpropagation.

In self-attention, every word plays all three roles at once: asking a question (query), holding up a descriptive tag (key), and offering content to be retrieved (value). Because all words ask their questions simultaneously, the entire sentence is processed **in parallel**. One catch remains: if we only compare tags, the network has no idea which word came first and which came last—"dog bites cat" and "cat bites dog" look identical. We will need to inject position information explicitly.

The problem for this lesson is: **build a neural network layer where every token attends directly to every other token in one parallel step via learnable soft lookup, while still knowing token order and—when generating text—never peeking at future tokens.**

## Theory

### From RNNs to attention

Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio (2014) first introduced attention for machine translation: at each decoding step, an RNN decoder took a weighted average of all encoder hidden states instead of relying on a single final vector ([Sequence-to-Sequence Models (Seq2Seq)](/en/llm/seq2seq/)). At that point, attention was still an add-on to an RNN. Vaswani et al. (2017) took the next leap: drop the RNN completely and build the entire model out of attention. Their paper title said it plainly: *Attention Is All You Need*. That architecture is the **Transformer**.

### Scaled dot-product attention

Start with a **single** query. We have:

- one **query** $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$: what this position is looking for;
- $L$ **keys** $\mathbf{k}_1, \dots, \mathbf{k}_L \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$: the descriptive label of each position;
- $L$ **values** $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_L \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v}$: the content offered by each position.

Here $L$ is the sequence length and $d_k$ is the **key dimension**. Queries and keys must share the same dimension $d_k$ because we compare them via a dot product. Attention proceeds in three steps.

**Step 1: score compatibility.** The similarity between the query and key $j$ is their dot product divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$ (we derive the $\sqrt{d_k}$ factor in the next subsection):

$$
s_j = \frac{\mathbf{q}^\top \mathbf{k}_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}, \qquad j = 1, \dots, L
$$

The dot product is positive when two vectors point in similar directions and zero or negative when they point apart, making it the simplest measure of alignment.

**Step 2: turn scores into weights.** Applying softmax across $j = 1, \dots, L$ produces non-negative weights that sum to 1:

$$
\alpha_j = \frac{\exp(s_j)}{\sum_{l=1}^{L} \exp(s_l)}
$$

**Step 3: mix the values.** The output vector is the weighted average of the value vectors:

$$
\mathbf{o} = \sum_{j=1}^{L} \alpha_j \mathbf{v}_j
$$

Now run this for all $L$ queries at once. Stack the $L$ queries as the rows of a matrix $\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d_k}$, the keys into $\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d_k}$, and the values into $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d_v}$. Because entry $(i, j)$ of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ is $\mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{k}_j$, all three steps compact into a single matrix equation:

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V}
$$

- $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ is the $L \times L$ score matrix: row $i$ holds the compatibility scores of query $i$ against all $L$ keys.
- Softmax is applied **row-wise**, so every row of the attention weight matrix $\mathbf{A}$ sums to 1.
- $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}$ has shape $L \times d_v$: row $i$ is $\mathbf{o}_i$, the output for position $i$.

The entire operation consists of two matrix multiplications and a row-wise softmax—no loop over time steps, so all $L$ positions run in parallel on a GPU.

### Why divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$

Suppose the components $q_r$ and $k_r$ of a query and key are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1 (the exact assumption analyzed in a footnote of Vaswani et al., 2017). Their dot product is a sum of $d_k$ terms:

$$
\mathbf{q}^\top \mathbf{k} = \sum_{r=1}^{d_k} q_r k_r
$$

Consider a single product term $q_r k_r$. By independence, its mean is $\mathbb{E}[q_r]\,\mathbb{E}[k_r] = 0$, and its variance is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Var}(q_r k_r) &= \mathbb{E}[q_r^2 k_r^2] - \left(\mathbb{E}[q_r k_r]\right)^2 \\
&= \mathbb{E}[q_r^2]\,\mathbb{E}[k_r^2] - 0 \\
&= 1 \cdot 1 = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is the definition of variance; the second factors the expectation of the product using independence and zero mean; the third uses $\mathbb{E}[q_r^2] = \mathrm{Var}(q_r) + (\mathbb{E}[q_r])^2 = 1$.

Because the $d_k$ terms are independent, the variance of their sum is the sum of their variances: $\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{q}^\top\mathbf{k}) = d_k$, giving a standard deviation of $\sqrt{d_k}$. When $d_k = 64$, raw dot products routinely spread across $\pm 8$, where $e^{8}/e^{-8}$ is nearly nine million. Softmax then puts almost all weight on the single largest score and enters a flat saturation region where its gradients are tiny, slowing training to a crawl. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ rescales the variance back to 1 regardless of $d_k$:

$$
\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}^\top \mathbf{k}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) = \frac{d_k}{d_k} = 1
$$

The second code cell verifies this variance law empirically.

### Self-attention: Q, K, V from the same sequence

In **self-attention**, the queries, keys, and values all come from the same input sequence $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$, where row $i$ is the representation of token $i$ and $d$ is the **model dimension**. Three learnable projection matrices $\mathbf{W}^Q, \mathbf{W}^K \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_k}$ and $\mathbf{W}^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_v}$ produce:

$$
\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^Q, \qquad \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^K, \qquad \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^V
$$

Why not set $\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}$ directly? Because what a token searches for (its query) is different from how it advertises itself to others (its key) and what information it hands over when selected (its value). In our earlier sentence, "it" needs a query that asks "who is an animate subject?", whereas "cat" needs a key that says "I am an animate subject." Separate projection matrices let the model learn these three roles independently. And because every position shares the same $\mathbf{W}^Q$, $\mathbf{W}^K$, and $\mathbf{W}^V$, the parameter count does not depend on sequence length $L$.

With self-attention, "it" reaches "cat" in **one** hop no matter how many words sit between them: the path length between any two positions is $O(1)$ instead of $O(L)$.

### Multi-head attention

A single attention computation gives each position **one** weighted average. Yet a word usually needs to track several relationships at once: "it" may need to find its noun antecedent, check the tense of the governing verb, and look at the immediately preceding word. Forcing all of those into a single attention distribution averages their signals together.

**Multi-head attention** runs $h$ attention operations (**heads**) in parallel, each with its own projection matrices, and concatenates their outputs:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{head}_i &= \mathrm{Attention}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_i^Q, \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_i^K, \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_i^V), \quad i = 1, \dots, h \\
\mathrm{MultiHead}(\mathbf{X}) &= \mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{head}_1, \dots, \mathrm{head}_h)\,\mathbf{W}^O
\end{aligned}
$$

- Each head projects down to $d_k = d_v = d/h$ dimensions. Concatenating all $h$ heads restores dimension $d$, and the output projection $\mathbf{W}^O \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ mixes information across heads.
- Because each head operates in its own $d/h$-dimensional subspace, different heads can specialize in different linguistic patterns (coreference, local syntax, adjacent tokens) without interfering with one another.
- Because each head is $h$ times narrower, the total compute across all $h$ heads is roughly the same as a single full-$d$ attention head.

The base Transformer in Vaswani et al. (2017) uses $d = 512$ and $h = 8$, so $d_k = 64$. In code, we never loop over heads one by one: we multiply $\mathbf{X}$ by a single $(d, d)$ matrix and reshape the $(L, d)$ result into $(h, L, d_k)$ so all heads run in one batched matrix multiply.

### Positional encoding

Self-attention by itself is blind to word order. If you swap two rows of $\mathbf{X}$, the corresponding rows of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ swap, the rows and columns of the score matrix swap, and the output rows swap in the exact same way—nothing else changes. Formally, self-attention is **permutation-equivariant**. For a token whose own position stays put—like "bites" in "dog bites cat" vs. "cat bites dog"—the output vector is completely unchanged. The third code cell tests this directly.

To break this symmetry, we add to each token embedding a **positional encoding** vector $\mathbf{p}_{\text{pos}} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ that depends only on its position index $\text{pos} \in \{0, 1, \dots, L-1\}$. The original Transformer uses parameter-free **sinusoidal positional encoding**:

$$
p_{\text{pos},\,2i} = \sin\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{2i/d}}\right), \qquad
p_{\text{pos},\,2i+1} = \cos\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{2i/d}}\right)
$$

where $i = 0, 1, \dots, d/2 - 1$ indexes pairs of dimensions. Reading the formula:

- **Each dimension pair is a clock hand rotating at its own frequency.** Pair $i = 0$ rotates fastest (one radian per position); the last pair rotates slowest, with wavelengths growing geometrically from $2\pi$ to $10000 \cdot 2\pi$. Just as reading the second, minute, and hour hands together tells you the exact time, reading all $d/2$ pairs gives each position a unique signature.
- **Every entry is bounded in $[-1, 1]$**, so long sequences never produce huge position numbers.
- **A fixed offset is a linear rotation.** For any fixed offset $k$, the $2\times 2$ block $(\sin, \cos)$ at position $\text{pos} + k$ is a fixed rotation of the block at $\text{pos}$, making $\mathbf{p}_{\text{pos}+k}$ a linear function of $\mathbf{p}_{\text{pos}}$. Vaswani et al. chose sinusoids so the model could easily attend by relative offset.

Two other position schemes are widely used in modern models:

- **Learned positional embeddings**: a trainable lookup table of shape $L_{\max} \times d$, added to the token embeddings just like sinusoids. Vaswani et al. found learned and sinusoidal encodings performed almost identically; GPT models use learned positional embeddings.
- **RoPE** (Rotary Position Embedding): instead of adding position vectors to the input embeddings, RoPE rotates the query and key vectors inside every attention layer so that each dot product $\mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{k}_j$ depends only on the relative distance $i - j$. This is the standard in the Llama family; see [The LLaMA Family: RoPE and Parallel Training](/en/llm/llama-family/).

### The Transformer block

Attention mixes information **across** positions. A full network also needs to transform representations **within** each position and stack many layers deep. A **Transformer block** pairs multi-head attention with a position-wise feed-forward network, wrapping each sublayer in a residual connection and layer normalization. Writing $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$ for the block's input, the original paper computes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{H}' &= \mathrm{LN}\big(\mathbf{H} + \mathrm{MultiHead}(\mathbf{H})\big) \\
\mathbf{H}_{\text{out}} &= \mathrm{LN}\big(\mathbf{H}' + \mathrm{FFN}(\mathbf{H}')\big)
\end{aligned}
$$

- **Position-wise feed-forward network** (FFN): a two-layer MLP applied **independently to each position** using the same weights across all positions: $\mathrm{FFN}(\mathbf{x}) = \max(0, \mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}_1 + \mathbf{b}_1)\mathbf{W}_2 + \mathbf{b}_2$. The hidden layer is wider than $d$ (the original paper uses $d_{\text{ff}} = 2048 = 4d$). Intuitively, attention is where tokens communicate with one another, and the FFN is where each token processes what it just gathered.
- **Residual connection** ($\mathbf{H} + \dots$): each sublayer only has to learn a **residual adjustment** on top of its input, and gradients have an unimpeded additive highway through the entire stack—the exact same principle as ResNet in [Modern CNN Architectures](/en/dl/modern-cnn-architectures/).
- **Layer normalization** ($\mathrm{LN}$): normalizes each token's $d$-dimensional vector to mean 0 and variance 1, then applies learnable per-dimension scale and shift parameters. Unlike BatchNorm, LayerNorm normalizes across the features of a **single** token, so it behaves identically at any batch size or sequence length.

Placing $\mathrm{LN}$ **after** the residual addition as written above is called **Post-LN**. Most models from GPT-2 onward place $\mathrm{LN}$ **before** each sublayer—$\mathbf{H} + \mathrm{MultiHead}(\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{H}))$—which is called **Pre-LN**: Xiong et al. (2020) showed that Pre-LN keeps gradient norms well-behaved at initialization without requiring a delicate learning-rate warmup. [Build Your First LLM](/en/llm/build-first-llm/) uses Pre-LN.

A complete Transformer stacks $N$ such blocks ($N = 6$ in the base model of Vaswani et al.) on top of the sum of token embeddings and positional encodings, followed by a task-specific output head. Every block takes an $L \times d$ matrix and returns an $L \times d$ matrix.

### Causal masking

When generating text left to right, the model must predict token $i + 1$ using only tokens $1, \dots, i$. If position $i$ could attend to position $j > i$, it would peek directly at the target answer. A **causal mask** prevents this by adding $-\infty$ to all score entries with $j > i$ right before softmax:

$$
\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + \mathbf{M}\right)\mathbf{V},
\qquad
M_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
0, & j \le i \\
-\infty, & j > i
\end{cases}
$$

Because $e^{-\infty} = 0$, masked future positions receive weight 0, while the allowed positions $j \le i$ still sum to 1. The attention weight matrix $\mathbf{A}$ becomes lower triangular: row 1 can only look at position 1, whereas row $L$ sees the entire sequence and matches the unmasked row $L$ exactly. Thanks to causal masking, a single parallel forward pass computes all $L$ next-token predictions during training without any position seeing its future target; [Build Your First LLM](/en/llm/build-first-llm/) uses this exact mask to train a miniature GPT.

### The three Transformer families

From this single block design, three major model families are built:

| Family | Stack | Attention pattern | Classic examples | Best suited for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Encoder-only | Stack of encoder blocks | Bidirectional self-attention (no causal mask) | BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) | Understanding a complete input: classification, token tagging, search embeddings |
| Decoder-only | Stack of decoder blocks | Causally masked self-attention | GPT (Radford et al., 2018), Llama | Autoregressive text generation |
| Encoder–decoder | Encoder stack + decoder stack | Bidirectional encoder; causally masked decoder + cross-attention to encoder | Original Transformer (2017), T5 | Transforming one sequence into another: translation, summarization |

Here we only map out the three families at a high level. Cross-attention, teacher forcing, and beam search for encoder–decoder models are covered in [Sequence-to-Sequence Models (Seq2Seq)](/en/llm/seq2seq/), while BERT and GPT are explored in depth in the NLP and LLM parts.

### The cost: $O(L^2)$

Table 1 of Vaswani et al. (2017) compares a self-attention layer against a recurrent layer:

| Criterion | Self-attention | Recurrent layer (RNN/LSTM) |
|---|---|---|
| Compute per layer | $O(L^2 d)$ | $O(L d^2)$ |
| Sequential steps required | $O(1)$ | $O(L)$ |
| Maximum path length between two positions | $O(1)$ | $O(L)$ |

The bottom two rows explain why Transformers won: full parallelization across the sequence length, and a one-step path between any pair of tokens. The top row is the price tag. Every head in every layer materializes an $L \times L$ score matrix. At $L = 8192$, a single head's score matrix has $8192^2 \approx 67$ million entries, and doubling the sequence length quadruples both the compute and memory of standard attention. When $L < d$ (typical for single sentences), self-attention is actually cheaper than an RNN layer; when context windows stretch to tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens, the $O(L^2)$ term dominates.

:::note[Key takeaway]
Scaled dot-product attention is differentiable soft lookup: $\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top/\sqrt{d_k})\mathbf{V}$, where each row of the attention matrix sums to 1 and dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the score variance at 1. Self-attention projects $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}$ from the same sequence; multi-head attention runs $h$ parallel heads of width $d/h$. Because self-attention is permutation-equivariant, positional information must be injected (via sinusoidal encodings, learned embeddings, or RoPE). A Transformer block combines multi-head attention and a position-wise FFN with residual connections and layer normalization. Causal masking blocks future tokens during generation, and the main cost of standard attention is $O(L^2)$ in sequence length.
:::

### Limitations

- **Long sequences are expensive.** The $O(L^2)$ compute and memory scaling of full attention motivates memory-efficient attention kernels (such as FlashAttention) and sparse or linear approximations covered in Advanced Transformers.
- **Weak built-in inductive bias.** Unlike CNNs (which bake in local translation invariance) or RNNs (which bake in sequential order), a Transformer starts with almost no structural assumptions about the input. That makes it remarkably general across text, images, and audio, but often more data-hungry on small datasets.
- **Word order comes solely from positional encoding.** If you omit positional encodings, every matrix shape still matches and the code runs without error, yet the model treats its input as a bag of tokens—as the first Common pitfalls box demonstrates.

- **Every row sums to 1** on both sides (up to $\pm 0.01$ from two-decimal rounding).
- **The right-hand matrix is lower triangular.** Position 1 can only see itself, so its weight on position 1 is 1.00.
- **Row 5 is identical on both sides**, because the final position has no future tokens to mask out. Row 2 on the right is simply row 2 on the left with the last three entries zeroed out and the remaining two renormalized: $0.38 : 0.18$ becomes $0.67 : 0.33$.

Here the input vectors are random, so the weights carry no linguistic meaning. In a trained Transformer, each head produces a table like this, and many heads learn interpretable patterns such as attending to the previous token or linking pronouns to their antecedents.

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Forgetting positional encoding]
If you leave out positional encodings, every tensor shape still matches and the model trains without raising an error—it simply has no notion of order. Set `USE_POS = False` in the PyTorch sorting script and run it again: the loss stalls at 1.71, per-digit accuracy drops to 30.0%, and **full-sequence accuracy collapses to 0.0%**. The reason is the exact same permutation equivariance we saw in the "dog bites cat" cell: without positions, identical input digits produce identical output vectors, so the network has no way to route the minimum to slot 0 and the maximum to slot 7. Whether your model uses sinusoidal or learned positional encodings, make sure they are **added to the embeddings** before block 1 (or applied inside each attention layer if using RoPE).
:::

:::caution[Dividing by $\sqrt{d}$ instead of $\sqrt{d_k}$, or forgetting to scale]
In multi-head attention, each head has key dimension $d_k = d/h$, so its dot products must be divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$, not by the full model dimension $\sqrt{d}$. Dividing by $\sqrt{d}$ shrinks the scores by an extra factor of $\sqrt{h}$, making attention overly uniform early in training; forgetting to divide at all pushes softmax into saturation for wide heads (as shown in the second code cell). Neither bug raises an exception—the model just trains worse. PyTorch's `nn.MultiheadAttention` and `F.scaled_dot_product_attention` scale by $\sqrt{d_k}$ automatically; watch out for this when implementing attention from scratch.
:::

:::caution[Applying softmax along the wrong axis]
In the score matrix $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, rows index queries and columns index keys. Softmax must be applied **across columns within each row** (`axis=-1` or `axis=1` in 2D NumPy) so that each query distributes 1 unit of attention across all keys. Using `axis=0` normalizes each **column** to sum to 1—making each key split itself across queries, which makes no sense (and in causal attention, leaks future information through the column sum). The output shape is unchanged, so always sanity-check `A.sum(axis=-1)` on a small batch to verify that every row sums to 1.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- RNNs and LSTMs process tokens sequentially, have an $O(L)$ path between distant tokens, and compress history into a single state vector. Self-attention lets every token attend directly to every other token in $O(1)$ hops and computes all positions in parallel.
- Scaled dot-product attention $\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top/\sqrt{d_k})\mathbf{V}$ performs differentiable soft lookup: queries score keys to form row-wise probability weights that mix values. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps dot-product variance at 1.
- Self-attention projects $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}$ from the same sequence; multi-head attention runs $h$ parallel heads of width $d/h$. Because self-attention is permutation-equivariant, token order must be injected via sinusoidal encodings, learned positional embeddings, or RoPE.
- A Transformer block pairs multi-head attention with a position-wise FFN, wrapping each in a residual connection and layer normalization (Post-LN in the 2017 paper, Pre-LN in most modern models). Causal masking hides future positions. The three families are encoder-only (BERT), decoder-only (GPT, Llama), and encoder–decoder (original Transformer, T5), and standard self-attention costs $O(L^2)$ in sequence length.
