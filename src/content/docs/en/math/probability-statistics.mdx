## Intuition

The real world is full of uncertainty and noise:
- A temperature sensor never reads the exact same number twice even in a stable room; its readings fluctuate slightly around the true temperature.
- When a user clicks an online ad, we cannot know with 100% certainty whether they will buy; we can only predict a **probability**.
- A handwritten digit classifier looking at a smudged "8" does not make a blind guess; it outputs a distribution: $85\%$ digit 8, $12\%$ digit 3, $3\%$ digit 0.

Probability and statistics provide the mathematical language that enables AI to quantify uncertainty, model real-world noise, and make optimal decisions from finite evidence.

## Theory

### 1. Random Variables: Discrete and Continuous

A **random variable** $X$ is a function that assigns a numerical value to each outcome in a sample space.

#### Discrete Random Variables

Take distinct, countable values — for example, a class label $y \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ or the number of tokens in a sentence.
- Described by a **Probability Mass Function (PMF)**: $p(x) = P(X = x)$.
- Probabilities across the entire sample space sum to 1:
  $$
  \sum_{x} P(X = x) = 1, \quad 0 \le P(X = x) \le 1
  $$

#### Continuous Random Variables

Take values across a continuous interval on the real line $\mathbb{R}$ — for example, house price, execution time, or weight.
- Described by a **Probability Density Function (PDF)**, denoted $f(x)$.
- The total area under the PDF curve equals 1:
  $$
  \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx = 1, \quad f(x) \ge 0
  $$
- **Crucial rule:** The probability at any single exact point is $P(X = x_0) = 0$! Probability is only meaningful over an interval $[a, b]$:
  $$
  P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx
  $$

---

### 2. Core Distributions in AI

#### The Gaussian (Normal) Distribution ($\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$)

The iconic symmetric bell curve, completely characterized by two parameters: its mean $\mu$ (the center of the bell) and its variance $\sigma^2$ (the spread of the bell):

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)
$$

**Central Limit Theorem:** The sum (or average) of a large number of independent random variables — regardless of their individual distributions — converges toward a Gaussian normal distribution. This is why natural noise and measurement errors in machine learning are almost universally modeled with a Gaussian.

#### The Bernoulli Distribution

Models a binary trial with two outcomes: success ($y = 1$) with probability $p$, and failure ($y = 0$) with probability $1 - p$:

$$
P(Y = y) = p^y (1 - p)^{1 - y}, \quad y \in \{0, 1\}
$$

This is the foundational distribution of binary classification and the Binary Cross-Entropy loss function.

---

### 3. Expectation, Variance, and Covariance

- **Expectation (Mean $\mathbb{E}[X]$ or $\mu$):** The probability-weighted average value of a random variable. For continuous variables: $\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} x f(x) \, dx$; for discrete variables: $\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum x p(x)$.
- **Variance ($\mathrm{Var}(X)$ or $\sigma^2$):** Measures how far values spread out around the mean:
  $$
  \mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2
  $$
- **Standard Deviation ($\sigma$):** The square root of the variance, $\sigma = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}$, which shares the same measurement units as the original data.
- **Covariance ($\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)$):** Measures whether two random variables vary together in the same direction or opposite directions:
  $$
  \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\right]
  $$
  If $\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) > 0$, $Y$ tends to increase when $X$ increases.

---

### 4. Bayes' Theorem: Updating Beliefs from Evidence

Conditional probability $P(A \mid B)$ is the probability that event $A$ occurs given that event $B$ has already occurred:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}
$$

From this definition, **Bayes' Theorem** lets us invert the direction of conditioning:

$$
P(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{P(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) P(\theta)}{P(\mathcal{D})}
$$

In machine learning and Bayesian inference:
- $P(\theta)$: **Prior** — our initial belief about parameter $\theta$ before observing any data.
- $P(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$: **Likelihood** — the probability of observing dataset $\mathcal{D}$ if the true parameter were $\theta$.
- $P(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})$: **Posterior** — our updated belief about $\theta$ after observing the data $\mathcal{D}$.
- $P(\mathcal{D})$: **Evidence** — the normalizing constant ensuring the posterior integrates to 1.

:::note[Key takeaway]
The golden rule of Bayesian thinking: $\text{Posterior} \propto \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior}$. Newly observed evidence continuously refines and updates the model's prior beliefs.
:::

### 5. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

Suppose we collect a dataset of $m$ independent samples $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(1)}, x^{(2)}, \dots, x^{(m)}\}$. We ask: **Which parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ was most likely to have generated this dataset?**

The **Likelihood function** is the product of the probabilities of all samples:

$$
L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_{i=1}^m p(x^{(i)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})
$$

To avoid multiplying tiny numbers into floating-point underflow and to simplify differentiation, we take the natural logarithm to obtain the **Log-Likelihood**:

$$
\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \ln L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{i=1}^m \ln p(x^{(i)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})
$$

The Maximum Likelihood Estimate is the parameter vector that maximizes this objective:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \sum_{i=1}^m \ln p(x^{(i)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})
$$

#### Mathematical Origins of MSE and Cross-Entropy

1. **Origin of MSE Loss:**
   Suppose our model predicts $\hat{y}^{(i)} = f(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})$ and the true label is corrupted by Gaussian noise: $y^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat{y}^{(i)}, \sigma^2)$.
   The log-likelihood of the dataset is:
   $$
   \ell = \sum_{i=1}^m \ln \left( \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{(y^{(i)} - \hat{y}^{(i)})^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \right) = -m \ln(\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^m (y^{(i)} - \hat{y}^{(i)})^2
   $$
   Maximizing $\ell$ is mathematically equivalent to **minimizing the sum of squared errors** $\sum (y^{(i)} - \hat{y}^{(i)})^2$ — which is MSE Loss!

2. **Origin of Cross-Entropy Loss:**
   Suppose a binary label $y^{(i)} \in \{0, 1\}$ follows a Bernoulli distribution with predicted probability $\hat{y}^{(i)} = P(Y=1)$.
   The log-likelihood is:
   $$
   \ell = \sum_{i=1}^m \left[ y^{(i)} \ln \hat{y}^{(i)} + (1 - y^{(i)}) \ln(1 - \hat{y}^{(i)}) \right]
   $$
   Negating this expression (minimizing $-\ell$) gives the exact **Binary Cross-Entropy Loss** formula!

### Limitations

- **i.i.d. assumption:** Standard MLE assumes samples are Independent and Identically Distributed. For text or time-series data (such as stock prices), later tokens or steps depend heavily on earlier ones, requiring autoregressive sequence models.
- **Overfitting on small samples:** If you flip a coin 3 times and get 3 heads, MLE concludes the probability of heads is $100\%$ ($\hat{p}_{\text{MLE}} = 1.0$). Incorporating a reasonable prior via Bayesian inference (MAP estimation) prevents such extreme conclusions when data is scarce.

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[The Base Rate Fallacy]
When evaluating predictions from a classifier (or medical test), ignoring the prior probability (Base Rate) is the most common mistake. A credit-card fraud detector with $99\%$ accuracy sounds impressive, but if only $0.01\%$ of real transactions are fraudulent, the vast majority of fraud alerts will still be false alarms!
:::

:::caution[Confusing Probability with Likelihood]
Probability measures the chance of observing future data when the model parameters are held fixed: $P(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$. Conversely, Likelihood evaluates how plausible different parameter values are after the observed data has been collected and fixed: $L(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathcal{D})$.
:::

:::caution[Confusing probability density $f(x)$ with probability $P(X = x)$]
For a continuous random variable, the probability density height $f(x)$ at a point can easily exceed $1.0$ (for example, a uniform distribution on $[0, 0.2]$ has density $f(x) = 5.0$). What must equal $1.0$ is the total integrated area under the curve. The value $f(x)$ represents relative probability concentration around $x$, not the probability at $x$.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- Random variables quantify uncertainty; continuous random variables have zero probability at any single point and are described by a probability density function (PDF).
- The Gaussian distribution sits at the heart of statistics thanks to the Central Limit Theorem; the Bernoulli distribution models binary classification.
- Bayes' Theorem provides the exact rule for updating prior beliefs with observed evidence.
- Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) is the probabilistic root of standard machine learning losses: MSE arises from Gaussian noise and Cross-Entropy arises from Bernoulli likelihood.
