## Intuition

Imagine you are blindfolded at the top of a mountain shrouded in thick fog. Your goal is to find your way down to the bottom of the valley as safely and quickly as possible. How would you do it?

Because you cannot see the whole landscape, your only option is to feel the slope of the ground right beneath your feet:
1. You feel around in a circle to find which direction slopes **downward the steepest**.
2. You take a careful small step in that downhill direction.
3. At your new spot, you feel the slope again and take another step.

In machine learning:
- The foggy mountain is the high-dimensional **loss surface**.
- The slope of the ground beneath your feet is the **derivative** and **Gradient vector**.
- Each step you take is a weight update in the **Gradient Descent** algorithm.

Calculus is the mathematical tool that lets a computer "feel the slope" and automatically step toward the optimal minimum!

## Theory

### 1. Derivatives: Instantaneous Rate of Change

Consider a single-variable function $f(x)$. When we perturb the input by a tiny amount $\epsilon$, the output changes by $f(x + \epsilon) - f(x)$. The **derivative** of the function at $x$, denoted $f'(x)$ or $\frac{df}{dx}$, is the rate of change as $\epsilon$ approaches $0$:

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \frac{f(x + \epsilon) - f(x)}{\epsilon}
$$

Geometric meaning: $f'(x)$ is the **slope** of the line tangent to the graph at $x$:
- If $f'(x) > 0$: the function is increasing. Increasing $x$ increases $f(x)$; to decrease $f(x)$, we must decrease $x$.
- If $f'(x) < 0$: the function is decreasing. Increasing $x$ decreases $f(x)$.
- If $f'(x) = 0$: the tangent line is horizontal; $x$ may be a local minimum, a local maximum, or a saddle/inflection point.

### 2. Partial Derivatives and the Gradient Vector

In practice, an AI model has thousands or billions of weights $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n$. The loss function $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w})$ is a multivariable function.

#### Partial Derivatives

The **partial derivative** of $f$ with respect to $x_i$, denoted $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}$, measures how $f$ changes when we nudge only $x_i$ while **holding all other variables constant**:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \frac{f(x_1, \dots, x_i + \epsilon, \dots, x_n) - f(x_1, \dots, x_i, \dots, x_n)}{\epsilon}
$$

#### The Gradient Vector ($\nabla f$)

Stacking all partial derivatives of a function into a column vector gives the **Gradient vector**, denoted $\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$:

$$
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} \\ \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix} = \left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \right]^\top
$$

**The most important properties of the Gradient in optimization:**
1. The vector $\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$ always points in the **direction of steepest increase** of the function.
2. Its magnitude $\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_2$ measures how steep that maximum slope is.
3. The opposite vector $-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$ always points in the **direction of steepest decrease**.

That is why the parameter update rule in Gradient Descent has a minus sign:

$$
\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta \nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
$$

where $\eta > 0$ is the **learning rate** controlling the size of each step.

:::note[Key takeaway]
The gradient is the compass of machine learning: follow $+\nabla f$ to maximize an objective, and step along $-\nabla f$ to minimize loss.
:::

### 3. The Chain Rule and Backpropagation

A deep neural network is a massive composite function of stacked layers:
Input $\mathbf{x} \longrightarrow$ Layer 1 ($z_1$) $\longrightarrow$ Layer 2 ($z_2$) $\longrightarrow \dots \longrightarrow$ Prediction $\hat{y} \longrightarrow$ Loss $\mathcal{L}$.

How can we compute the derivative of the final loss $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to a weight $w$ all the way back in the first layer?

#### The Chain Rule in Calculus

If $z$ depends on $y$, and $y$ depends on $x$ ($z = f(g(x))$), then the rate of change of $z$ with respect to $x$ is the product of the intermediate rates of change:

$$
\frac{dz}{dx} = \frac{dz}{dy} \cdot \frac{dy}{dx}
$$

When an intermediate variable branches into multiple paths — say $z = f(y_1, y_2)$ where each $y_j = g_j(x)$ — we sum over all paths:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = \sum_{j} \frac{\partial z}{\partial y_j} \cdot \frac{\partial y_j}{\partial x}
$$

#### Application in Backpropagation

Consider a simple linear regression neuron:
1. **Forward pass:**
   $$
   \hat{y} = w x + b
   $$
   $$
   \mathcal{L} = (\hat{y} - y)^2
   $$
2. **Backward pass:**
   - Derivative of the loss with respect to the prediction:
     $$
     \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{y}} = 2(\hat{y} - y)
     $$
   - Derivatives of the prediction with respect to weight $w$ and bias $b$:
     $$
     \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial w} = x, \quad \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial b} = 1
     $$
   - Applying the chain rule:
     $$
     \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial w} = 2(\hat{y} - y) x
     $$
     $$
     \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial b} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial b} = 2(\hat{y} - y)
     $$

Thanks to the chain rule, we never need to derive a giant monolithic symbolic formula for the entire neural network at once. Instead, each layer simply computes the local gradient of its own operation and multiplies it backward from output to input.

### 4. Second Derivatives and the Hessian Matrix

The first derivative tells us the slope, but not whether the surface is flat or sharply curved. The **second derivative** measures the **curvature** of a function.

For a multivariable function, all second-order partial derivatives are organized into the **Hessian matrix** $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$:

$$
\mathbf{H}_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}
$$

By the second-order Taylor expansion:

$$
f(\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\Delta x}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x})^\top \boldsymbol{\Delta x} + \frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{\Delta x}^\top \mathbf{H} \boldsymbol{\Delta x}
$$

- If the Hessian matrix has a maximum eigenvalue $L$ ($\lambda_{\max} = L$), the surface curves very steeply along that direction. Choosing a learning rate $\eta > \frac{2}{L}$ causes Gradient Descent to overshoot the valley walls and **diverge**.
- A large gap between the sharpest and flattest curvatures (the condition number $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$) explains why narrow "ravine" loss surfaces cause Gradient Descent to zig-zag slowly back and forth.

### Limitations

- **Saddle points:** In spaces with thousands of dimensions, a zero gradient ($\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$) is far more likely to be a saddle point (curving up along some directions and down along others) than a true global minimum.
- **Vanishing and exploding gradients:** When a neural network has many layers, multiplying dozens of local derivatives smaller than $1$ drives the gradient toward $0$ (vanishing gradient), stalling learning; conversely, factors larger than $1$ cause the gradient to blow up (exploding gradient).

## Illustration

The diagram below contrasts finite-difference gradient checking methods and traces the backward flow of the chain rule in backpropagation:

```text
1. FINITE DIFFERENCES FOR GRADIENT CHECKING:
    Forward difference (one-sided, error O(ε)):
           f(x + ε) - f(x)
      ─────────────────────────
                  ε
    Central difference (symmetric two-sided, error O(ε²)):
       f(x + ε) - f(x - ε)
      ─────────────────────   ◄── Millions of times more accurate!
              2ε

2. CHAIN RULE FLOW (BACKPROPAGATION):
    Forward pass:
       x ──► [ × w ] ──► z ──► [ + b ] ──► y_hat ──► [ Loss ] ──► L
    Backward pass:
       dL/dw ◄── [ * x ] ◄── dL/dz ◄── [ * 1 ] ◄── dL/dy_hat ◄── dL/dL (= 1)
```

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Forgetting the minus sign when updating weights in Gradient Descent]
The correct update rule is $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta \nabla \mathcal{L}$. If you accidentally write $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \eta \nabla \mathcal{L}$, you are running Gradient Ascent to maximize the loss! The loss value will rapidly explode to infinity (`inf` or `NaN`) within a few iterations.
:::

:::caution[Choosing a step size $\epsilon$ that is too large or too small in gradient checking]
If you pick $\epsilon$ too large (e.g., $\epsilon = 0.1$), Taylor series truncation error will make the numerical gradient inaccurate. Conversely, if you pick $\epsilon$ too small (below $10^{-12}$), floating-point subtraction suffers from catastrophic cancellation in 64-bit precision (`float64`). In practice, the sweet spot is $\epsilon \approx 10^{-5}$ or $10^{-7}$.
:::

:::caution[Forgetting to treat other variables as constants when taking partial derivatives]
When computing $\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1}(x_1^2 + 3 x_1 x_2 + 2 x_2^2)$, learners often forget that $x_2$ acts as a fixed constant and miscalculate the $3 x_1 x_2$ term. Because the derivative of $c \cdot x_1$ with respect to $x_1$ is simply $c$, setting $c = 3 x_2$ gives the exact partial derivative $3 x_2$.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- A derivative measures the instantaneous rate of change; the gradient vector collects partial derivatives and points in the direction of steepest ascent.
- Gradient Descent optimizes models by stepping opposite to the gradient: $\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta \nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$.
- The Chain Rule is the mathematical foundation that enables Backpropagation to efficiently compute derivatives across hundreds of neural network layers.
- Second derivatives and the Hessian matrix govern the curvature of the loss surface and dictate how large the learning rate $\eta$ can safely be.
