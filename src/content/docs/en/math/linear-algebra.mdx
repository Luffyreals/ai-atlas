## Intuition

How can a computer understand the meaning of a cat photo or the semantics of a scientific article?

Computers cannot directly perceive colors or read human words. All multimodal data in AI is converted into arrays of real numbers:
- A $28 \times 28$ grayscale image is represented as a $28 \times 28$ matrix of numbers, or flattened into a 784-dimensional **vector**.
- A passage of text is encoded by a language model into a 1536-dimensional **embedding vector** in semantic space.
- A fully connected neural network layer (Dense layer) takes a feature vector, multiplies it by a **weight matrix**, and adds a bias vector to transform the representation space.

While introductory school algebra often treats a matrix as just a "rectangular grid of numbers", in Artificial Intelligence you should view a matrix as a **spatial transformation**: it can stretch, compress, rotate, or warp data space to expose underlying patterns.

## Theory

### 1. Vectors: Points and Directions in Space

An $n$-dimensional **vector** is an ordered tuple of $n$ real numbers, denoted $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix} = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n]^\top
$$

Geometrically:
- A vector can be viewed as an arrow starting at the origin $(0, 0, \dots, 0)$ and pointing to the coordinate $(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)$.
- The arrow's orientation represents **direction**, and its length represents **magnitude**.

#### Dot Product

The dot product between two vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the sum of the products of their corresponding entries:

$$
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i
$$

Geometrically, the dot product is closely tied to the angle $\theta$ between the two vectors:

$$
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2 \cos(\theta)
$$

- If $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} > 0$: acute angle ($\theta < 90^\circ$), the two vectors point in roughly the same direction.
- If $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$: the two vectors are perpendicular (**orthogonal**).
- If $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} < 0$: obtuse angle ($\theta > 90^\circ$), the two vectors point in opposing directions.

In the Attention mechanism of Transformer models, the correlation score $\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{k}$ between a Query vector and a Key vector is literally a dot product!

#### Vector Norms

A **norm** measures the magnitude or length of a vector:

1. **$L_1$ norm (Manhattan norm):** The sum of absolute values of the elements:
   $$
   \|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = \sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|
   $$
   The $L_1$ norm underpins Lasso Regularization, which drives unimportant weights to exact $0$ and produces sparse models.

2. **$L_2$ norm (Euclidean norm):** The straight-line geometric distance from the origin to the vector tip:
   $$
   \|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2} = \sqrt{\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}}
   $$
   The $L_2$ norm underpins Ridge Regularization (Weight Decay), which constrains neural network weight magnitudes to prevent overfitting.

#### Cosine Similarity

When comparing two text passages via embedding vectors, a 5000-word article and a 500-word summary on the same topic will have very different $L_2$ lengths. To measure semantic similarity without being distorted by length, we use **Cosine Similarity**:

$$
\text{CosineSimilarity}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2} = \cos(\theta)
$$

This value always lies in $[-1, 1]$:
- $1.0$: identical semantic direction.
- $0.0$: completely orthogonal and unrelated.
- $-1.0$: diametrically opposed in meaning.

:::note[Key takeaway]
Euclidean distance is heavily influenced by vector magnitude, whereas Cosine Similarity cares only about the **angle** between two vectors in feature space.
:::

### 2. Matrices: Linear Transformation Machines

A matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ consists of $m$ rows and $n$ columns:

$$
\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} & \dots & a_{1n} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} & \dots & a_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{m1} & a_{m2} & \dots & a_{mn} \end{bmatrix}
$$

When we multiply matrix $\mathbf{A}$ by a vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, we obtain a new vector $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m$:

$$
\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
$$

Each $j$-th column of matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is the new landing spot of the $j$-th basis vector after the space is transformed. A matrix is a complete "relocation map" for the coordinate space.

#### Matrix Multiplication

Multiplying two matrices $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}$ and $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times p}$ yields a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p}$ where:

$$
C_{ij} = \sum_{r=1}^k A_{ir} B_{rj}
$$

**Essential properties to remember:**
- **Associativity:** $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C})$.
- **Generally NOT commutative:** $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \neq \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$. Swapping the multiplication order changes the spatial transformation result or causes a shape mismatch!
- **Transpose of a product:** $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^\top = \mathbf{B}^\top \mathbf{A}^\top$ (reverses the order of the factors).

#### Determinant and Inverse Matrix

- **Determinant ($\det(\mathbf{A})$ or $|\mathbf{A}|$):** Measures the area or volume scaling factor when space is transformed by a square matrix $\mathbf{A}$.
  - If $\det(\mathbf{A}) = 2$: the area of every geometric shape in space doubles.
  - If $\det(\mathbf{A}) < 0$: space flips its orientation (reflection).
  - If $\det(\mathbf{A}) = 0$: space collapses into a lower dimension (e.g., a 2D plane squashed onto a 1D line segment).
- **Inverse matrix ($\mathbf{A}^{-1}$):** The transformation that restores space back to its original state:
  $$
  \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{A}^{-1} = \mathbf{I}
  $$
  A square matrix is invertible if and only if $\det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0$ (a **non-singular** matrix).

### 3. Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

When matrix $\mathbf{A}$ transforms space, most vectors get knocked off their original line into a new direction. However, there are special directions where vectors **do not rotate at all**, but are merely stretched or shrunk!

Those special vectors are called **eigenvectors** ($\mathbf{v}$), and their corresponding scaling factors are called **eigenvalues** ($\lambda$):

$$
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v} \quad (\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0})
$$

To find the eigenvalues $\lambda$, we solve the characteristic equation:

$$
\det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda \mathbf{I}) = 0
$$

**Core applications in AI:**
- **PCA (Principal Component Analysis):** The eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix points in the direction of greatest data variance — the direction carrying the most information. By projecting data onto the top eigenvectors, we reduce dimensionality from thousands of features down to a few while preserving key structure.
- **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD):** The mathematical backbone of LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) weight compression in large language models (LLMs).

### Limitations

- **Computational complexity:** Standard matrix multiplication takes $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ operations, and matrix inversion also costs $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$. For AI models with billions of parameters, we never invert matrices directly; instead, we use iterative optimization algorithms like Gradient Descent.
- **Numerical instability:** When a matrix has a determinant very close to $0$ (a huge condition number $\kappa$), matrix operations on computers suffer from severe floating-point rounding errors.


## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Confusing elementwise multiplication (`*`) with matrix multiplication (`@`)]
In NumPy, the `*` operator performs the Hadamard product (elementwise multiplication between two arrays of matching shape). To perform linear algebra matrix multiplication $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})$, you must use the `@` operator or `np.dot(A, B)`. Writing `A * B` by mistake will either raise a shape error or produce a matrix with completely wrong spatial transformation semantics.
:::

:::caution[Using Euclidean distance instead of Cosine Similarity for texts of different lengths]
When matching two documents in a retrieval system (RAG), if you use Euclidean distance $\|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\|_2$, a 3-sentence summary and a 30-page in-depth report on the exact same AI topic will still have a large distance (due to word count and vector magnitude differences). Normalizing for angle via Cosine Similarity is the standard choice to eliminate document length bias.
:::

:::caution[Assuming every square matrix has an inverse]
A square matrix is invertible only when its determinant is nonzero ($\det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0$). If your dataset contains linearly dependent features (for example, one column measuring area in $\text{m}^2$ and another in $\text{ft}^2$), the matrix $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ becomes singular with determinant $0$, causing the closed-form Normal Equation of linear regression to fail.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- Vectors represent data samples or directions in space; the dot product measures directional alignment between two vectors.
- The $L_1$ and $L_2$ norms measure vector length and form the basis of Lasso and Ridge weight penalties in machine learning.
- Matrices are spatial transformations; the determinant measures area/volume scaling and determines whether a matrix is invertible.
- Eigenvalues and eigenvectors reveal the invariant axes of a spatial transformation, underpinning PCA dimensionality reduction and SVD/LoRA model compression.
