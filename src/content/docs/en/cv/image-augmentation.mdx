## Intuition

### A digit shifted by 1 pixel

Suppose you train a small neural network to classify handwritten digits on 300 $8 \times 8$ images from `load_digits`. On the test set, it reaches about 94% accuracy. Then you shift each test image by at most 1 pixel horizontally or vertically. To a human eye, nothing meaningful changed. To the model, accuracy plunges to around 40% (the scikit-learn cell in the Code examples section reproduces these exact numbers).

What went wrong? The model had never seen a digit 6 sitting one pixel to the right. Instead of learning "a loop at the bottom means 6," it learned a brittle shortcut like "a dark pixel in column 3 means 6." On 300 centered training images, both rules fit the training data equally well, and the network latched onto the simpler pixel-coordinate rule.

### Teaching what should not change the label

The simplest fix: show the model digits at slightly different positions during training. You do not need to collect new photographs. You simply shift the existing training images yourself, keep their original labels, and feed them into training. Every shifted copy tells the model: "shifting by 1 pixel does not change which digit this is."

That is the entire idea behind data augmentation. You know ahead of time that certain transformations **preserve the ground-truth answer**: a horizontally flipped dog is still a dog; a slightly darker photo is still the same scene. Because a neural network only learns from data, you turn that domain knowledge into data.

Not every transformation is safe, however. Rotating a 6 by 180 degrees turns it into a 9. Flipping the letter "b" horizontally turns it into a "d". Horizontally flipping a chest X-ray moves the heart to the right side of the chest—a rare congenital condition called situs inversus. Whether a transform is valid depends on the **task**, not just the image.

**The core question of this lesson:** which transforms should you pick, what targets must transform along with the image (labels, masks, bounding boxes), and why does "making up extra data" help a model generalize rather than just memorize more?

## Theory

### Notation

Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$ be an image (height $H$, width $W$, $C$ color channels), $y$ be its label, and $f$ be the model. The $i$-th training example is $(\mathbf{X}^{(i)}, y^{(i)})$, for $i = 1, \dots, m$. A **transformation** $T$ takes an image and returns an image of the same or different size, such as "flip horizontally" or "shift right by 1 pixel." In semantic segmentation, each image also comes with a mask $\mathbf{M}$ assigning a class ID to every pixel.

### Invariance and equivariance

In image classification, we want the model to be **invariant** to $T$: transforming the input image should leave the predicted class unchanged.

$$
f(T(\mathbf{X})) = f(\mathbf{X})
$$

Semantic segmentation is different. If you flip the input image horizontally, the ground-truth segmentation map must flip horizontally too: when the cat moves to the left half of the image, the "cat" region in the mask must move to the left half as well. Here we want the model to be **equivariant** to $T$: transforming the input transforms the output in the exact same way.

$$
f(T(\mathbf{X})) = T(f(\mathbf{X}))
$$

Object detection works the same way: bounding boxes must move, scale, and rotate along with the objects they enclose. That is why the Code examples section passes the image, segmentation mask, and bounding boxes through **the exact same** random transform call.

CNNs have a piece of this built into their architecture: sliding the same convolutional kernel across the image makes feature maps translation-equivariant (see [Image Classification with CNNs](/en/cv/cnn-image-classification/)). Even so, a CNN is not automatically invariant to rotation, scale, or brightness shifts. And a fully connected MLP has no spatial inductive bias at all—to an MLP, a pixel in column 3 and a pixel in column 4 are two unrelated features. Data augmentation supplies the missing invariances through training examples.

### Augmentation in the loss function

Standard training minimizes the average loss $\ell$ over the $m$ original training examples:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \ell\big(f(\mathbf{X}^{(i)}), y^{(i)}\big)
$$

With data augmentation, each time the model visits image $i$, we draw a random transform $T$ from a distribution of allowed transforms (for instance, "shift randomly by $-1$, $0$, or $+1$ pixel along each axis"). Across many epochs, with a fresh draw every epoch, training minimizes the **expected loss over all random transforms $T$**:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{aug}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \mathbb{E}_{T}\Big[\ell\big(f(T(\mathbf{X}^{(i)})), y^{(i)}\big)\Big]
$$

Here $\mathbb{E}_T$ averages over the random choice of $T$. You can read this objective in two complementary ways:

- **An expanded dataset.** Each original image becomes a whole cloud of transformed variants sharing the same label, multiplying the effective size of the training set.
- **A regularization penalty.** Any model that outputs different predictions on $\mathbf{X}$ and $T(\mathbf{X})$ must be wrong on at least one of them, incurring higher loss. The augmented objective rules out functions that memorize exact pixel coordinates, much like [Ridge, Lasso, and Elastic Net](/en/ml/regularization/) rule out models with huge weights.

Data augmentation is therefore a direct weapon against [overfitting](/en/ml/overfitting-underfitting/): it stops the model from clinging to accidental quirks of a few hundred training images. And just like a regularization strength $\lambda$, you can overdo it: if transforms distort images beyond recognition, the model underfits.

:::note[Key takeaway]
Data augmentation tells the model "these transformations do not change the answer" (for classification) or "the answer transforms in the exact same way" (for segmentation and detection) by feeding transformed examples into the training loop. It helps whenever that assumption holds for your task.
:::

### Geometric and photometric transforms

**Geometric transforms** move pixels to new spatial positions without changing their color values:

- **Horizontal flip**: almost always safe for natural photos. **Vertical flip** only makes sense for top-down imagery with no "up" direction, such as satellite imagery or microscope slides.
- **Random crop** and **random resized crop**: crop a random sub-rectangle and resize it back to a fixed resolution. This forces the model to recognize objects at different locations and scales. AlexNet (Krizhevsky et al., 2012) extracted random $224 \times 224$ crops from $256 \times 256$ images along with horizontal flips—one of the paper's two main defenses against overfitting, alongside dropout.
- **Rotation** by a few degrees, **scaling**, and **shear**. Rotating an image exposes empty triangles at the corners that must be filled (typically with 0), and requires interpolating pixel values onto the rotated grid.

**Photometric transforms** change pixel intensities or colors while leaving every pixel in its original location:

- **Brightness and contrast**: simulate photos taken in sunny or dim lighting, or with different camera exposures.
- **Color jitter** (saturation, hue): simulates warm indoor lighting versus cool daylight.
- **Gaussian noise** and **blur**: simulate noisy camera sensors or motion blur.

Because photometric transforms never move a pixel, segmentation masks and bounding boxes **stay untouched**. Only geometric transforms need to be applied to masks and boxes.

### When a transform corrupts the label

A transform $T$ is safe only if the ground-truth label is genuinely invariant to $T$. Common cases where the label is **not** invariant include:

- **Digits and text (OCR):** rotating a 6 by 180 degrees turns it into a 9; horizontally flipping letters produces mirror writing that never appears at test time.
- **Directional semantics:** a "turn left" sign flipped horizontally becomes "turn right." Medical scans have anatomical left and right: flipping a chest X-ray moves the heart to the opposite side of the body.
- **Color-defining classes:** classifying ripe vs. unripe fruit, or red vs. green traffic lights—strong hue jitter changes the true class.
- **Aggressive cropping:** cropping a tiny patch in the corner can miss the target object entirely, leaving only background with an object label.

The simplest sanity check in practice: plot a few dozen augmented training images with their labels and ask yourself, "Would a human still assign this label to this image?"

### Cutout, mixup, and CutMix

The next three techniques do not try to mimic natural camera variations at all. Instead, they deliberately synthesize "unnatural" images so the model cannot rely on a single narrow cue.

**Cutout** (DeVries & Taylor, 2017): zero out a fixed-size square patch at a random location. Because the dog's head might be masked out in one epoch and its legs in the next, the network must learn to recognize the dog from multiple parts rather than fixating on a single feature.

**Mixup** (Zhang et al., 2018): linearly blend two images **and** their one-hot label vectors $\mathbf{y}$ using the same weight $\lambda$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf{X}} &= \lambda\,\mathbf{X}^{(i)} + (1 - \lambda)\,\mathbf{X}^{(j)} \\
\tilde{\mathbf{y}} &= \lambda\,\mathbf{y}^{(i)} + (1 - \lambda)\,\mathbf{y}^{(j)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The mixing weight $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ is drawn from a symmetric $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$ distribution; for small $\alpha$, $\lambda$ usually lands near 0 or near 1, so one of the two images dominates. The paper found $\alpha \in [0.1, 0.4]$ improved ImageNet accuracy, whereas large $\alpha$ caused underfitting. In practice, you blend a mini-batch with a randomly shuffled copy of itself.

The blended target $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$ is a **soft label**—say, $0.7$ for class 6 and $0.3$ for class 1. Cross-entropy with a soft label is still $-\sum_k \tilde{y}_k \ln p_k$, where $p_k$ is the model's predicted probability for class $k$. Substituting $\tilde{y}_k$ in gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\sum_k \tilde{y}_k \ln p_k &= -\sum_k \big(\lambda y^{(i)}_k + (1-\lambda) y^{(j)}_k\big) \ln p_k \\
&= \lambda\Big(-\sum_k y^{(i)}_k \ln p_k\Big) + (1-\lambda)\Big(-\sum_k y^{(j)}_k \ln p_k\Big)
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line simply splits the sum in two. Each parenthesized term is ordinary cross-entropy against a single hard label. Thus, the mixup loss is simply the **weighted average of the two standard cross-entropy losses**: weight $\lambda$ on label $i$ and weight $1 - \lambda$ on label $j$. Many implementations compute mixup directly from this second line without ever constructing a one-hot soft label vector.

Why does blending two unrelated images into a ghostly double exposure help? Because mixup forces the model's predictions to transition **linearly** between training examples: halfway between a 6 and a 1, the predicted probabilities must also sit halfway in between, rather than snapping abruptly from 100% confident in one class to 100% confident in the other. That smoothness acts as a strong regularizer and improves calibration.

**CutMix** (Yun et al., 2019) combines the best of cutout and mixup: instead of zeroing out a rectangle (cutout) or ghosting two whole images together (mixup), it **cuts a rectangular patch from image $j$ and pastes it onto image $i$**. Let $\mathbf{B}$ be a binary mask of the same spatial size as the image, equal to 1 where image $i$ is kept and 0 inside the pasted rectangle, and let $\odot$ denote elementwise multiplication:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{B} \odot \mathbf{X}^{(i)} + (\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{B}) \odot \mathbf{X}^{(j)}
$$

The labels are blended just like in mixup, with $\lambda$ equal to the **area fraction** of image $i$ that remains visible. To make the rectangle cover fraction $1 - \lambda$ of an $H \times W$ image, its width and height are set to $W\sqrt{1-\lambda}$ and $H\sqrt{1-\lambda}$, whose product is $(1 - \lambda)WH$. Because the box is clipped at the image borders, code recomputes the exact $\lambda$ from the remaining pixel count. Compared with cutout, no pixels are wasted on blank zeros; compared with mixup, every local patch looks like a real natural image, helping the model localize objects.

### From AutoAugment to RandAugment

Which transforms should you combine, at what magnitudes, and with what probabilities? With dozens of candidate operations and continuous magnitudes, the space of possible pipelines is enormous.

**AutoAugment** (Cubuk et al., 2019) framed policy design as a search problem. A **policy** consists of several sub-policies; each sub-policy chains two operations, each with its own application probability and magnitude. Every image in a mini-batch is transformed by one randomly chosen sub-policy. The authors used reinforcement learning to search over policies by training small proxy models on a subset of the data and scoring them on a validation set. The discovered policies worked well, but the search itself was extraordinarily expensive—as the RandAugment paper notes, finding the AutoAugment policy for object detection took 15,000 GPU hours.

**RandAugment** (Cubuk et al., 2020) eliminated the separate search phase altogether. It starts with a fixed list of $K = 14$ standard transforms (rotate, shear, translate, auto-contrast, brightness, sharpness, and so on). For each image:

1. Pick $N$ transforms uniformly at random from the list.
2. Apply them one after another using **the same shared magnitude** $M$, on an integer scale from 0 to 10 (where 10 is the strongest setting of each operation).

The entire augmentation policy is now controlled by just two interpretable integers: larger $N$ and $M$ mean stronger regularization. The paper estimates this shrinks the search space from roughly $10^{32}$ (in AutoAugment) down to $10^2$—small enough to tune with a quick grid search directly on your target task. Crucially, the paper also shows that the optimal magnitude $M$ **grows** with both model size and dataset size, which means a policy tuned on a small proxy model is often suboptimal for a large model anyway.

### Augmenting only the training set, and test-time augmentation

Random data augmentation belongs strictly to **training**. Validation and test images should pass only through deterministic preprocessing: resize to a standard resolution, center crop, and normalize pixel values. If you apply random transforms to the test set, your test score changes from run to run and cannot be fairly compared against other models.

The one deliberate exception is **test-time augmentation** (TTA): at inference time, create a fixed set of deterministic views of each test image (for example, the original image plus its horizontal flip, or crops from the four corners and center), run the model on all of them, and average the predicted probability vectors. AlexNet used 10-crop TTA (four corners and center, plus their horizontal flips). TTA often nudges accuracy up slightly, at the cost of multiplying inference time by the number of views.

### Limitations

- **Augmentation does not create genuinely new domain knowledge.** It only multiplies variations you already know how to write down mathematically. Dimming daytime street photos will not teach a model what glowing headlights and wet asphalt reflections look like at night.
- **Too much augmentation causes underfitting.** If transforms are too strong for the model's capacity, even clean-image accuracy can drop—as you can verify by swapping the MLP for logistic regression in the first code example below.
- **Self-supervised learning turns augmentation into the primary learning signal:** two augmented views of the same image are forced to produce similar representations. [Self-Supervised Learning for Images](/en/cv/self-supervised-learning/) builds directly on this idea.

To add RandAugment to a training pipeline, insert `v2.RandAugment(num_ops=2, magnitude=9)` ($N = 2$, $M = 9$, torchvision's defaults). If your pretrained backbone expects normalized inputs, append `v2.Normalize(mean, std)` at the end of both pipelines.

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Augmenting the entire dataset before splitting into train and test]
If you generate augmented copies for every image first and then randomly split into train and test, a 1-pixel shifted copy of an image lands in the training set while the original lands in the test set. A model that simply memorizes training images will score artificially high on test—classic data leakage. Always split into train, validation, and test on the original images first (and group by subject or patient if multiple photos show the same entity). Only then augment the training set, ideally sampling fresh random transforms on the fly each epoch.
:::

:::caution[Transforming the image while forgetting the mask or bounding boxes]
In segmentation and object detection, every geometric transform must be applied to the image, mask, and bounding boxes using **the exact same random draw**. Calling the pipeline twice in separate statements desynchronizes the image and mask, as shown in the PyTorch cell above (agreement drops to 40%). Wrap targets in `tv_tensors.Mask` and `tv_tensors.BoundingBoxes` and pass them in a single call. If you write custom code, always interpolate masks with nearest-neighbor, never bilinear. And after random cropping, boxes may be clipped to thin slivers or pushed completely outside the frame—use `v2.SanitizeBoundingBoxes` to clean them up.
:::

:::caution[Copy-pasting default augmentations without checking whether labels stay valid]
Boilerplate snippets online almost always include `RandomHorizontalFlip` and `ColorJitter` because they work well on ImageNet. Copy-pasting them into an OCR task, a traffic-sign classifier, a medical imaging pipeline, or a color-based grading system can silently flip ground-truth labels. Before launching a long training run, always visualize a grid of augmented training examples alongside their labels and inspect them with your own eyes.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- Data augmentation encodes domain knowledge into training data: "this transform leaves the label unchanged" (invariance, for classification) or "the target transforms the same way as the image" (equivariance, for segmentation and detection). Minimizing expected loss over random transforms both expands the effective dataset and regularizes the model.
- Geometric transforms (flips, crops, rotations) move pixels and must be applied identically to masks and bounding boxes; photometric transforms (brightness, contrast, color jitter, noise, blur) only alter pixel intensities. Whether a transform is safe depends on the task: rotating 180 degrees turns a 6 into a 9.
- Cutout masks out a square patch, mixup blends pairs of images and labels via $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \lambda\mathbf{y}^{(i)} + (1-\lambda)\mathbf{y}^{(j)}$, and CutMix pastes a rectangular patch from one image onto another while weighting labels by area. RandAugment replaces expensive policy search with two hyperparameters $N$ and $M$.
- Augment only the training set, after splitting the dataset. Validation and test sets use deterministic preprocessing only, with test-time augmentation as a deliberate inference-time exception.

**Further reading:**

- Shorten, C., Khoshgoftaar, T. M. (2019). [*A survey on Image Data Augmentation for Deep Learning*](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40537-019-0197-0). Journal of Big Data 6, 60. Broad overview of augmentation families; see the sections on geometric vs. photometric transforms and label safety.
- Zhang, H., Cissé, M., Dauphin, Y. N., Lopez-Paz, D. (2018). [*mixup: Beyond Empirical Risk Minimization*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.09412). ICLR 2018. Section 2 gives the formula and a self-contained PyTorch implementation in a few lines of code.

Section 3 gives the entire algorithm in two lines of Python; Figure 3 shows how optimal distortion magnitude scales with model size and dataset size.
