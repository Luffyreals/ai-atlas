## Intuition

### A classifier locked to a fixed label list

A standard image classifier trained on ImageNet has exactly 1,000 output neurons. It knows `"golden retriever"` and `"tabby cat"`. Ask it whether a photo shows a `"folding bike"`, however, and it is helpless: none of its 1,000 outputs corresponds to that concept. Adding a new class means collecting labeled photos and retraining the classifier head.

Meanwhile, the internet already contains hundreds of millions of images **paired with free-form text**: captions, HTML alt-text, and article titles. That text is noisy, but it describes visual concepts in open-ended natural language rather than a closed list of integer IDs.

### A matching game

Imagine playing a card game. Someone hands you 4 photos and 4 captions shuffled out of order. Your job is to match each photo with its true caption.

To win this game consistently, you do not need a fixed list of class numbers. You simply need to understand images and understand text well enough to tell when a photo and a sentence **describe the same thing**. Now imagine playing that game with batches of 32,768 photos and 32,768 captions at a time, across hundreds of millions of pairs. Along the way, you end up learning a remarkably broad visual-linguistic vocabulary.

Once you have mastered the matching game, image classification becomes just another round of matching: turn each candidate class name into a caption (`"a photo of a folding bike"`, `"a photo of a cat"`) and see which caption best matches the photo. Want to add a brand-new class? Just write one more sentence.

### Beyond matching: what comes next?

Matching answers "do this image and this sentence go together?"—but leaves two major capabilities open: **writing** a new caption from scratch for an image (rather than picking from a list), and **localizing** where a mentioned phrase sits inside the image with a bounding box. **BLIP** tackles the first job; **GLIP** tackles the second.

The central question of this lesson is: **how do we learn a shared embedding space from web image-text pairs so that matching images and sentences land near each other—and then how do we extend that idea to zero-shot classification (CLIP), caption generation (BLIP), and phrase-grounded object detection (GLIP)?**

## Theory

### Notation

A training **mini-batch** consists of $N$ paired examples $(\text{image}_i, \text{text}_i)$ (note that $N$ here is the batch size, not the patch count from the ViT lesson). An image encoder $f$ (a ViT or ResNet) and a text encoder $g$ (a Transformer) map image $i$ and caption $j$ into vectors of the same dimension $D_e$ in a **joint embedding space**. Dividing each vector by its Euclidean norm gives unit vectors:

$$
\mathbf{u}_i = \frac{f(\text{image}_i)}{\lVert f(\text{image}_i) \rVert},
\qquad
\mathbf{v}_j = \frac{g(\text{text}_j)}{\lVert g(\text{text}_j) \rVert}
$$

Because both $\mathbf{u}_i$ and $\mathbf{v}_j$ have unit length, their dot product $\mathbf{u}_i^\top \mathbf{v}_j$ equals their **cosine similarity**, bounded in $[-1, 1]$.

### Step 1: the $N \times N$ similarity matrix

Collect the dot products of all $N^2$ possible image-caption pairs in the batch into a matrix $\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$:

$$
S_{ij} = \mathbf{u}_i^\top \mathbf{v}_j
$$

Row $i$ compares image $i$ against all $N$ captions; column $j$ compares caption $j$ against all $N$ images. The $N$ diagonal entries $S_{ii}$ are the **true (positive) pairs**, while the $N^2 - N$ off-diagonal entries are **mismatched (negative) pairs**. No human class labels are required: the pairing within the batch itself provides the supervision.

### Step 2: each row is an $N$-way classification problem

Look at row $i$: "out of the $N$ captions in this batch, which one belongs to image $i$?" That is an $N$-class classification problem whose ground-truth answer is column $i$. Dividing the similarities by a temperature $\tau > 0$ and applying **softmax** across row $i$ gives a probability distribution:

$$
p_{ij} = \frac{\exp(S_{ij} / \tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^{N} \exp(S_{ik} / \tau)}
$$

The loss for row $i$ is the standard **cross-entropy** loss for target class $i$, namely $-\ln$ of the probability assigned to the true caption $i$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_i^{\text{img} \to \text{txt}} = -\ln p_{ii} = -\ln \frac{\exp(S_{ii} / \tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^{N} \exp(S_{ik} / \tau)}
$$

This "one positive sample against $N-1$ in-batch negatives scored by softmax" objective is called **InfoNCE** (van den Oord et al., 2018).

### Step 3: symmetrizing across columns

We can play the exact same game in reverse: "out of the $N$ images in this batch, which one belongs to caption $j$?" That applies softmax down **column** $j$ of the same matrix:

$$
\mathcal{L}_j^{\text{txt} \to \text{img}} = -\ln \frac{\exp(S_{jj} / \tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^{N} \exp(S_{kj} / \tau)}
$$

Notice that the denominator now sums over the row index $k$ down column $j$, while the numerator is still the diagonal entry $S_{jj}$. CLIP averages both directions across the batch:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_i^{\text{img} \to \text{txt}} + \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_j^{\text{txt} \to \text{img}}\right)
$$

In code, this is nothing more than standard cross-entropy on $\mathbf{S}/\tau$ and on $\mathbf{S}^\top/\tau$ with target labels $0, 1, \dots, N-1$. The CLIP paper's famous pseudocode expresses the entire loss in under ten lines.

### Step 4: gradients pull the diagonal up and push off-diagonals down

Why does this loss shape the embedding space the way we want? Rewrite the row-$i$ loss by splitting the log of the fraction into a difference of two logs:

$$
\mathcal{L}_i^{\text{img} \to \text{txt}} = -\frac{S_{ii}}{\tau} + \ln \sum_{k=1}^{N} \exp\!\left(\frac{S_{ik}}{\tau}\right)
$$

Now differentiate with respect to any entry $S_{ik}$ in row $i$. The first term depends only on $S_{ii}$, contributing $-1/\tau$ when $k = i$ and $0$ when $k \ne i$. The second term is a log-sum-exp, whose derivative with respect to $S_{ik}$ is $\frac{1}{\tau} \cdot \frac{\exp(S_{ik}/\tau)}{\sum_{k'} \exp(S_{ik'}/\tau)} = p_{ik}/\tau$. Combining both gives:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_i^{\text{img} \to \text{txt}}}{\partial S_{ik}} = \frac{p_{ik} - [k = i]}{\tau}
$$

where $[k = i]$ is $1$ on the diagonal and $0$ off the diagonal. Because gradient descent steps in the **negative** gradient direction:

- The **diagonal entry** has gradient $(p_{ii} - 1)/\tau < 0$, so $S_{ii}$ is **pulled up** until $p_{ii} \to 1$.
- Every **off-diagonal entry** ($k \ne i$) has gradient $p_{ik}/\tau > 0$, so $S_{ik}$ is **pushed down**—and the push is proportional to $p_{ik}$. A wrong caption that currently looks deceptively similar to image $i$ (a **hard negative** with large $p_{ik}$) receives the strongest downward push.

Larger batches expose each image to more hard negatives per step; CLIP trained with a massive batch size of $N = 32,768$.

### Step 5: temperature $\tau$

Because cosine similarities are bounded in $[-1, 1]$, setting $\tau = 1$ means two logits can differ by at most 2. Softmax over such narrow logit differences stays relatively flat: even if a model aligns every pair perfectly, the loss cannot drop near zero. Dividing by a small $\tau$ stretches the logit range: at $\tau = 0.01$, the maximum logit gap becomes 200.

On the flip side, if $\tau$ is too tiny, a single noisy negative pair whose cosine happens to edge out the true pair creates a huge gradient spike that destabilizes training. Rather than tuning $\tau$ as a fixed hyperparameter, CLIP **learns** $\tau$ during training by parameterizing $1/\tau = e^{t}$ (so $\tau$ stays strictly positive). Following the paper, $\tau$ is initialized to $0.07$ and clipped so that $1/\tau \le 100$ to prevent training instability. In our `LocalCode` cell below, inspecting the pretrained CLIP checkpoint prints `1/tau = 100.0`—showing that the learned scale hit the exact ceiling of 100.

Note that $\tau$ plays the exact same mathematical role here as the sampling temperature $T$ in our LLM lessons (dividing logits before softmax).

### CLIP: pretraining data and results

CLIP (*Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training*, Radford et al., 2021) was trained from scratch on **400 million** image-text pairs collected from the web. The authors trained five ResNet variants and three Vision Transformer variants (ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14) alongside a Transformer text encoder. Their largest model (ViT-L/14 fine-tuned at $336 \times 336$ resolution) achieved **76.2% zero-shot top-1 accuracy on ImageNet**, matching the accuracy of a supervised ResNet-50 without ever training on a single one of ImageNet's 1.28 million labeled images.

### Zero-shot classification

Suppose a downstream task has $K$ candidate classes. Turn each class name $k$ into a text prompt, pass it through the text encoder, and normalize to get $\mathbf{v}_k$. Given a test image with normalized embedding $\mathbf{u}$:

$$
\hat{y} = \arg\max_{k} \; \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}_k,
\qquad
P(y = k \mid \text{image}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}_k / \tau)}{\sum_{k'=1}^{K} \exp(\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}_{k'} / \tau)}
$$

This is simply a single row of the similarity matrix $\mathbf{S}$, with the $K$ class prompts standing in for captions. As the CLIP paper observes, you can view zero-shot CLIP as a bias-free **multinomial logistic regression classifier** whose weight matrix $[\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_K]$ is **synthesized on the fly by the text encoder** from natural-language class names.

**Why prompt engineering matters.** Web captions are rarely single isolated words, so feeding a bare class name like `"crane"` creates a distribution shift and leaves polysemous words ambiguous (a construction crane or a wading bird?). Using the default template `"A photo of a {label}."` boosts ImageNet accuracy by 1.3% right away, and domain-specific templates like `"A photo of a {label}, a type of pet."` help even more.

**Prompt ensembling.** Instead of relying on a single template, you can write $M$ diverse templates for each class (`"a photo of a big {label}"`, `"a blurry photo of a {label}"`, `"a close-up photo of a {label}"`, …), average their normalized text embeddings, and re-normalize the mean vector:

$$
\bar{\mathbf{v}}_k = \frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathbf{v}_k^{(m)}}{\left\lVert \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathbf{v}_k^{(m)} \right\rVert}
$$

(Dividing by $M$ before normalizing cancels out, so the $1/M$ factor is omitted.) Because the ensemble average $\bar{\mathbf{v}}_k$ is computed once in embedding space and cached, inference on new images costs no more than using a single prompt. Across 80 prompt templates, the CLIP paper reports an additional 3.5% gain on ImageNet over the single default prompt—nearly 5% combined.

### Known limitations of CLIP

- **Counting and abstract spatial tasks.** The CLIP paper notes that zero-shot CLIP struggles on object counting (CLEVRCounts) and estimating distance to the nearest car (KITTI Distance), sometimes performing near random chance. Our `LocalCode` script tests counting on three circles.
- **Word order and relational attribution.** Because an image is compressed into a single global vector and web-caption retrieval rarely requires parsing syntax, Yuksekgonul et al. (2023) showed that contrastive vision-language models often behave like **bags of words**: they struggle to distinguish *"the horse is eating the grass"* from *"the grass is eating the horse,"* scoring near or below chance on relational benchmarks.
- **Text inside images (typographic attacks vs. MNIST).** CLIP reads printed English text inside photos surprisingly well—so well that Goh et al. (2021) demonstrated **typographic attacks**: sticking a piece of paper with the word `"iPod"` onto a Granny Smith apple causes CLIP to classify the apple as an iPod. Conversely, on handwritten MNIST digits, zero-shot CLIP reaches only 88% accuracy, trailing simple logistic regression on raw pixels.
- **Polysemy and fine-grained categories.** Standalone labels like `"boxer"` (dog breed vs. athlete) or fine-grained distinctions between car models and flower species remain weaker than task-supervised models.

### BLIP: unified understanding and caption generation

Because CLIP has only two encoders and no text decoder, it can score candidate sentences but cannot generate a caption. **BLIP** (*Bootstrapping Language-Image Pre-training*, Li et al., 2022) unifies **understanding** (retrieval, visual question answering) and **generation** (image captioning) within a **Multimodal Mixture of Encoder-Decoder (MED)** architecture that operates in three modes with three joint objectives:

| Mode | Training objective | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Unimodal encoders (image encoder + text encoder) | **ITC** (Image-Text Contrastive): CLIP-style contrastive loss | Aligns global image and text embeddings for fast retrieval |
| Image-grounded text encoder (inserts cross-attention to image features) | **ITM** (Image-Text Matching): binary classification ("matched" vs. "unmatched") | Fine-grained token-to-patch alignment, sampling high-ITC in-batch pairs as **hard negatives** |
| Image-grounded text decoder (causal self-attention + cross-attention) | **LM** (Language Modeling): next-token cross-entropy loss | Generates natural-language captions conditioned on the image |

The text encoder and text decoder share all parameters except their self-attention layers (bidirectional for encoding vs. causal for decoding). Following ALBEF (Li et al., 2021), BLIP's ITC loss uses a momentum encoder to generate **soft pseudo-targets**, mitigating the false-negative issue when multiple captions in a batch describe similar images.

**CapFilt: bootstrapping clean captions from noisy web data.** Web alt-text is full of noise (`"IMG_2031.jpg"`, `"sale 50%"`). BLIP takes a pretrained MED model, fine-tunes two copies on human-annotated COCO data, and cleans the web dataset in two steps:

1. **Captioner** (MED in LM decoder mode): generates a synthetic caption for each web image using nucleus (top-$p$) sampling, which the authors found produces more diverse and informative training text than beam search.
2. **Filter** (MED in ITM mode): scores both the original web alt-text and the synthetic caption, discarding whichever ones the ITM head predicts as unmatched to the image.

Combining the surviving clean pairs with human-labeled data yields a bootstrapped dataset used to pretrain a stronger model (scaling from 14M images up to 129M images).

**BLIP-2** (Li et al., 2023) goes one step further for the LLM era: it **freezes** both a pretrained vision encoder and a pretrained LLM, training only a lightweight 188M-parameter bridge called a **Q-Former** (*Querying Transformer*). The Q-Former uses 32 learnable query vectors (each 768-dimensional) that interact with each other via self-attention and attend to the frozen image features via cross-attention, distilling the entire image into just $32 \times 768$ visual tokens—far more compact than the $257 \times 1,024$ patch tokens of ViT-L/14. A single linear layer projects those 32 tokens into the LLM's word-embedding space as a "soft visual prompt." How BLIP-2 and LLaVA connect vision encoders to LLMs is the focus of [Vision and Language (2): LLaVA and Variants](/en/cv/llava/).

### GLIP: phrase grounding as object detection

Both CLIP and BLIP operate on whole images. **Object detection**, by contrast, requires localizing where each object sits via a **bounding box** and a class label. A classical detector extracts feature vectors for $R$ candidate image regions, $\mathbf{O} \in \mathbb{R}^{R \times d}$, and multiplies them by a fixed classification weight matrix $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{c \times d}$ for $c$ predefined classes:

$$
\mathbf{S}_{\text{cls}} = \mathbf{O}\,\mathbf{W}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{R \times c}
$$

**GLIP** (*Grounded Language-Image Pre-training*, Li et al., 2022) observes that object detection is simply a special case of **phrase grounding** (locating which image regions correspond to which noun phrases in a sentence). Simply concatenate all candidate class names into a single text prompt—`"person. bicycle. car. ... toothbrush"`—pass the prompt through a language encoder to get $M$ contextual word token vectors $\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times d}$, and replace $\mathbf{W}$ with $\mathbf{P}$:

$$
\mathbf{S}_{\text{ground}} = \mathbf{O}\,\mathbf{P}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{R \times M}
$$

Entry $(r, m)$ scores the alignment between image region $r$ and word token $m$. Notice the parallel: GLIP plays the exact same dot-product matching game as CLIP's $\mathbf{S}$, but at the **region–word** level instead of the **image–sentence** level. Because the prompt is free-form text, detecting any new set of categories at test time only requires changing the prompt string.

Two architectural ingredients distinguish GLIP from a naive region-CLIP:

- **Deep cross-modality fusion.** In CLIP, image and text features never interact until the final dot product. In GLIP, multiple cross-attention fusion layers interleave the top stages of the vision and language backbones, so region features are already conditioned on what the text prompt is asking for before boxes are scored.
- **Scaling grounding data with self-training.** Human-annotated phrase-grounding datasets are small (~3M pairs), so the authors trained a teacher GLIP model on gold data and used it to predict pseudo-boxes for noun phrases parsed from 24 million web image-text pairs—training the student on 27 million grounded examples in total.

Without seeing a single COCO training image, GLIP-L achieved 49.8 zero-shot box AP on COCO val2017 and 26.9 AP on the long-tailed LVIS benchmark (2022 paper).

:::note[Key takeaway]
CLIP computes the $N \times N$ cosine matrix $S_{ij} = \mathbf{u}_i^\top \mathbf{v}_j$ and minimizes symmetric row- and column-wise cross-entropy on $\mathbf{S}/\tau$ (InfoNCE). Zero-shot classification evaluates one row of $\mathbf{S}$ against class-name prompts. BLIP adds ITM and LM objectives to unify understanding and caption generation, using CapFilt to clean noisy web text. GLIP brings contrastive alignment down to the region–word level via $\mathbf{S}_{\text{ground}} = \mathbf{O}\mathbf{P}^\top$ for open-vocabulary object detection.
:::

- **`tau=1.0`:** the true pairing achieves a loss of only `0.930`—not far below $\ln 4$—because cosine differences in $[-1, 1]$ are too narrow without temperature scaling, confirming Step 5.
- **`tau=0.1` and `tau=0.01`:** the matched loss drops to `0.023` and `0.000`, while the mismatched loss shoots up to `6.815` and `67.923`. Small $\tau$ makes softmax sharp and confident, rewarding right matches and heavily penalizing mismatches.
- **Zero-shot classification:** the new `"dog"` image has cosines `[-0.57, 0.78, 0.23, 0.25]` with the 4 label vectors; dividing by CLIP's initial $\tau = 0.07$ and applying softmax puts `0.9991` probability on `"dog"`. The final line takes `argmax` along each row of $\mathbf{S}$, recovering `['cat', 'dog', 'car', 'tree']`.

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Forgetting to normalize embeddings, or comparing softmax probabilities across different label sets]
If you skip L2 normalization when calling `model.get_image_features` and `model.get_text_features` directly, their dot product is no longer cosine similarity—vectors with larger norms will dominate the score regardless of semantic alignment. Always divide both embeddings by their `.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)`. Also remember that zero-shot probabilities come from a softmax **over the specific candidate labels you passed in**: a score of `0.94` means "94% relative to these 5 prompts," not an absolute calibrated confidence. Adding or removing a candidate label changes every probability in the row.
:::

:::caution[Assuming zero-shot CLIP works out of the box on every domain]
CLIP aligns concepts that appear frequently in web captions. It is weak on exact counting, spatial prepositions (*"to the left of"*), handwritten digits, and specialized scientific or medical imagery (such as chest X-rays or satellite bands), and it is vulnerable to typographic attacks when text is visible in the scene. Always evaluate on a small labeled validation set in your target domain before deploying, and experiment with domain-specific prompt templates (`"a satellite photo of {label}"`) or prompt ensembling before concluding that CLIP fails.
:::

:::caution[Using CLIP when your task requires spatial localization]
CLIP compresses an entire image into a single vector, so it can tell you *whether* a cat is present, but not *where* the cat is or *how many* cats there are. If you need bounding boxes for open-vocabulary phrases, use a grounded detector like GLIP; if you need generated descriptions or visual question answering, use BLIP or the vision-language models in [Vision and Language (2): LLaVA and Variants](/en/cv/llava/).
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- CLIP learns by playing an in-batch matching game: given $N$ image–text pairs, the cosine matrix $S_{ij} = \mathbf{u}_i^\top \mathbf{v}_j$ has positives on the diagonal and negatives off the diagonal, trained with symmetric row- and column-wise cross-entropy on $\mathbf{S}/\tau$ (InfoNCE). Gradients pull diagonal entries up and push hard negatives down hardest.
- The temperature $\tau$ controls softmax sharpness: too large leaves the loss flat, too small destabilizes gradients. CLIP learns $\tau$ from an initial value of $0.07$, clamped so $1/\tau \le 100$.
- Zero-shot classification encodes class-name prompts (`"a photo of a {label}"`) into text vectors and picks the highest cosine similarity; prompt ensembling averages multiple templates in embedding space for a free accuracy boost. Known weak spots include counting, spatial relations, typographic attacks, and specialized domains.
- BLIP combines ITC, ITM, and causal LM objectives to unify understanding and caption generation while cleaning noisy web text via CapFilt; BLIP-2 bridges a frozen vision encoder and a frozen LLM with a lightweight Q-Former. GLIP reformulates object detection as phrase grounding via region–word alignment $\mathbf{S}_{\text{ground}} = \mathbf{O}\mathbf{P}^\top$.
