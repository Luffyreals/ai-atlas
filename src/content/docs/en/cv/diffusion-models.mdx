## Intuition

### Dropping ink into a glass of water

Drop a bead of ink into a glass of water. At first you can still see the shape of the droplet. A few seconds later it clouds outward. A few minutes later, the entire glass is a uniform pale gray. That physical process is **diffusion**, and in nature it only runs one way: nobody ever sees a gray glass of water spontaneously gather itself back into a drop of ink.

A diffusion model does the exact same thing to an image. Take a clean image and add a tiny amount of random noise. Repeat a few hundred times. Eventually the image is indistinguishable from pure static, with no trace of the original picture left.

The magic is learning to run the movie backward. Each forward step only corrupts the image **a tiny bit**. Looking at a slightly noisy image, guessing "which part of this is the noise that was just added" is manageable. If we can train a neural network to do that small cleanup step, we can start from pure random noise and apply the network over and over: each step peels off a little noise until a brand-new image emerges.

### Why not generate an image in a single step?

GANs and VAEs generate an image in a single forward pass: from $\mathbf{z}$ straight to pixels. That is a giant leap from random numbers to a finished picture. A GAN learns that leap through a delicate adversarial game; a VAE learns it with a pixel reconstruction loss that averages over possibilities and blurs fine detail.

Diffusion breaks that one giant leap into a thousand small steps. Each step is an ordinary regression problem: look at a noisy image, predict the noise. Training is as stable as fitting a standard regression network—no adversarial game required. The trade-off is that generating a single image requires running the network many times in sequence.

### From digits to "an astronaut riding a horse"

On $8 \times 8$ digits, this idea works right out of the box. But a $512 \times 512$ color image has nearly 800,000 numbers, and running a large network a thousand times on that many numbers is painfully slow. On top of that, we want to steer the image with a text prompt like "an astronaut riding a horse," and we want to teach the model a new artistic style without retraining nearly a billion parameters.

Those three practical needs motivate the three pieces of Stable Diffusion covered in this lesson: denoising in a compressed latent space, conditioning on text, and LoRA.

**The core question of this lesson: how do we train a network to reverse the noise-adding process so it can walk from pure noise to a new image, and how do we make that fast on high-resolution images, faithful to a text prompt, and tunable with a tiny fraction of the parameters?**

## Theory

### Notation

The clean image is $\mathbf{x}_0$, and the image after $t$ noise steps is $\mathbf{x}_t$, for $t = 1, \dots, T$. **Throughout this lesson $T$ is the number of diffusion steps**, not the sampling temperature $T$ from the LLM lessons. Each step has a small number $\beta_t$ called the **noise variance** at step $t$; the sequence $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T$ is the **noise schedule**. We also define:

$$
\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t,
\qquad
\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s = \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \cdots \alpha_t
$$

Here $\boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ is standard Gaussian noise with the same shape as the image: every element is independent with mean 0 and variance 1. DDPM (Ho, Jain, & Abbeel, 2020) scales pixel values to $[-1, 1]$ first so the clean image and standard normal noise live on the same scale.

### Forward process: adding noise step by step

A single noise-adding step in DDPM is:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_{t-1}
$$

where $\boldsymbol\epsilon_{t-1}$ is a fresh standard normal noise sample, independent of all previous steps. This is the **forward process**. Nothing in it is learned; it is a fixed formula.

Why scale the previous image by $\sqrt{\alpha_t}$ instead of simply adding noise? Suppose each element of $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ has variance 1. Then the scaled image term contributes variance $\alpha_t$ and the noise term contributes variance $1 - \alpha_t$, which sum to $\alpha_t + (1 - \alpha_t) = 1$. The overall variance stays at 1 across all $T$ steps instead of blowing up; only the mix between "signal" and "noise" shifts.

DDPM uses $T = 1000$ and a linear schedule where $\beta_t$ increases linearly from $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 0.02$. Early steps add a whisper of noise; later steps add much more.

### Closed form: jumping straight to step $t$

If we need $\mathbf{x}_{500}$ during training, do we have to run the loop 500 times? Thankfully, no. We can derive a closed-form shortcut straight from $\mathbf{x}_0$.

**Step 1: unroll one step.** Express $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ in terms of $\mathbf{x}_{t-2}$ using the same step rule and substitute it into the equation for $\mathbf{x}_t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{x}_t &= \sqrt{\alpha_t}\Big(\sqrt{\alpha_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_{t-1}}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_{t-2}\Big) + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_{t-1} \\
&= \sqrt{\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-2} + \sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_{t-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line simply distributes $\sqrt{\alpha_t}$ across the parentheses.

**Step 2: merge the two noise terms into one.** The last two terms are independent zero-mean Gaussian variables with variances $\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})$ and $1-\alpha_t$. Because the sum of two independent Gaussians is another Gaussian whose variance is the sum of the two variances:

$$
\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1}) + (1-\alpha_t) = 1 - \alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}
$$

the sum of those two noise terms has the exact same distribution as $\sqrt{1-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\bar{\boldsymbol\epsilon}$ for a single standard normal vector $\bar{\boldsymbol\epsilon}$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\bar{\boldsymbol\epsilon}
$$

**Step 3: unroll all the way back to $\mathbf{x}_0$.** This two-step formula has the exact same form as the one-step formula, with $\alpha_t$ replaced by the product $\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}$. Unrolling $\mathbf{x}_{t-2}$, then $\mathbf{x}_{t-3}$, and so on down to $\mathbf{x}_0$ turns the coefficient into the cumulative product $\bar\alpha_t$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon,
\qquad \boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})
$$

This is the centerpiece of the forward process. Under DDPM's linear schedule (numbers taken from the NumPy cell below):

| $t$ | $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ (signal weight) | $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ (noise weight) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 | 0.9471 | 0.3209 |
| 250 | 0.7239 | 0.6899 |
| 500 | 0.2803 | 0.9599 |
| 1000 | 0.0064 | 1.0000 |

At $t = 1000$, the original image weight is down to 0.0064: $\mathbf{x}_T$ is virtually pure standard normal noise. That is why image generation at test time can start from a freshly drawn standard normal sample.

:::note[Key takeaway]
$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon$. With $\bar\alpha_t$ precomputed, we can jump to a noisy image at **any** step $t$ in a single addition, without simulating the steps before it.
:::

### Training: predicting the noise

Look again at the closed-form equation. If you are given $\mathbf{x}_t$, the step index $t$, and the exact noise $\boldsymbol\epsilon$ that was mixed in, you can solve directly for the clean image:

$$
\mathbf{x}_0 = \frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}
$$

(subtract the noise term and divide by $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$). Thus, "predicting the noise" and "predicting the clean image" are two ways of phrasing the exact same problem. DDPM chooses to train a network to predict the noise. Call that network $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$: it takes the noisy image and the step index $t$, and outputs a tensor of the same shape as the image. Passing $t$ tells the network how noisy the input is expected to be.

Ho et al. derive the training objective from a variational lower bound on the log-likelihood (Section 3 of the paper) and show that a simplified, unweighted version produces better sample quality. That simplified loss is just the mean squared error between the true noise and the predicted noise:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0,\, t,\, \boldsymbol\epsilon}\Big[\big\lVert \boldsymbol\epsilon - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon,\; t\big)\big\rVert^2\Big]
$$

A single training step (Algorithm 1 in the paper) is therefore remarkably compact: pick a clean image $\mathbf{x}_0$, sample $t$ uniformly from $\{1, \dots, T\}$, draw $\boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$, form $\mathbf{x}_t$ with the closed-form formula, and take a gradient descent step on $\lVert \boldsymbol\epsilon - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\rVert^2$. The closed-form jump is what makes training cheap: each training example costs a single weighted sum, even when $t = 900$.

In DDPM, $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta$ is a **U-Net**: a convolutional network with symmetric downsampling and upsampling paths whose output has the exact same spatial size as its input (see [Semantic Segmentation](/en/cv/semantic-segmentation/)). The integer step $t$ is converted into a sinusoidal embedding (just like Transformer positional encodings) and added into every block of the U-Net. One shared network handles all $T$ steps.

### Sampling: stepping backward

Once $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta$ is trained, we generate a new image using Algorithm 2 of the paper. Start from pure noise $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$, and for $t = T, T-1, \dots, 1$ compute:

$$
\mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\right) + \sigma_t\,\mathbf{z}
$$

where $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ when $t > 1$ and $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$ on the final step $t = 1$. Reading the formula from the inside out:

- **Subtract a fraction of the predicted noise.** The network predicts the total cumulative noise $\boldsymbol\epsilon$; a single reverse step only removes the slice corresponding to step $t$, scaled by the small factor $\beta_t/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$.
- **Divide by $\sqrt{\alpha_t}$.** The forward step multiplied the signal by $\sqrt{\alpha_t}$, so dividing restores the scale.
- **Add a small amount of fresh noise** $\sigma_t\mathbf{z}$. Many slightly different $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ images could plausibly have produced $\mathbf{x}_t$. Injecting fresh noise samples one of those possibilities, preserving diversity. The paper tests $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ alongside a second variance choice and finds both work similarly well.

The expression inside the parentheses is the mean of the reverse transition derived in the paper (Equation 11), which we do not re-derive here. The crucial practical point: every reverse step runs the network once, so generating a single image with DDPM costs $T = 1000$ U-Net evaluations.

### DDIM: fewer steps without retraining

Running a large U-Net 1,000 times per image is slow. DDIM (Song, Meng, & Ermon, 2021) keeps the **exact same** trained network $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta$ and changes only the reverse update rule. At step $t$, it first estimates the clean image using the algebraic inversion formula from earlier:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}
$$

and then plugs $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ and the predicted noise back into the closed-form forward equation to jump directly to any earlier step $t' < t$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_{t'} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t'}}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t'}}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)
$$

This is the deterministic version of DDIM: no fresh random noise is added. Because $t'$ can be dozens of steps below $t$, you only need around 50 steps instead of 1,000. The paper reports that DDIM produces high-quality samples 10 to 50 times faster in wall-clock time than DDPM.

### Latent diffusion: denoising in a compressed space

A $512 \times 512 \times 3$ image contains $786\,432$ numbers. Running a U-Net dozens of times on a tensor that size for every generated image is expensive, and training it is even more expensive. Worse, most of those numbers describe imperceptible high-frequency pixel variations.

Rombach et al. (2022) proposed **latent diffusion**, splitting the job into two stages:

1. **A pretrained autoencoder, trained once.** An encoder $\mathcal{E}$ compresses image $\mathbf{x}$ into a much smaller latent tensor $\mathbf{z} = \mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})$, and a decoder $\mathcal{D}$ reconstructs the image from $\mathbf{z}$. The main autoencoder in the paper includes a light KL penalty toward a standard normal prior—a close cousin of the VAE from the previous lesson.
2. **Diffusion inside the latent space.** Every DDPM/DDIM equation stays identical, except the clean image $\mathbf{x}_0$ is replaced by the clean latent $\mathbf{z}_0$. The training loss becomes $\mathbb{E}\big[\lVert \boldsymbol\epsilon - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t)\rVert^2\big]$. At generation time, once the final latent is denoised, a single pass through $\mathcal{D}$ turns it back into a full-resolution pixel image.

In Stable Diffusion v1, the autoencoder downsamples each spatial side by a factor of $f = 8$ and outputs 4 latent channels: an $H \times W \times 3$ image becomes an $H/8 \times W/8 \times 4$ latent. For a $512 \times 512 \times 3$ image:

$$
\frac{512 \cdot 512 \cdot 3}{64 \cdot 64 \cdot 4} = \frac{786\,432}{16\,384} = 48
$$

The diffusion U-Net operates on a tensor with 48 times fewer numbers (computed). The paper experiments with downsampling factors $f$ from 1 to 32 and finds that $f = 4$ and $f = 8$ hit the sweet spot between visual fidelity and speed; larger $f$ values lose too much detail.

### Text conditioning: cross-attention

To steer generation with a text prompt $y$, latent diffusion encodes the prompt with a text encoder $\tau_\theta$ and injects those features into the U-Net via **cross-attention**. Let $\boldsymbol\varphi(\mathbf{z}_t)$ denote the U-Net's intermediate feature map at some layer, flattened into a sequence of spatial positions. A cross-attention layer computes:

$$
\mathbf{Q} = \boldsymbol\varphi(\mathbf{z}_t)\,\mathbf{W}_Q,
\quad
\mathbf{K} = \tau_\theta(y)\,\mathbf{W}_K,
\quad
\mathbf{V} = \tau_\theta(y)\,\mathbf{W}_V
$$

followed by $\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top/\sqrt{d})\,\mathbf{V}$ just like standard attention. Queries come from the image features, while keys and values come from the text: each spatial position in the latent "asks" the prompt tokens what it should draw there. (The paper writes these products with column vectors as $\mathbf{W}_Q\,\boldsymbol\varphi$; we write row vectors here to match [Multimodal](/en/llm/multimodal/).)

In the original latent diffusion paper, the text-to-image model trained a Transformer text encoder jointly with the U-Net. **Stable Diffusion v1** (2022), built on latent diffusion, replaces $\tau_\theta$ with the **frozen** text encoder of CLIP ViT-L/14. According to the official README, Stable Diffusion v1 has an 860M-parameter U-Net and a 123M-parameter text encoder, trained on a subset of LAION-5B first at $256 \times 256$ and then fine-tuned at $512 \times 512$. Because CLIP was trained to align images with text (see [Vision and Language (1): CLIP, BLIP, GLIP](/en/cv/clip-blip-glip/)), its text features already carry visual meaning.

### Classifier-free guidance

Conditional diffusion models often treat the prompt too gently: the generated image looks plausible, but only loosely matches the text. **Classifier-free guidance** (Ho & Salimans, 2022) amplifies the prompt's influence without needing a separate classifier network.

**During training:** randomly replace the text prompt with an empty prompt $\varnothing$ a fraction of the time (Stable Diffusion v1-4 drops the condition 10% of the time). That teaches a single network to do both jobs: predict noise with a prompt, $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{c})$, and without one, $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing)$.

**During sampling:** run the network twice at each step and extrapolate. The paper writes the combined prediction with weight $w$:

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol\epsilon} = (1+w)\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{c}) - w\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing)
$$

Adding and subtracting $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing)$ and setting the **guidance scale** $s = 1 + w$ gives the form used in most libraries:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\boldsymbol\epsilon} &= \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing) + (1+w)\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{c}) - (1+w)\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing) \\
&= \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing) + s\,\big(\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{c}) - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing)\big)
\end{aligned}
$$

The difference in parentheses is "the direction in which the prompt pulls the image." Setting $s = 0$ gives unconditional generation, $s = 1$ gives standard conditional generation, and $s > 1$ pushes further along the prompt direction. Stable Diffusion v1's sampling script defaults to $s = 7.5$. As the paper shows, increasing the guidance scale trades sample diversity for sharper, more prompt-faithful individual images; pushing it too high makes images oversaturated and repetitive.

### LoRA for diffusion

Suppose you want the model to paint in your own illustration style, or draw your specific pet cat. Fine-tuning all 860 million parameters of the U-Net takes heavy GPU memory, and saving each custom style produces a multi-gigabyte checkpoint.

**LoRA** (Hu et al., 2022) was originally developed for LLMs and derived in detail in [Model Evaluation and PEFT](/en/llm/evaluation-peft/). To recap: given a frozen weight matrix $\mathbf{W}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$, LoRA learns two low-rank matrices $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times r}$ and $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}$ with small rank $r$:

$$
\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{W}_0\mathbf{x} + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
$$

Each adapted matrix learns only $r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}})$ parameters, and initializing $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{0}$ ensures the adapted model starts out identical to the base model.

In Stable Diffusion, the natural place to attach LoRA is the **U-Net's attention layers**: the four linear projections for queries, keys, values, and output (`to_q`, `to_k`, `to_v`, `to_out`) across every self-attention and cross-attention layer. That is the exact setup in the `diffusers` library's LoRA training script, with default rank $r = 4$. Cross-attention is where text meets image, so adapting those projections directly adjusts how words map to visual features.

Let's count parameters for a single cross-attention layer at a $d = 320$ channel stage with 768-dimensional CLIP text features and $r = 4$: `to_q` and `to_out` are $320 \to 320$, costing $4 \cdot 640 = 2\,560$ parameters each; `to_k` and `to_v` are $768 \to 320$, costing $4 \cdot 1\,088 = 4\,352$ parameters each. Together that is $13\,824$ numbers. Across all 32 attention layers of Stable Diffusion v1's U-Net, the PyTorch cell below counts $797\,184$ trainable parameters—about 0.09% of the U-Net. Stored in 32-bit float, that is roughly 3.2 MB (computed) instead of several gigabytes.

**DreamBooth** (Ruiz et al., 2023) is a complementary personalization technique: given just a few photos of a specific subject, it binds that subject to a rare identifier token in the prompt. While original DreamBooth fine-tuned the full model, in practice it is commonly paired with LoRA to keep memory and file sizes tiny.

:::note[Key takeaway]
Stable Diffusion = an autoencoder that compresses images ($f = 8$, 4-channel latent) + a U-Net predicting noise in latent space + a frozen CLIP text encoder wired into the U-Net via cross-attention + classifier-free guidance at sampling time. LoRA adapts the attention projections using less than 0.1% of the U-Net's parameters.
:::

### Limitations

- **Slow sampling.** Every reverse step runs the U-Net once—or twice with classifier-free guidance. DDIM cuts the step count to ~50; newer distillation and flow-based samplers cut it further, beyond the scope of this lesson.
- **Noise schedules are not interchangeable.** Stable Diffusion v1 uses a different schedule from DDPM's linear $10^{-4} \to 0.02$ (see "Common pitfalls" below), and our 2D PyTorch example has to rescale $\beta_t$ because it uses a smaller $T$.
- **Visual fidelity is bounded by the autoencoder.** Any fine detail lost during latent compression—such as tiny text or distant faces—cannot be recovered by the diffusion process.

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Thinking the network learns to draw the whole image in one shot]
The network $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta$ only learns a modest local task: look at a noisy input at step $t$ and estimate the noise. The final picture emerges only after chaining dozens to thousands of network evaluations. Consequently, the "number of sampling steps" is a real inference knob: if you use too few steps with a standard DDPM sampler, the output remains noisy or distorted; switch to a sampler like DDIM instead of simply truncating DDPM's step loop.
:::

:::caution[Adding noise sequentially during training, or copying a noise schedule blindly]
During training, always jump to $\mathbf{x}_t$ in one shot using $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon$; running $t$ sequential steps gives the same distribution at $t$ times the compute cost. Also remember that a noise schedule is tied to $T$ and to the specific model: the linear $10^{-4} \to 0.02$ schedule is calibrated for $T = 1000$ (our PyTorch script had to multiply it by 10 for $T = 100$), while Stable Diffusion v1 uses a scaled-linear schedule from 0.00085 to 0.012. When sampling from a pretrained checkpoint, always load the schedule from its own configuration file—and scale inputs to the range the model expects, such as $[-1, 1]$ for DDPM.
:::

:::caution[Cranking the guidance scale as high as possible, or assuming LoRA shrinks the model in memory]
A larger guidance scale $s$ makes images follow the prompt more tightly, but sacrifices diversity, and pushing $s$ too high causes oversaturated, unnatural artifacts. Start from the model's default (7.5 for Stable Diffusion v1) and adjust gradually. Remember too that classifier-free guidance doubles the per-step compute cost because the U-Net runs twice per step. Finally, LoRA only shrinks the **trainable parameters** and the saved adapter file: at inference time, you still need the full 860M-parameter U-Net in memory, just as in [Model Evaluation and PEFT](/en/llm/evaluation-peft/).
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- Diffusion models corrupt an image with Gaussian noise over $T$ small steps (where $T$ is the step count, not temperature) and train a network to reverse the process. Because independent Gaussians sum to a Gaussian, we can jump to any step in closed form: $\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon$.
- DDPM trains a noise-prediction network $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ with simple MSE loss and generates images by stepping backward 1,000 times from pure noise. DDIM reuses the exact same network to skip across steps, needing only ~50 steps.
- Stable Diffusion runs diffusion inside the latent space of a pretrained autoencoder (shrinking a $512 \times 512 \times 3$ image into a $64 \times 64 \times 4$ latent, 48 times fewer numbers), injects CLIP text features via cross-attention, and uses classifier-free guidance $\boldsymbol\epsilon_\varnothing + s(\boldsymbol\epsilon_{\mathbf{c}} - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\varnothing)$ to follow the prompt.
- Attaching LoRA to the U-Net's attention projections with rank $r = 4$ trains only ~0.8M parameters—under 0.1% of the U-Net—which is enough to teach a custom style or subject.
