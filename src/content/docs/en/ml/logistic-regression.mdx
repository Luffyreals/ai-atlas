## Intuition

A class advisor recorded the weekly study hours of 18 students before an exam, along with whether each passed or failed. The numbers below represent synthetic data generated for this lesson.

| Result | Weekly Study Hours |
|---|---|
| Fail (9 students) | 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4.5, 6 |
| Pass (9 students) | 4, 5, 5.5, 6.5, 7, 7.5, 8, 9, 10 |

At a glance: studying less often leads to failing, studying more often leads to passing, with an overlapping region in between (a student with 4 hours passed, while students with 4.5 and 6 hours failed). The teacher wants to answer: **if a student studies for 5 hours, what is their probability of passing?**

This is a **classification** problem: the label $y$ takes only two values, 1 (pass) or 0 (fail). What the teacher needs is not just a discrete "pass or fail", but a **probability**: 90% and 51% both predict "pass", but carry vastly different confidence levels.

### Trying Linear Regression with a Threshold

The first intuitive attempt is to treat 0 and 1 as numerical targets, fit an ordinary least squares line as in [Linear Regression](/en/ml/linear-regression/), and predict "pass" whenever the predicted value is at least 0.5. For our 18 students, the fitted line is $\hat{y} = 0.1413x - 0.1828$. The 0.5 threshold falls at $x = 4.83$ hours, correctly classifying 16 out of 18 students. While seemingly reasonable, this approach suffers from two fundamental flaws.

**First, predictions are not valid probabilities.** A student studying 0 hours receives $\hat{y} = -0.183$, while 10 hours yields $\hat{y} = 1.230$. Probabilities below 0 or above 1 are mathematically meaningless.

**Second, the line is distorted by "overly correct" points.** Suppose a student studies for 30 hours and naturally passes. This extreme point makes the classification problem no harder. Yet the regression line must stretch to reach $(30, 1)$, tilting the line downward: $\hat{y} = 0.0421x + 0.2671$. The 0.5 threshold shifts from 4.83 to **5.53 hours**. Consequently, students studying 5 and 5.5 hours—who actually passed—are now misclassified as failures: accuracy on the original 18 students drops to 14/18. Mean squared error penalizes the 30-hour student simply because $\hat{y}$ exceeds 1, even though in classification, being "confidently correct" should never be penalized.

### Squashing Outputs into (0, 1)

What we need is an S-shaped curve: hovering near 0 for very low study hours, approaching 1 for high study hours, and transitioning smoothly from 0 to 1 through the overlapping zone. At both extremes, the curve must flatten **horizontally**, so an extreme point at 30 hours that is already near 1 exerts almost no pull on the curve. This is the sigmoid curve, and logistic regression is the method for fitting it.

The objective is: **given feature values of a sample, estimate the probability that it belongs to class 1 using an S-shaped curve, choosing parameters that maximize the likelihood of observing the training data.**

## Theory

### Problem Formulation

We have $m$ training samples. The $i$-th sample has a feature vector $\mathbf{x}^{(i)}$ with $n$ dimensions and a label $y^{(i)} \in \{0, 1\}$. Class 1 is called the positive class, and class 0 is the negative class. For our 18 students, $m = 18$, $n = 1$, and $x$ is study hours.

Logistic regression proceeds in two stages. The first stage mirrors linear regression by computing a linear score:

$$
z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b
$$

where $\mathbf{w}$ represents weights and $b$ is the intercept (bias). Here $z$ can take any real value and is called the **logit**. The second stage passes $z$ through the **sigmoid function** $\sigma$ to produce a valid probability:

$$
\hat{y} = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad \hat{y} = P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})
$$

Unlike linear regression where $\hat{y}$ is a continuous target prediction, $\hat{y}$ here represents the **model's estimated probability for class 1**. The probability of class 0 is simply $1 - \hat{y}$.

### The Sigmoid Function

Inspecting the formula reveals why it produces an S-shaped curve:

- When $z \to +\infty$: $e^{-z} \to 0$, so $\sigma(z) \to 1/1 = 1$.
- When $z \to -\infty$: $e^{-z} \to +\infty$, so $\sigma(z) \to 0$.
- When $z = 0$: $e^{0} = 1$, so $\sigma(0) = 1/2$.

Because $e^{-z} > 0$ for all real $z$, the denominator strictly exceeds 1, guaranteeing that $\sigma(z)$ lies strictly **inside** the open interval $(0, 1)$, never reaching exactly 0 or 1. The function is symmetric around $(0, 0.5)$: $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$. Representative values include:

| $z$ | $-6$ | $-4$ | $-2$ | $0$ | $2$ | $4$ | $6$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $\sigma(z)$ | 0.0025 | 0.018 | 0.1192 | 0.5 | 0.8808 | 0.982 | 0.9975 |

The curve is steepest around $z = 0$, and flattens out almost entirely once $\lvert z\rvert > 5$: exactly the behavior desired in the Intuition section. The diagram in the Visualization section illustrates this curve.

### Odds and Log-Odds: Interpreting the Model Linearly

The **odds** of a probability $p$ is the ratio $p / (1 - p)$: the probability of occurrence divided by non-occurrence. A probability of 0.8 corresponds to odds of $0.8 / 0.2 = 4$ ("4 to 1"). A probability of 0.5 has odds of 1. Odds range from 0 to $+\infty$.

Inverting $\hat{y} = \sigma(z)$ allows us to express $z$ in terms of $\hat{y}$. Invert both sides, subtract 1, and take the natural logarithm:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{\hat{y}} &= 1 + e^{-z} \\
\frac{1 - \hat{y}}{\hat{y}} &= e^{-z} \\
\ln\frac{\hat{y}}{1 - \hat{y}} &= z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b
\end{aligned}
$$

The second step subtracts 1 from both sides and takes a common denominator: $\frac{1}{\hat{y}} - 1 = \frac{1 - \hat{y}}{\hat{y}}$. The third step flips the fraction (turning $e^{-z}$ into $e^{z}$) and takes $\ln$. The left-hand side is the **log-odds**, also called the logit of $\hat{y}$. Hence:

:::note[Key Takeaway]
Logistic regression is a **linear model for log-odds**: $\ln\frac{\hat{y}}{1 - \hat{y}} = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$. The name "logistic" comes from the logistic function, another name for the sigmoid.
:::

This formulation provides a clear interpretation for weights. Increasing $x_j$ by 1 unit while holding other features constant increases log-odds by exactly $w_j$, meaning odds are **multiplied by $e^{w_j}$**:

$$
\text{odds}(x_j + 1) = e^{z + w_j} = e^{z} \cdot e^{w_j} = \text{odds}(x_j) \cdot e^{w_j}
$$

In our first code cell, fitting the 18 students gives $w = 1.263$ and $b = -6.006$. Each additional hour of study multiplies the odds of passing by $e^{1.263} \approx 3.54$. A student studying 4 hours has $\hat{y} = 0.278$ with odds 0.386; studying 5 hours gives $\hat{y} = 0.577$ with odds 1.365, exactly $3.54\times$ greater. Notice that probability does **not** increase linearly: moving from 4 to 5 hours boosts probability by 0.3, while moving from 9 to 10 hours changes it minimally as it approaches saturation near 1. If $w_j$ is negative, $e^{w_j} < 1$, reducing the odds.

### Decision Boundary

To assign a discrete predicted class, we compare $\hat{y}$ against a **threshold**, typically 0.5 by default: predicting class 1 when $\hat{y} \ge 0.5$. Because $\sigma$ is strictly monotonic and $\sigma(0) = 0.5$:

$$
\hat{y} \ge 0.5 \iff z \ge 0 \iff \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b \ge 0
$$

The set of points where $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = 0$ defines the **decision boundary**. For one feature, it is a single point: $x = -b / w = 6.006 / 1.263 \approx 4.75$ hours. For two features, it is a line $w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b = 0$; for $n$ features, it is a hyperplane. The non-linear S-curve only serves to map real numbers to probabilities; the decision boundary itself is always **flat (linear)**. Thus, logistic regression is fundamentally a **linear classifier**.

The 0.5 threshold is merely a default. When missing a positive case carries greater penalty than a false alarm (e.g., medical diagnosis), one lowers the threshold. Choosing thresholds and evaluating classifiers beyond simple accuracy is covered in [Model Evaluation: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1](/en/ml/classification-metrics/).

### Loss Function: From Maximum Likelihood to Log Loss

We need an objective function measuring model performance. The principle of **maximum likelihood** states: choose parameters that make the **observed data most probable** under the model.

**Step 1: Probability of an individual sample.** The model specifies $P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = \hat{y}$ and $P(y = 0 \mid \mathbf{x}) = 1 - \hat{y}$. We unify both cases into a single concise expression:

$$
P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \hat{y}^{\,y}\,(1 - \hat{y})^{1 - y}
$$

Verification: when $y = 1$, the second factor becomes $(1 - \hat{y})^0 = 1$, leaving $\hat{y}$. When $y = 0$, the first factor becomes $\hat{y}^0 = 1$, leaving $1 - \hat{y}$. This is the Bernoulli distribution.

**Step 2: Probability of the entire dataset.** Assuming training samples are independent and identically distributed, the joint probability of observing all $m$ labels is the product:

$$
\text{Lik}(\mathbf{w}, b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\hat{y}^{(i)}\right)^{y^{(i)}}\left(1 - \hat{y}^{(i)}\right)^{1 - y^{(i)}}
$$

This quantity, viewed as a function of the parameters, is the **likelihood**. We seek to maximize it.

**Step 3: Taking the logarithm.** The product of many numbers strictly less than 1 rapidly underflows to 0. For the optimal model on our 18 students, likelihood is merely 0.0062; with thousands of samples, floating-point arithmetic rounds it to zero. Because $\ln$ is strictly increasing, maximizing $\ln\text{Lik}$ is equivalent to maximizing $\text{Lik}$. Applying $\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b$ and $\ln a^k = k\ln a$:

$$
\ln\text{Lik}(\mathbf{w}, b) = \sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[y^{(i)}\ln\hat{y}^{(i)} + \left(1 - y^{(i)}\right)\ln\left(1 - \hat{y}^{(i)}\right)\right]
$$

**Step 4: Converting to a minimization problem.** In machine learning, loss functions are conventionally minimized. Negating the log-likelihood and averaging over $m$ samples yields **log loss**, also known as **binary cross-entropy**:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, b) = -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[y^{(i)}\ln\hat{y}^{(i)} + \left(1 - y^{(i)}\right)\ln\left(1 - \hat{y}^{(i)}\right)\right]
$$

Minimizing $\mathcal{L}$ directly maximizes likelihood since $\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{m}\ln\text{Lik}$.

Examining each sample: every point contributes **$-\ln$ of the probability assigned to its true label**. Assigning 0.9 to the true label incurs a loss of 0.105; 0.5 incurs 0.693; 0.1 incurs 2.303; approaching 0 incurs nearly infinite penalty. Log loss heavily punishes **confident incorrect predictions**. Among our 18 students, the two most heavily penalized samples are the two misclassified students: the one studying 4 hours who passed ($\hat{y} = 0.278$, loss $-\ln 0.278 = 1.28$) and the one studying 6 hours who failed ($\hat{y} = 0.828$, loss $-\ln 0.172 = 1.76$). Averaged over all 18 students, $\mathcal{L} = 0.2826$. An uninformative model with $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}, b = 0$ giving every student 0.5 yields $\mathcal{L} = \ln 2 \approx 0.6931$.

### Why Not Mean Squared Error

Why not retain MSE $\frac{1}{m}\sum_i\left(\hat{y}^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)^2$ with $\hat{y} = \sigma(z)$? Consider a sample with $y = 1$ and examine its loss as a function of $z$:

- **MSE**: $\left(\sigma(z) - 1\right)^2$. As $z \to -\infty$, it plateaus near 1; as $z \to +\infty$, it plateaus near 0. A function that transitions from flat, down a slope, and back to flat is S-shaped, **not a convex bowl**. When summing over multiple samples, the loss surface contains extensive plateaus where gradient descent crawls extremely slowly.
- **Log loss**: $-\ln\sigma(z)$. As $z \to -\infty$, it increases asymptotically along the straight line $-z$, without plateauing. The worse the error, the steeper the slope.

Numerically: at $z = -6$ ($\hat{y} = 0.0025$, a confident error), the derivative with respect to $z$ under MSE is merely $-0.0049$, whereas under log loss it is $-0.9975$—roughly 200 times stronger. The following derivation shows why.

### Gradient of Log Loss

Gradient descent requires the partial derivatives of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to each $w_j$ and $b$. Consider a single sample, omitting the $(i)$ superscript: $\ell = -\left[y\ln\hat{y} + (1 - y)\ln(1 - \hat{y})\right]$, with $\hat{y} = \sigma(z)$ and $z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$. By the **chain rule**:

$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial w_j} = \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\hat{y}} \cdot \frac{d\hat{y}}{dz} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial w_j}
$$

**Step 1: Derivative of sigmoid.** Writing $\sigma(z) = \left(1 + e^{-z}\right)^{-1}$, the derivative of $u^{-1}$ is $-u^{-2}$, multiplied by the derivative of $1 + e^{-z}$, which is $-e^{-z}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma'(z) &= \frac{e^{-z}}{\left(1 + e^{-z}\right)^2} \\
&= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \cdot \frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} \\
&= \sigma(z)\left(1 - \sigma(z)\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

The second step factors the fraction. The third step uses $\frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = \frac{(1 + e^{-z}) - 1}{1 + e^{-z}} = 1 - \sigma(z)$. Thus $\frac{d\hat{y}}{dz} = \hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})$. This derivative peaks at 0.25 at $z = 0$ and vanishes at both extremes: this explains why MSE paired with sigmoid saturates and stalls.

**Step 2: Derivative of $\ell$ with respect to $\hat{y}$.** Differentiating $\ln u$ gives $1/u$; differentiating $\ln(1 - \hat{y})$ with respect to $\hat{y}$ gives $-\frac{1}{1 - \hat{y}}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\hat{y}} &= -\frac{y}{\hat{y}} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - \hat{y}} \\
&= \frac{-y(1 - \hat{y}) + (1 - y)\hat{y}}{\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})} \\
&= \frac{\hat{y} - y}{\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})}
\end{aligned}
$$

The second step finds a common denominator. The third expands the numerator: $-y + y\hat{y} + \hat{y} - y\hat{y} = \hat{y} - y$.

**Step 3: Putting it together.** Since $z = w_1x_1 + \dots + w_nx_n + b$, we have $\frac{\partial z}{\partial w_j} = x_j$ and $\frac{\partial z}{\partial b} = 1$. Multiplying all three terms:

$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial w_j} = \frac{\hat{y} - y}{\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})} \cdot \hat{y}(1 - \hat{y}) \cdot x_j = (\hat{y} - y)\,x_j
$$

The factor $\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})$ in the numerator and denominator **cancels out completely**. This is why log loss does not saturate when making severe mistakes: the derivative with respect to $z$ simplifies cleanly to $\hat{y} - y$.

**Step 4: Averaging over $m$ samples.** Because $\mathcal{L}$ is the mean of individual sample losses $\ell^{(i)}$, its derivative is the mean of individual gradients:

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_j} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\hat{y}^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)x^{(i)}_j, \qquad
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial b} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\hat{y}^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)
$$

In compact matrix notation with feature matrix $\mathbf{X}$ ($m \times n$) and vector $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ of predicted probabilities: $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{m}\mathbf{X}^\top\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}\right)$.

:::note[Key Takeaway]
The gradient of log loss shares the **exact same form** as the MSE gradient in linear regression: the average of (prediction minus true label) multiplied by feature values. In linear regression, a factor of 2 arises from squaring, and $\hat{y}$ is $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$ rather than $\sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b)$.
:::

### Training with Gradient Descent

Setting the gradient to zero yields transcendental equations involving $\sigma$, which lack closed-form analytical solutions like the normal equation. We turn to [gradient descent](/en/ml/gradient-descent/):

$$
w_j \leftarrow w_j - \eta\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\hat{y}^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)x^{(i)}_j, \qquad
b \leftarrow b - \eta\,\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\hat{y}^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)
$$

The reassuring fact: **log loss is convex** in $(\mathbf{w}, b)$. As a function of $z$, a sample loss has derivative $\sigma(z) - y$ and second derivative $\sigma(z)\left(1 - \sigma(z)\right) > 0$, curving upward like a bowl. Since $z$ is linear in the parameters, the sum across all samples remains strictly convex. There are no local minima to get trapped in. By default, scikit-learn uses L-BFGS rather than standard gradient descent, leveraging curvature approximations to converge in fewer iterations to the same global optimum.

### Regularization in scikit-learn

Scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression` includes **L2 regularization by default**, similar to Ridge regression in [Ridge, Lasso, and Elastic Net](/en/ml/regularization/). In version 1.8, the objective function (for uniformly weighted samples) is formulated as:

$$
J(\mathbf{w}, b) = \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, b) + \frac{1}{2mC}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_2^2
$$

- **`C` is the inverse of regularization strength.** Comparing with $\mathcal{L} + \lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_2^2$ in the Regularization lesson, $\lambda = \frac{1}{2mC}$. Smaller `C` imposes stronger regularization, whereas larger `C` weakens it. The default is `C=1`.
- **$b$ is not regularized** (with the default `lbfgs` solver).
- **To disable regularization**, scikit-learn 1.8 recommends `C=np.inf` (the `penalty` parameter is deprecated). Because version 1.8.0 emits an extraneous warning with `C=np.inf`, our code examples use `C=1e10`: penalty is negligible and does not alter results.
- **Features should be standardized**, for the same reason as Ridge: regularization penalizes all $w_j$ equally.

Regularization also prevents pathology in a critical scenario: when classes are **linearly separable**. In that case, doubling $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ leaves the boundary unchanged while pushing every $\hat{y}$ closer to 0 or 1, further driving log loss down. There is no finite minimum: weights inflate indefinitely. The third code example demonstrates this phenomenon.

### Limitations

- **Decision boundaries are always linear.** If two classes form concentric circles, no straight boundary can separate them. One must engineer features like $x_1^2, x_1x_2$ (akin to polynomial regression), or switch to non-linear algorithms as in [K-Nearest Neighbors](/en/ml/k-nearest-neighbors/) and [Decision Trees and Random Forests](/en/ml/decision-tree-random-forest/).
- **Limited to binary classification natively.** For multi-class classification, softmax replaces sigmoid, as covered in [Softmax Regression](/en/ml/softmax-regression/).
- **Probabilities are trustworthy only if the model is well-specified.** If true log-odds are non-linear with respect to features, estimated probabilities may be miscalibrated even when classification accuracy remains high.
- **Weights are hard to interpret under multicollinearity** (see the third pitfall below).

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Forgetting That `LogisticRegression` Regularizes by Default]
`LogisticRegression()` applies L2 penalty with `C=1` by default, rather than standard unregularized logistic regression from classic statistics textbooks. On our 18 students, it yields $w = 1.002$ instead of $1.263$. When comparing with formulas in textbooks or interpreting coefficients as odds ratios, always verify what value of `C` is in use. Keep in mind the inverted direction: smaller `C` means **stronger** penalty, opposite to `alpha` or $\lambda$ in `Ridge`.
:::

:::caution[Misinterpreting the Class Label Orientation]
A positive coefficient means that increasing the feature increases the odds of **class 1**. Which outcome corresponds to class 1 depends entirely on the dataset: in `load_breast_cancer`, class 1 is benign (`data.target_names` is `['malignant', 'benign']`), meaning negative coefficients are malignant indicators. Reversing the interpretation flips clinical conclusions. `predict_proba(X)[:, 1]` corresponds to the second class in `model.classes_`, not necessarily the more severe class.
:::

:::caution[Treating Coefficients as True Feature Importance]
Comparing coefficient magnitudes is only meaningful when features are standardized, and even then demands caution. The breast cancer dataset contains highly correlated features (radius, perimeter, area), allowing the model to divide weights among them somewhat arbitrarily, just like collinear features in Lasso. The assumption "increase one feature while holding others constant" is often unrealistic when physical features naturally move together.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key Takeaways:**

- Logistic regression computes $z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$ and applies sigmoid: $\hat{y} = \sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z}) = P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})$. Unlike straight lines, predictions stay strictly within $(0, 1)$ and remain robust to extreme, confidently correct points.
- Log-odds are linear: $\ln\frac{\hat{y}}{1 - \hat{y}} = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$. Increasing $x_j$ by one unit scales odds by $e^{w_j}$. The decision boundary at threshold 0.5 is the linear hyperplane $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = 0$.
- **Log loss** is $-\frac{1}{m}$ times the log-likelihood:
  $$
  \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, b) = -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[y^{(i)}\ln\hat{y}^{(i)} + \left(1 - y^{(i)}\right)\ln\left(1 - \hat{y}^{(i)}\right)\right]
  $$
  This loss function is convex, heavily penalizes confident errors, and has gradient $\frac{1}{m}\sum_i\left(\hat{y}^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)x^{(i)}_j$, mirroring linear regression. Since no closed-form solution exists, training relies on gradient descent or L-BFGS.
- `LogisticRegression` applies L2 regularization by default with `C=1`, $\lambda = \frac{1}{2mC}$: smaller `C` means stronger regularization. Regularization keeps coefficients bounded when data is linearly separable.
