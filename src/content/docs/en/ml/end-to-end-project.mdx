## Intuition

A medical clinic provides records for 442 diabetes patients. Each patient has 10 baseline measurements: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), average blood pressure, and six blood serum measurements. One year later, a quantitative measure of disease progression was recorded. The clinic asks: **for a new patient, can we predict disease progression in advance to identify who needs intensive monitoring?**

This is the standard `load_diabetes` dataset available in scikit-learn, published by Efron et al. in 2004. Here are the first five patients:

| Patient | age | sex | bmi | bp | s1 | s2 | s3 | s4 | s5 | s6 | Progression |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 59 | 2 | 32.1 | 101 | 157 | 93.2 | 38 | 4 | 4.8598 | 87 | 151 |
| 1 | 48 | 1 | 21.6 | 87 | 183 | 103.2 | 70 | 3 | 3.8918 | 69 | 75 |
| 2 | 72 | 2 | 30.5 | 93 | 156 | 93.6 | 41 | 4 | 4.6728 | 85 | 141 |
| 3 | 24 | 1 | 25.3 | 84 | 198 | 131.4 | 40 | 5 | 4.8903 | 89 | 206 |
| 4 | 50 | 1 | 23.0 | 101 | 192 | 125.4 | 52 | 4 | 4.2905 | 80 | 135 |

`bp` is mean arterial pressure. `s1` through `s6` represent blood serum measurements: total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, total cholesterol / HDL ratio, log of serum triglycerides, and blood sugar level. `sex` is coded as 1 and 2; the original documentation does not specify which code corresponds to male or female.

### The Rushed Approach

With familiar tools in hand, it is tempting to proceed as follows: standardize the entire table, train ten candidate models, evaluate each on a test set, pick the one with the best score, and report that figure. Each step sounds reasonable in isolation. Yet the reported score will be **overly optimistic**: the test set contributed to computing mean and variance during scaling, and it was actively used to select among ten models. When the clinic deploys the model, real-world error will exceed what was promised.

The root issue is not model architecture. It is the **order of operations** and **which data is accessible at each moment**.

### The Disciplined Approach

Experienced ML engineers follow a structured checklist. This lesson follows an 8-step workflow, closely mirroring chapter 2 of Aurélien Géron's textbook (see Further Reading). The guiding principle is simple: **set aside the test set immediately, make all modeling and engineering decisions using only the training set, and open the test set exactly once at the very end**.

The task for this lesson: **predict disease progression after one year from 10 baseline features, reporting an error estimate that the clinic can trust on future patients.**

## Theory

### Step 1: Frame the Problem and Choose a Metric

Before writing code, answer three fundamental questions.

**Supervised or unsupervised? Regression or classification?** We have ground-truth targets, making it supervised learning. The target is a continuous value ranging from 25 to 346, so this is a **regression** problem. If the clinic only needed to know "severe vs. mild progression", the task would become classification, requiring entirely different metrics.

**How will performance be evaluated?** We use **Mean Absolute Error** (MAE):

$$
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lvert y^{(i)} - \hat{y}^{(i)}\right\rvert
$$

Reason: MAE shares the exact unit of the target. Saying "on average, predictions deviate by 46 points on a scale from 25 to 346" is immediately clear to medical practitioners. MSE penalizes large errors quadratically, making it harder to interpret directly. If a large error is catastrophic compared to several smaller errors, MSE (or RMSE) is preferable. There is no universally "correct" metric: the metric must reflect the actual domain cost of errors.

**What constitutes "good" performance?** An isolated MAE value lacks context. We require a **baseline**: the simplest sensible heuristic that learns nothing complex. For MAE, the optimal constant prediction is the **median** of the training labels. Why the median rather than the mean?

Let $c$ be our constant prediction. Shifting $c$ upward by an infinitesimal amount $\delta$ without crossing any target values reduces error by $\delta$ for every patient whose label is greater than $c$, but adds $\delta$ for every patient whose label is less than $c$. The total change in MAE is:

$$
\frac{\delta}{m}\Big(\#\{y^{(i)} < c\} - \#\{y^{(i)} > c\}\Big)
$$

where $\#\{\cdot\}$ denotes the count of matching patients. As long as more points lie above $c$ than below, increasing $c$ decreases MAE. Equilibrium is reached when the counts on both sides balance, which defines the median. For MSE, setting the derivative with respect to $c$ to zero yields the mean. In scikit-learn, this baseline is `DummyRegressor(strategy="median")`. Any model that fails to outperform this baseline has learned nothing of practical value.

### Step 2: Acquire Data, Split Immediately, Put the Test Set Away

As soon as data is loaded, reserve a **test set** (here 20%, or 89 patients) and do not inspect it. Why split before even exploring the data? Because **human intuition acts like an estimator**. If you examine the full dataset and notice "feature `s5` appears strongly predictive", that hypothesis was derived from the test set. A test set remains an unbiased benchmark only if zero decisions were influenced by its contents.

**Stratified split**. Purely random splitting can, by chance, underrepresent severe cases in the test set. In classification, passing targets to `stratify` preserves class ratios. In regression, target categories do not exist naturally, so we create them: bin the continuous target into 5 equal-frequency quantiles (`pd.qcut`), then stratify across these bins. Consequently, the test set contains 18, 18, 18, 17, and 18 patients across the five progression tiers from mild to severe.

### Step 3: Explore Data on the Training Set

**Exploratory Data Analysis** (EDA) investigates distributions and anomalies before modeling: feature ranges, missing or corrupted values, categorical encodings, and correlations with the target. Crucially, all exploration occurs strictly on the **training set**.

Fundamental tools include `describe()` and **correlation coefficients** between each feature and the target. Pearson correlation ranges in $[-1, 1]$, where values further from 0 indicate stronger **linear** association. Two caveats:

- A near-zero correlation does not imply no relationship; it only indicates the absence of a **linear** relationship. A U-shaped relationship yields near-zero correlation.
- Correlation is not **causation**. This observational dataset contains no clinical interventions. Even though BMI correlates with disease progression, the model cannot infer how much weight loss would slow progression.

EDA also uncovers features requiring dedicated encoding. Here, `sex` takes values 1 and 2: it is a numerically encoded categorical variable, not a continuous measurement.

### Step 4: Prepare Data Using a Pipeline

In this step, data transformations are specified: imputing missing entries, encoding categorical variables, scaling numerical features, and engineering new interactions. Detailed techniques are covered in [Data Preprocessing, Augmentation, and Imbalanced Data](/en/ml/data-preprocessing/). The core requirement is that **every transformation step that learns from data** (such as computing scaling statistics) must be **fitted strictly on training folds**.

The most robust design encapsulates preprocessing and estimation inside a `Pipeline`. For the diabetes data:

- `OneHotEncoder(drop="if_binary")` converts `sex` into a single 0/1 binary feature.
- `StandardScaler()` standardizes the remaining 9 numerical features. Ridge regression requires this step because its regularization penalty treats all feature weights symmetrically.

When cross-validating the pipeline, scikit-learn refits `StandardScaler` inside **each fold** on the training partition only. The validation fold plays no part in computing mean and variance, preventing data leakage.

### Step 5: Shortlist Models via Cross-Validation

Now we evaluate candidate models. Avoid committing early to a single model. Train several diverse architectures with default or sensible hyperparameters, evaluating them via **cross-validation** on the training set:

1. **Baseline** `DummyRegressor`: establishes the minimum performance floor.
2. **Regularized linear model**, specifically Ridge: fast, minimal hyperparameters, resistant to overfitting.
3. **Nonlinear ensemble**, specifically Random Forest: captures feature interactions and nonlinear curves, though requiring larger sample sizes.

Record both the mean **and standard deviation** of metric scores across folds. When two models differ by less than their cross-validation standard deviation, neither can be declared superior with confidence. The output of this stage is a shortlist of two or three promising architectures for further tuning.

### Step 6: Hyperparameter Tuning

Every estimator possesses **hyperparameters** set prior to training: `alpha` in Ridge, or tree depth and ensemble size in Random Forest. **Hyperparameter tuning** searches for optimal configurations via cross-validation, using one of two primary strategies:

- **Grid Search** (`GridSearchCV`): evaluates **all** parameter combinations across a predefined grid. A grid of 6 `alpha` values across 5 folds requires 30 fits. Suitable when tuning one or two parameters.
- **Random Search** (`RandomizedSearchCV`): samples a fixed budget of configurations (`n_iter`) randomly from defined probability distributions. When tuning many hyperparameters where only a few govern performance, random search finds competitive solutions with significantly fewer evaluations (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012).

Within a pipeline, hyperparameters are referenced via `<step_name>__<param_name>` with double underscores. `make_pipeline` generates lowercase step names from class names, making Ridge's regularization parameter `ridge__alpha`. After search completion, `GridSearchCV` automatically **refits** the winning configuration on the entire training set (`refit=True` by default).

A subtle detail: the cross-validation score of the winning hyperparameter configuration is slightly optimistic, as it represents the maximum of multiple noisy estimates. This selection bias reinforces why an untouched test set remains necessary.

### Step 7: Error Analysis, Then Evaluate on the Test Set Once

Before opening the test set, investigate **where** the model errs. **Error analysis** leverages **out-of-fold predictions**: `cross_val_predict` obtains a prediction for every training patient using a model that never saw that specific individual. Errors can then be grouped across cohorts, such as progression severity. For instance, the model might predict mild cases accurately while underestimating severe cases—the exact group the clinic cares about most. Such insights may warrant revisiting Step 1 (redefining the metric), Step 4 (engineering specific features), or Step 5 (trying different model families). Revisiting earlier stages is fully sound here because the test set remains untouched.

Once the pipeline is finalized, evaluate on the test set **exactly once**. This single number serves as the unbiased estimate of generalization error on new patients. Post-evaluation modifications based on test results invalidate the estimate. If test MAE degrades substantially compared to cross-validation, investigate potential data leakage or train/test distribution shift. Re-evaluating requires fresh, unseen data.

### Step 8: Deployment and Monitoring

Models deliver real-world value only when integrated into serving systems. This section outlines key principles; serving models behind HTTP endpoints is detailed in [Deploying Custom Models](/en/llm/deploy-custom-model/), and containerization is covered in [Docker and Cloud](/en/llm/docker-cloud/).

**Serialize the complete pipeline**, not merely the estimator weights. Preprocessing is an integral part of the model: incoming patient records must be scaled using the training set's exact mean and standard deviation. The standard tool is `joblib`:

```python
import joblib

joblib.dump(search.best_estimator_, "diabetes-ridge.joblib")  # save
model = joblib.load("diabetes-ridge.joblib")  # load in serving environment
```

This snippet assumes an already fitted `search` object as shown in the third code example, intended for execution in a local environment. Note two recommendations from the scikit-learn documentation: `joblib` files can execute arbitrary code upon deserialization, so only load trusted artifacts; and models should be loaded in an environment with the **matching scikit-learn version** used during training, alongside records of training data checksums and cross-validation scores.

**Monitoring.** Environments evolve over time. Clinics upgrade diagnostic equipment, measurement scales shift, or demographic distributions drift: the input distribution diverges from training data. This phenomenon is known as **data drift**. Alternatively, the relationship between features and outcomes can change—such as when a new medication slows progression rates—termed **concept drift**. A minimal monitoring workflow checks incoming input statistics against the training baseline and recalculates MAE once true progression outcomes become available. If error degrades, retrain on fresh data following the complete 8-step cycle.

### Limitations

- **The workflow is iterative, not linear.** Step 7 frequently routes work back to Steps 1, 4, or 5. The only irreversible boundary is opening the test set.
- **Small sample sizes exhibit variance across splits.** With only 89 test patients, test MAE fluctuates noticeably depending on the random split seed (explored in the third code cell).
- **Strong predictive correlation does not imply causality** (Step 3).
- **This overview abstracts certain complex production concerns**: raw data collection and labeling pipelines, patient privacy and HIPAA compliance, demographic fairness auditing, and non-technical stakeholder communication.

## Common Pitfalls

:::caution[Preprocessing or Feature Selection Prior to Splitting]
Fitting scalers, imputing missing values, or selecting "top 10 features correlated with $y$" on the **entire** dataset prior to splitting introduces severe data leakage. Information from test samples leaks directly into the model. Selecting features based on global correlation with $y$ is particularly hazardous because it utilizes test labels directly. Leakage can also emerge from domain features: recording a feature created **after** the outcome is known (such as "annual follow-up visits") inflates training metrics but is unavailable during real inference. Remedy: split immediately, wrap all transformers inside a `Pipeline`, and verify whether each feature exists at the exact moment of clinical prediction.
:::

:::caution[Peeking Repeatedly at the Test Set]
Iteratively evaluating test scores and tweaking hyperparameters converts the test set into a validation set. After multiple iterations, the reported test metric becomes just as optimistically biased as the training score. Perform all comparisons and tuning via cross-validation on training data (`cross_val_score`, `GridSearchCV`). Open the test set once for the finalized candidate. If subsequent changes are unavoidable, clearly disclose in reporting that test evaluation integrity has been compromised.
:::

:::caution[Defaulting to Conventional Metrics Uncritically]
Using accuracy for imbalanced medical diagnosis, MSE when stakeholders evaluate absolute point deviations, or MAE when severe outliers pose catastrophic safety risks: an incorrect loss function misaligns optimization objectives. Select evaluation metrics in Step 1 based on real-world error consequences, always benchmarking against a `DummyRegressor` or `DummyClassifier`. For classification metrics, consult [Model Evaluation: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1](/en/ml/classification-metrics/).
:::

## Summary and Further Reading

**Key Takeaways:**

- A disciplined ML project advances through 8 steps: problem framing and metric selection, data splitting, EDA, preprocessing, model shortlisting, hyperparameter tuning, error analysis with single-pass test evaluation, and deployment with monitoring. Each step yields a defined deliverable, and Steps 3 through 7 form an iterative feedback loop.
- Isolate the test set at the onset (stratifying across target quantiles for regression when appropriate). Formulate all modeling decisions using training data, encapsulate transformers within a `Pipeline`, and tune configurations via cross-validation. Evaluate on the test set exactly once.
- Metrics must reflect practical error penalties and be benchmarked against simple heuristics. MAE $= \frac{1}{m}\sum_i\lvert y^{(i)} - \hat{y}^{(i)}\rvert$ uses the training median as its optimal constant baseline. On the diabetes dataset, Ridge (cross-validation MAE $\approx 46$) outperforms both the baseline (65.6) and Random Forest (49.3).
- Persist the entire pipeline rather than standalone model weights, record dependency versions, and monitor for input data drift post-deployment.
