## Intuition

Minh frequently joins friends to play soccer in the afternoon, but not every day. A friend recorded weather conditions and Minh's attendance over 12 recent afternoons:

| Afternoon | Rain | Temperature (°C) | High Wind | Plays Soccer |
|---:|---|---:|---|---|
| 1 | no | 30 | no | yes |
| 2 | no | 35 | no | no |
| 3 | yes | 27 | yes | no |
| 4 | no | 32 | yes | yes |
| 5 | no | 28 | no | yes |
| 6 | yes | 25 | no | no |
| 7 | no | 34 | yes | no |
| 8 | no | 31 | no | yes |
| 9 | yes | 29 | yes | no |
| 10 | no | 36 | no | no |
| 11 | no | 29 | no | yes |
| 12 | yes | 26 | yes | no |

Today, it is not raining, the temperature is 31°C, and it is windy. Will Minh play?

Examining the table reveals a natural hierarchy of questions. **Is it raining?** On all 4 rainy days, Minh stayed home. If not raining, the next question is: **is it hotter than 33°C?** On the 3 hot days (34–36°C), Minh stayed home; on the remaining 5 temperate days, Minh played. Because today is dry and 31°C, we predict **yes**.

This cascade of binary conditions defines a decision tree: each step asks a yes/no question, progressively narrowing candidate possibilities.

### Which Question to Ask First?

You intuitively chose "rain" as the initial test because it cleanly separates the data. Compare it against testing "high wind":

- Splitting on **rain**: the "rain" branch contains 4 afternoons, all staying home (pure). The "no rain" branch has 8 afternoons (5 play, 3 stay home).
- Splitting on **wind**: the "windy" branch has 5 afternoons (1 play, 4 stay home). The "calm" branch has 7 afternoons (4 play, 3 stay home).

The rain query produces an entirely pure subset, whereas the wind query leaves two mixed subsets. An optimal query isolates subsets that are as **pure** as possible—concentrated on a single class. With 3 features and 12 rows, inspection by eye suffices. With 30 features and thousands of rows, automated quantification is essential.

The objective of this lesson is: **quantify the impurity of a sample subset with a numerical score, enabling algorithms to select optimal split questions automatically, and determine when to stop growing the tree.**

## Theory

### Anatomy of a Tree

A decision tree consists of **nodes**:

- **Root node**: The initial question where all training samples enter.
- **Internal node**: A test of the form "$x_j \le t$?", where $x_j$ is the $j$-th feature and $t$ is a numerical **threshold**. An affirmative answer routes the sample to the left child; negative routes to the right.
- **Leaf node**: A terminal node asking no further questions. In classification, a leaf predicts the **majority class** among training instances reaching it.

The length of the longest path from root to any leaf defines the **depth** of the tree. Minh's decision tree has depth 2.

Predicting for a new observation is straightforward: start at the root, evaluate each test, and traverse branches until reaching a leaf. Categorical features are encoded numerically as 0/1; "Is it raining?" translates to "rain $\le 0.5$?", where no rain (0) routes left and rain (1) routes right.

### Measuring Impurity: Gini and Entropy

Consider a node with $m$ samples belonging to $K$ classes. Let $p_k$ denote the fraction of samples belonging to class $k$ within the node ($K = 2$ here: "yes" and "no").

**Gini impurity** is defined as:

$$
G = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k^2
$$

Intuition: randomly draw a sample from the node, and predict its label by sampling from the class distribution $p_k$. $G$ is the probability of misclassification. A pure node ($p_k = 1$) has $G = 0$. A perfectly balanced 50/50 binary node has $G = 1 - 0.5^2 - 0.5^2 = 0.5$, representing maximum binary impurity.

**Entropy** measures disorder from an information-theoretic angle:

$$
H = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log_2 p_k
$$

with convention $0 \cdot \log_2 0 = 0$. A pure node yields $H = 0$; a 50/50 node yields $H = 1$ bit (requiring exactly one binary question to resolve class identity).

A candidate split divides a node into a left child with $m_L$ samples and a right child with $m_R$ samples ($m_L + m_R = m$). The post-split impurity is the **weighted average** across both children:

$$
G_{\text{split}} = \frac{m_L}{m}G_L + \frac{m_R}{m}G_R
$$

Weighting is critical: a pure branch containing only 1 sample does not offset a large 100-sample branch that remains completely mixed. The optimal question minimizes $G_{\text{split}}$, maximizing **Gini reduction** $G - G_{\text{split}}$. Under entropy, this reduction is termed **information gain** ($\mathrm{IG}$):

$$
\mathrm{IG} = H - \left(\frac{m_L}{m}H_L + \frac{m_R}{m}H_R\right)
$$

In practice, Gini and entropy select nearly identical splits. Scikit-learn uses Gini by default (`criterion="gini"`), which is slightly faster because it avoids computing logarithms.

### Manual Calculation on the 12 Afternoons

**Step 1: Impurity at the root.** There are 5 "yes" and 7 "no" afternoons, giving $p_{\text{yes}} = 5/12$ and $p_{\text{no}} = 7/12$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G &= 1 - \left(\tfrac{5}{12}\right)^2 - \left(\tfrac{7}{12}\right)^2 \\
&= 1 - \tfrac{25}{144} - \tfrac{49}{144} \\
&= \tfrac{70}{144} \approx 0.4861
\end{aligned}
$$

The root impurity sits near 0.5, reflecting high uncertainty.

**Step 2: Evaluating the "rain" split.** The "no rain" branch has 8 days (5 yes, 3 no), and the "rain" branch has 4 days (0 yes, 4 no):

$$
\begin{aligned}
G_L &= 1 - \left(\tfrac{5}{8}\right)^2 - \left(\tfrac{3}{8}\right)^2 = \tfrac{30}{64} \approx 0.4688 \\
G_R &= 1 - 0^2 - 1^2 = 0 \\
G_{\text{split}} &= \tfrac{8}{12} \cdot 0.4688 + \tfrac{4}{12} \cdot 0 = 0.3125
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3: Comparing candidates.** Testing "windy" yields branches with 7 days (4 yes, 3 no) and 5 days (1 yes, 4 no). Testing "temperature $\le 33$" yields 9 days (5 yes, 4 no) and 3 days (0 yes, 3 no). The table summarizes candidate splits:

| Candidate Question | Left Branch ("yes") | Right Branch ("no") | $G_{\text{split}}$ | Gini Reduction | Information Gain |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| No rain? | 5 yes, 3 no | 0 yes, 4 no | 0.3125 | 0.1736 | 0.3436 |
| Calm wind? | 4 yes, 3 no | 1 yes, 4 no | 0.4190 | 0.0671 | 0.1043 |
| Temperature $\le 33$? | 5 yes, 4 no | 0 yes, 3 no | 0.3704 | 0.1157 | 0.2366 |

The "no rain" question wins under both metrics, confirming our initial intuition. It forms the root node.

**Step 4: Recursing on child nodes.** The "rain" branch is pure (4 no) and becomes a leaf predicting "no". The "no rain" branch retains 8 days with $G = 0.4688$. Evaluating all candidate questions on **these 8 days alone**, the condition "temperature $\le 33$?" splits them into 5 "yes" and 3 "no" days, achieving $G_L = 0, G_R = 0$, and $G_{\text{split}} = 0$. Both child leaves are now pure, terminating growth.

### CART: A Greedy Algorithm

The process above describes the **CART** algorithm (Classification And Regression Trees; Breiman et al., 1984), as implemented by scikit-learn:

1. For each feature $x_j$, sort unique values in the current node in ascending order. Every candidate threshold $t$ is the **midpoint** between adjacent sorted values. Temperature has 11 distinct values, generating 10 candidate thresholds (33 is the midpoint of 32 and 34).
2. Compute $G_{\text{split}}$ across all (feature, threshold) pairs and pick the pair achieving minimum impurity.
3. Split the node and recursively repeat step 1 on each child.
4. Stop when a node is pure or a stopping criterion (e.g., maximum depth) is reached.

CART is a **greedy algorithm**: it selects the best immediate split at each step without anticipating downstream tree configurations. Finding the globally optimal tree is NP-complete; greedy partitioning provides a computationally efficient and practically effective approximation.

### Regression Trees

Trees adapt naturally to continuous targets as **regression trees**:

- Leaves predict the **mean** $\bar{y}$ of training targets reaching that leaf, rather than a majority class.
- Node impurity is measured using mean squared error around that mean: $\frac{1}{m}\sum_i (y^{(i)} - \bar{y})^2$, choosing splits that minimize weighted child MSE.

The resulting regression function is a **piecewise constant step function**, outputting a constant prediction per leaf.

### Deep Trees and Overfitting

If left unconstrained, CART continues splitting until every leaf is pure, effectively creating custom leaves for outlier noise: training accuracy hits 1.000 while test performance degrades. Tree **depth** controls model capacity, analogous to polynomial degree in [Overfitting and Underfitting](/en/ml/overfitting-underfitting/).

Three common regularization strategies include:

- **`max_depth`**: Caps the maximum path length from root to leaf. A depth of $d$ yields at most $2^d$ leaves.
- **`min_samples_leaf`**: Requires every terminal leaf to contain at least a specified number of samples, preventing single-sample outlier splits.
- **Cost-complexity pruning**: Allows the tree to grow deep, then prunes branches contributing negligible impurity gain via hyperparameter `ccp_alpha`, penalizing leaf count in the spirit of [Regularization](/en/ml/regularization/).

These hyperparameters must be selected using cross-validation rather than the test set.

### Pros and Cons of a Single Decision Tree

| Property | Decision Trees |
|---|---|
| Interpretability | Highly transparent: directly inspectable as explicit "if-then" rules |
| Feature Scaling | Unnecessary: splits depend strictly on relative ordering, remaining invariant to monotonic transformations |
| Decision Boundaries | **Axis-aligned orthogonal steps**, because each split evaluates a single feature. Diagonal boundaries must be approximated via fine stairs |
| Variance | High: minor alterations in training data can flip the root split, fundamentally altering the entire downstream tree |
| Extrapolation | None: outside training feature bounds, regression trees output the flat constant of the nearest leaf |

High variance is the primary limitation of single trees. Random forests were invented specifically to address this weakness.

### Random Forests: Many Trees, Each Slightly Different

Rather than relying on one tree, plant an **ensemble** of randomized trees and aggregate their predictions. Random forests (Breiman, 2001) inject two distinct sources of randomness:

**1. Bootstrap sampling.** Each tree is trained on a **bootstrap sample**: drawing $m$ instances from the training set with replacement. Some instances appear multiple times; others are omitted. Combining predictions across bootstrap-trained models is called **bagging** (bootstrap aggregating):

- Classification: Trees cast votes. Scikit-learn averages predicted class probabilities across trees and takes the argmax.
- Regression: Predictions are averaged directly.

**2. Random feature selection.** At **each split**, the tree only evaluates a random subset of features, typically $\sqrt{n}$ out of $n$ available features (`max_features="sqrt"`, default for `RandomForestClassifier`). With 30 features, each split evaluates only 5. This prevents a single dominant feature from dictating the root across all trees, which would cause ensemble predictions to be overly correlated.

### Why Averaging Multiple Trees Reduces Variance

Treat individual tree predictions at an input point as random variables with variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$. The variance of the ensemble mean across $B$ trees evaluates to:

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} f_b\right) = \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2
$$

where $f_b$ is the prediction of tree $b$.

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

The variance of a sum equals the sum of individual variances plus pairwise covariances across all $B(B-1)$ ordered pairs:

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{b=1}^{B} f_b\right) = B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2
$$

Dividing the sum by $B$ scales variance by $1/B^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} f_b\right) &= \frac{\sigma^2}{B} + \frac{(B-1)\rho\sigma^2}{B} \\
&= \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2
\end{aligned}
$$

</details>

Inspecting both terms:

- The second term $\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$ vanishes as tree count $B \to \infty$. Adding trees never causes overfitting; beyond a certain size, returns simply plateau.
- The first term $\rho\sigma^2$ remains irreducible regardless of tree count $B$. Minimizing it requires decorrelating trees (lowering $\rho$) through bagging and random feature selection.

Individual trees are grown deep (low bias, high variance). Ensemble averaging handles the variance reduction.

### OOB Score: A Free Validation Set

A specific sample is not drawn on an individual bootstrap trial with probability $1 - \frac{1}{m}$. Over $m$ independent draws, the probability of never being selected is:

$$
\left(1 - \frac{1}{m}\right)^m \approx e^{-1} \approx 0.368
$$

for large $m$. Roughly 37% of training data is left out for each tree. These omitted points represent the **out-of-bag** (OOB) set.

The OOB score evaluates each training sample using only the sub-forest of trees that never saw it, computing an unbiased estimate of generalization error without requiring a separate validation split (`oob_score=True`).

### Feature Importance

The `feature_importances_` attribute measures **Mean Decrease in Impurity** (MDI). For each feature, impurity reductions are summed across all nodes split on that feature, weighted by the fraction of samples traversing each node, and normalized to sum to 1. In a forest, MDI is averaged across all trees.

In Minh's tree: the root rain split contributes $\frac{12}{12}(0.4861 - 0.3125) = 0.1736$, while the temperature split on 8 days contributes $\frac{8}{12}(0.4688 - 0) = 0.3125$. Temperature is assigned higher importance ($0.643$ vs. $0.357$) despite appearing below rain.

MDI carries two notable biases: it is computed on **training data** (attributing score to noise splits in deep unregularized trees) and **favors high-cardinality numerical features** offering numerous candidate thresholds. Permutation importance on a test split provides a more reliable alternative.

:::note[Key Takeaway]
Trees greedily select splits minimizing weighted impurity $\frac{m_L}{m}G_L + \frac{m_R}{m}G_R$. Unconstrained trees overfit and exhibit high variance. Random forests average predictions over decorrelated bootstrap trees using randomized feature subsets, driving ensemble variance down to $\rho\sigma^2$.
:::

### Limitations

- **Interpretability loss.** While a single tree reads as transparent logic, aggregating hundreds of trees yields an opaque black box.
- **Axis-aligned boundaries.** Averaging softens staircase boundaries into fine steps, but regression forests still cannot extrapolate beyond observed feature domains.
- **Gradient Boosting.** Alternative tree ensembles like **gradient boosting** (XGBoost, LightGBM) train trees sequentially to correct predecessor residuals, frequently outperforming random forests on tabular data.

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Taking MDI Feature Importance as Ground Truth]
Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI) is calculated on training data and inflates high-cardinality features. On correlated features, an unconstrained single tree places `worst perimeter` at the root with 0.725 importance, leaving `worst radius` with only 0.037 simply because perimeter was tested first. A random forest distributes importance more evenly (0.144 vs. 0.136). For unbiased importance, use `permutation_importance` on the test set.
:::

:::caution[Evaluating Trees or Forests on Training Accuracy]
Unconstrained decision trees and random forests both trivially reach 1.000 training accuracy on breast cancer data because trees grow until all leaves are pure. This number indicates memorization rather than generalization. Always evaluate models via cross-validation, test sets, or OOB scores.
:::

:::caution[Fearing That More Trees Cause Overfitting in Random Forests]
Unlike polynomial degree or tree depth, ensemble size `n_estimators` does not control model capacity. Increasing tree count solely drives the Monte Carlo variance term $\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$ toward zero, monotonically stabilizing predictions. If a forest overfits, regularize individual constituent trees (`max_depth`, `min_samples_leaf`) or reduce `max_features`—do not decrease tree count.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key Takeaways:**

- Decision trees route samples along binary tests $x_j \le t$ from root to leaf, predicting majority class (classification) or mean target (regression). They require no feature scaling and are easily interpreted.
- CART greedily selects splits minimizing weighted impurity $\frac{m_L}{m}G_L + \frac{m_R}{m}G_R$, using Gini impurity $G = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2$ or entropy for information gain.
- Unconstrained trees overfit and have high variance. Regularize growth with `max_depth`, `min_samples_leaf`, or cost-complexity pruning (`ccp_alpha`) tuned via cross-validation.
- Random forests average decorrelated bootstrap-trained trees with randomized feature subsets, reducing variance to the $\rho\sigma^2$ baseline. OOB scores provide validation metrics without data holdouts.
