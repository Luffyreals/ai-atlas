## Intuition

### Real-World Data Is Messy

Suppose you want to predict apartment rental prices. Unlike the tidy synthetic table in [Linear Regression](/en/ml/linear-regression/), real survey data from 8 applicants appears as follows:

| Apartment | Area (m²) | Floor | District | Balcony |
|---:|---:|---:|---|---|
| 1 | 30 | 5 | Cau Giay | yes |
| 2 | 45 | *missing* | Ba Dinh | no |
| 3 | *missing* | 12 | Hoang Mai | yes |
| 4 | 60 | 3 | Cau Giay | *missing* |
| 5 | 52 | 8 | Hoang Mai | no |
| 6 | 38 | 2 | Ba Dinh | yes |
| 7 | 75 | 15 | *missing* | yes |
| 8 | 41 | 6 | Cau Giay | no |

This table exhibits three critical obstacles for machine learning models:

- **Missing values.** A linear model computes $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$. A missing entry in $\mathbf{x}$ makes the dot product undefined, causing algorithms to crash.
- **Text columns.** Words like "Cau Giay" cannot be multiplied directly by numerical weights. We must encode text into numbers without inventing artificial mathematical relationships.
- **Disparate scales.** Area spans tens of square meters, floors span single digits, and binary indicators span 0 and 1. As shown in [Gradient Descent](/en/ml/gradient-descent/), unscaled features severely hinder optimization.

### Rare Classes and Limited Data

Consider another problem: fraud detection. Out of every 100 transactions, only 5 are fraudulent. A trivial baseline predicting "legitimate" every single time achieves 95% accuracy. Yet as shown in [Model Evaluation: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1](/en/ml/classification-metrics/), this model is completely useless because it catches zero fraudulent transactions.

Conversely, when training data is scarce (e.g., a few hundred medical images), data augmentation creates valid synthetic variations: horizontally flipping a cat photo still yields a cat photo.

The core challenge of this lesson is: **transform raw, messy tabular data into model-ready features without leaking information from test sets into the learning pipeline.**

## Theory

### Missing Values

**Dropping data.** The quickest reaction is dropping rows with missing values (`df.dropna()`). In our 8-row table, missing entries span four separate rows; dropping them discards half the dataset. Dropping an entire column is only defensible when it is overwhelmingly empty. Moreover, dropping rows introduces selection bias: if budget tenants frequently omit area details, the remaining data no longer represents the target population.

**Imputation.** Replace missing entries with aggregate statistics:

- **Mean** or **median** for numerical features. The 7 observed apartment areas have a mean of $341 / 7 \approx 48.71$ and median of $45$. The median is robust to outliers: replacing the 75 m² apartment with a 500 m² penthouse skews the mean to ~109, whereas the median remains 45.
- **Mode** (most frequent value) for categorical features. "Cau Giay" appears 3 times, making it the mode for Apartment 7.

**Missingness indicators.** The fact that an entry is missing can itself convey predictive signal: a landlord might intentionally withhold area if an apartment is unusually cramped. Practitioners frequently append a binary indicator column flagging whether an entry was imputed (`MissingIndicator`).

### Categorical Variables

The naive approach is integer assignment: Ba Dinh = 0, Cau Giay = 1, Hoang Mai = 2 (**ordinal encoding**). Under a linear model, the district's contribution to price evaluates to $w \cdot \text{code}$:

$$
\text{Ba Dinh: } 0, \qquad \text{Cau Giay: } w, \qquad \text{Hoang Mai: } 2w
$$

This forces the model to assume the three districts lie along an equally spaced spectrum, with Cau Giay mathematically situated halfway between Ba Dinh and Hoang Mai. Changing the arbitrary numbering order alters model predictions.

**One-hot encoding** resolves this by replacing one categorical column with $K$ binary indicator columns:

| Apartment | District | District Ba Dinh | District Cau Giay | District Hoang Mai |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | Cau Giay | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 2 | Ba Dinh | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| 3 | Hoang Mai | 0 | 0 | 1 |

Each district receives its own independent weight $w_{\text{BD}}$, $w_{\text{CG}}$, and $w_{\text{HM}}$. The trade-off is dimensional expansion: a categorical feature with 500 unique levels expands into 500 columns.

Ordinal encoding is only appropriate when categories possess a **natural ranking** (e.g., furnishing levels: "none" < "basic" < "full"). Tree-based models ([Decision Trees and Random Forests](/en/ml/decision-tree-random-forest/)) tolerate ordinal encodings better because they evaluate threshold splits.

In production, test sets may encounter unseen categories. Configuring `OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore")` sets all binary columns to 0 for unknown levels rather than throwing an exception.

### Feature Scaling

Two standard scaling transformations for feature $j$:

$$
\text{z-score: } z_j = \frac{x_j - \mu_j}{\sigma_j}
\qquad\qquad
\text{min-max: } \tilde{x}_j = \frac{x_j - \min_j}{\max_j - \min_j}
$$

**Standardization** (`StandardScaler`) subtracts mean $\mu_j$ and divides by standard deviation $\sigma_j$, yielding zero mean and unit variance. **Min-max scaling** (`MinMaxScaler`) bounds values to $[0, 1]$. Min-max is sensitive to outliers: an extreme 500 m² value compresses all typical apartments into a narrow cluster near 0.

Which models **require** feature scaling?

- **KNN** ([K-Nearest Neighbors](/en/ml/k-nearest-neighbors/)): Euclidean distance sums squared feature differences, allowing unscaled large features to completely dictate neighborhood proximity.
- **Gradient descent**: Disparate feature scales create elongated contour valleys, causing slow convergence or divergence.
- **Regularized models** ([Ridge, Lasso, Elastic Net](/en/ml/regularization/)): Penalty terms treat all $w_j$ equally, requiring weights to be invariant to measurement units.

Models that **do not require** scaling: decision trees and tree ensembles, which split features independently based on ordinal rank.

**Outliers.** Differentiate between data entry errors (4,500 m² due to an extra zero) and valid extreme instances (a luxury penthouse). Errors should be corrected or removed; valid extremes should be retained using robust scalers (`RobustScaler`, scaling via median and interquartile range).

### Data Leakage During Preprocessing

Every preprocessing step **learns parameters** from data: medians for imputation, category dictionaries for one-hot encodings, and $(\mu_j, \sigma_j)$ for standardization. In accordance with [Introduction to Machine Learning](/en/ml/ml-overview/), these parameters must be learned **strictly from the training split**:

- On training data: `fit` (learn parameters) followed by `transform`, combined via `fit_transform`.
- On test data and new queries: **strictly** `transform` using previously learned training parameters.

Fitting scalers across the full dataset prior to splitting exposes $\mu_j$ to test distributions. While scaling leakage is subtle, leakage during **feature selection** (ranking features by target correlation) is disastrous: the second code example demonstrates a model achieving 85% accuracy on pure random noise.

In cross-validation, every fold has its own training split. Scikit-learn's **`Pipeline`** packages transformers and estimators into a unified object, ensuring preprocessing parameters are fitted strictly on each fold's training subset. **`ColumnTransformer`** applies tailored pipelines to distinct column types (imputing and scaling numericals, while imputing and one-hot encoding categoricals) before concatenating them.

:::note[Key Takeaway]
Any transformation learning from data must be fitted strictly on the training set. Encapsulate preprocessing and models within a `Pipeline` to ensure correct fold isolation during cross-validation.
:::

### Data Augmentation

**Data augmentation** creates synthetic training instances by applying transformations that **preserve the ground-truth label**. This teaches the model invariance to irrelevant variations:

- **Images**: Horizontal flips, small rotations, random crops, brightness adjustments. Transformations must respect domain semantics: flipping a cat image preserves cat identity, but flipping a digit 6 turns it into a 9.
- **Tabular data**: Adding subtle Gaussian noise $x_j + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \ll \sigma_j$. Tabular augmentation is less common because label-preserving invariant transformations are harder to define.
- **Text**: Synonym replacement or back-translation through intermediate languages.

Augment **only the training set**, strictly after splitting. Placing augmented copies of a training sample into the test set causes identical-record evaluation leakage.

### Imbalanced Data

**Evaluation.** Because majority classes distort accuracy, evaluate performance via precision, recall, and F1 on the minority class ([Model Evaluation](/en/ml/classification-metrics/)). When partitioning data, specify `stratify=y` in `train_test_split` to maintain identical class ratios across splits.

Three principal remedies exist for severe class imbalance:

**1. Class weighting.** Scale individual sample losses inversely by class prevalence. Let $\ell^{(i)}$ be sample loss and $s_c$ be class weight:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} s_{y^{(i)}}\,\ell^{(i)},
\qquad
s_c = \frac{m}{K\,m_c}
$$

where $K$ is class count and $m_c$ is sample count of class $c$. This corresponds to `class_weight="balanced"`. Multiplying $s_c$ by $m_c$ yields $m / K$ for all classes, equalizing cumulative loss contributions. In our third code example ($m = 1400, m_1 = 74, m_0 = 1326$):

$$
s_1 = \frac{1400}{2 \cdot 74} \approx 9.46,
\qquad
s_0 = \frac{1400}{2 \cdot 1326} \approx 0.53
$$

Each minority transaction is weighted ~18 times more heavily than a majority transaction ($1326 / 74$).

**2. Training resampling.**

- **Random oversampling**: Duplicating minority instances with replacement until balanced. Replicating a sample $k$ times scales its loss contribution by $k$, making random oversampling mathematically similar to class weighting while consuming more memory.
- **Undersampling**: Discarding majority instances. Computationally efficient on massive datasets, but discards genuine information.
- **SMOTE** (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique; Chawla et al., 2002): Generates synthetic minority instances by interpolating between a minority sample $\mathbf{x}^{(i)}$ and its minority neighbor $\mathbf{x}^{(z)}$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{x}^{(i)} + u\left(\mathbf{x}^{(z)} - \mathbf{x}^{(i)}\right), \qquad u \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 1]
$$

**3. Threshold adjustment.** Retain standard model training and lower decision threshold $t$: predicting the minority class whenever $p \ge t$ with $t < 0.5$. This boosts recall while decreasing precision, tuned on the validation set.

All resampling and augmentation techniques must be applied **strictly to training data**. Test sets must preserve original class distributions.

### Limitations

- **No universal recipe.** Median vs. mean imputation, outlier removal vs. retention: optimal strategies depend on data properties and model families. Compare pipelines using cross-validation.
- **Imbalance techniques cannot manufacture new information.** Class weighting and resampling primarily rebalance the precision–recall trade-off; substantial improvements demand acquiring more minority samples or higher-fidelity features.
- **Augmentation requires domain expertise.** Unchecked transformations risk corrupting ground-truth labels.

The reference guide below summarizes standard preprocessing steps, their learned training statistics, and associated scikit-learn classes:

| Pipeline Step | Learned Training Statistics | Scikit-learn Class | Relevant Models |
|---|---|---|---|
| Numerical imputation | Mean or median | `SimpleImputer(strategy="median")` | All models lacking native NaN support |
| Missingness indicator | Identified missing columns | `MissingIndicator` | When missingness carries predictive signal |
| Categorical imputation | Most frequent level (mode) | `SimpleImputer(strategy="most_frequent")` | All models |

| Class weighting | Class counts $m_c$ | `class_weight="balanced"` | Imbalanced classification |
| Resampling / Augmentation | Target sample subsets | Custom scripts / `imblearn` | Imbalanced datasets, small sample regimes |

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Fitting Preprocessors Across the Full Dataset]
Calling `StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)` before `train_test_split` leaks test distribution statistics into training parameters. Calling `fit_transform` on the test split is even worse, scaling test data by different parameters than the training set. Always split data first, then bind transformers and estimators within a `Pipeline`.
:::

:::caution[Resampling or Augmenting Before Splitting]
Replicating minority samples before splitting places identical copies across both training and test sets, artificially inflating test metrics. Similarly, applying SMOTE globally interpolates synthetic training instances from test coordinates. Always split data first with `stratify=y`, applying resampling strictly to the training fold.
:::

:::caution[Imposing Ordinal Encodings on Nominal Features]
Assigning arbitrary integers (e.g., 0, 1, 2) to nominal categories forces linear models to assume artificial metric spacing. Use `OneHotEncoder` for nominal categories, reserving `OrdinalEncoder` strictly for inherently ranked variables.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key Takeaways:**

- Missing values: avoid row deletion; impute with median (numerical) or mode (categorical), appending missingness indicators when omissions carry signal. Encode nominal categories via one-hot encoding.
- Scale features via z-score $z_j = (x_j - \mu_j)/\sigma_j$ or min-max for KNN, gradient descent, and regularized models; decision trees require no scaling.
- All transformations learning from data must fit strictly on training splits. `Pipeline` and `ColumnTransformer` enforce fold isolation during cross-validation.
- Imbalanced datasets: evaluate via precision, recall, and F1; split with `stratify=y`. Address imbalances via class weighting $s_c = m / (K m_c)$, resampling, or threshold adjustment on the training set.
