## Intuition

Imagine rolling a heavy ball down a steep, narrow ravine whose floor gently slopes toward one end. The ball does not roll straight down the valley floor. It oscillates back and forth against the steep valley walls several times before gradually progressing along the bottom. Unlike gradient descent, however, a physical ball possesses **momentum**: the longer it rolls in a consistent direction, the faster it travels.

Standard gradient descent lacks momentum entirely. At each step, it only evaluates the instantaneous local slope under its feet. Consider a synthetic toy ravine to observe this limitation:

$$
\mathcal{L}(w_1, w_2) = \frac{1}{2}\left(w_1^2 + 100\,w_2^2\right)
$$

The minimum sits at $(0, 0)$. Curvature is 1 along $w_1$ and 100 along $w_2$, yielding condition number $\kappa = 100$: the valley is 10 times longer than it is wide ($\sqrt{\kappa} = 10$). We initialize optimization at $(-8, 1.5)$.

### Three Weaknesses of Standard Gradient Descent

**1. Narrow ravines.** The steep slope along $w_2$ forces a tight step bound $\eta < 2/100 = 0.02$. Setting $\eta = 0.018$ near the stability boundary, distance to the valley floor along $w_2$ scales by $1 - 0.018 \times 100 = -0.8$ each step: $w_2$ oscillates wildly back and forth across the ravine floor (zigzagging). Along $w_1$, distance scales by $1 - 0.018 \times 1 = 0.982$: each iteration advances by only 1.8%. Standard GD requires 369 iterations to enter a 0.01 radius around the origin, spending nearly all its steps crawling along the valley floor.

**2. Mini-batch noise.** In stochastic mini-batch settings, gradient estimates computed on small subsets fluctuate, causing search paths to jitter randomly. With fixed $\eta$, SGD wanders perpetually around the minimum rather than settling at the bottom.

**3. A single global learning rate for all parameters.** At the starting point, the gradient vector is $[-8, 150]^\top$: the gradient along $w_2$ is almost 19 times larger than along $w_1$. Under a single shared $\eta$, parameters with steep gradients take massive strides while flat parameters crawl, despite the flat parameter needing to cover far greater distance (from $-8$ to 0). In deep networks with millions of parameters, gradient magnitudes span several orders of magnitude.

### Two Remedial Ideas

1. **Retain directional history.** Accumulate historical gradients into a persistent "velocity". Along directions where gradients point consistently, velocity accumulates (accelerating down the ravine). Along directions where gradients oscillate signs, opposing components cancel out (damping transversal oscillation). This is **Momentum**.
2. **Individualize learning rates.** Scale each parameter's step inversely by its typical historical gradient magnitude. Steep parameters are damped; flat parameters are boosted. This is the core mechanism of **AdaGrad** and **RMSProp**.

**Adam** unifies both concepts. The objective of this lesson is: **engineer gradient update rules that navigate narrow ravines rapidly, dampen stochastic noise, and eliminate manual per-parameter learning rate tuning.**

## Theory

### Unified Notation

All algorithms in this lesson build directly on gradient descent. We index iterations starting from $t = 1$:

$$
\mathbf{g}_t = \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}\right), \qquad t = 1, 2, 3, \dots
$$

where $\mathbf{g}_t$ is the gradient computed at parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}$. Standard GD is expressed as $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t = \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} - \eta\,\mathbf{g}_t$. The optimizers below maintain internal auxiliary vectors of matching dimension, known as the **optimizer state**, initialized to $\mathbf{0}$.

From RMSProp onward, vector squares, square roots, and divisions operate **element-wise**: $\mathbf{g}_t^2$ denotes the vector of squared elements $g_j^2$, rather than a scalar dot product.

### Momentum: Adding Velocity

**Momentum** (Polyak, 1964), also known as the heavy-ball method, maintains a persistent **velocity** vector $\mathbf{v}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{v}_t &= \beta\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + \mathbf{g}_t \\
\boldsymbol{\theta}_t &= \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} - \eta\,\mathbf{v}_t
\end{aligned}
$$

The coefficient $\beta \in [0, 1)$ is the **momentum factor**, typically set to $\beta = 0.9$. The first equation retains fraction $\beta$ of previous velocity and adds the current gradient. The second equation updates parameters along velocity rather than raw gradient. When $\beta = 0$, $\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{g}_t$, recovering standard GD.

**What does velocity remember?** Expanding $\mathbf{v}_{t-1}$ recursively down to $\mathbf{v}_0 = \mathbf{0}$:

$$
\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{g}_t + \beta\,\mathbf{g}_{t-1} + \beta^2\,\mathbf{g}_{t-2} + \dots + \beta^{t-1}\,\mathbf{g}_1
$$

Velocity is an exponentially decayed sum of all past gradients. Consider two illustrative regimes:

- **Consistently aligned gradients** (along the valley floor), $\mathbf{g}_t = \mathbf{g}$: the sum forms a geometric series $\mathbf{g}\left(1 + \beta + \beta^2 + \dots\right)$, converging to $\mathbf{g} / (1 - \beta)$. For $\beta = 0.9$, steps become **10 times longer** than standard GD.
- **Alternating gradients** (across the ravine walls), $\mathbf{g}, -\mathbf{g}, \mathbf{g}, \dots$: the sum evaluates to $\mathbf{g}\left(1 - \beta + \beta^2 - \dots\right)$, converging to $\mathbf{g} / (1 + \beta) \approx 0.53\,\mathbf{g}$. Opposing components cancel out by roughly half.

On our toy ravine with $\eta = 0.018$, Momentum enters the 0.01 error ball in **111 steps** versus 369 for GD. The trade-off is inertial overshoot: the heavy ball surges down the valley, **overshooting the minimum** to $w_1 \approx 2.19$ before reversing.

Alternative literature formulates velocity as an **exponential moving average** (EMA): $\mathbf{v}_t = \beta\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta)\,\mathbf{g}_t$. This scales velocity down by $(1 - \beta)$, meaning both formulations produce identical trajectories if the latter scales $\eta$ up by $1 / (1 - \beta)$. Always verify which convention a codebase uses before adopting learning rates.

**Nesterov accelerated gradient** computes the gradient at the anticipated lookahead position $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} - \eta\beta\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1}$ rather than the current parameters:

$$
\mathbf{v}_t = \beta\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} - \eta\beta\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1}\right)
$$

If momentum is about to overshoot the basin, the lookahead gradient detects the opposing slope early and applies braking force. Scikit-learn's `MLPClassifier` enables this by default (`nesterovs_momentum=True`).

### AdaGrad and RMSProp: Per-Parameter Learning Rates

**AdaGrad** (Duchi, Hazan, Singer, 2011) accumulates squared gradients per parameter, dividing parameter updates by the square root of historical sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{s}_t &= \mathbf{s}_{t-1} + \mathbf{g}_t^2 \\
\boldsymbol{\theta}_t &= \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_t} + \varepsilon}\,\mathbf{g}_t
\end{aligned}
$$

The scalar $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-8}$ prevents division by zero. Parameter $j$ receives effective learning rate $\eta / (\sqrt{s_j} + \varepsilon)$: historical steepness scales steps down, while rarely occurring sparse features receive larger steps. However, accumulator $\mathbf{s}_t$ **strictly increases monotonically**, eventually causing effective learning rates to decay toward zero and freezing training before convergence.

**RMSProp** (Hinton, 2012) resolves AdaGrad's premature freezing by replacing cumulative sums with an exponential moving average, allowing older gradients to be forgotten:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{s}_t &= \rho\,\mathbf{s}_{t-1} + (1 - \rho)\,\mathbf{g}_t^2 \\
\boldsymbol{\theta}_t &= \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_t} + \varepsilon}\,\mathbf{g}_t
\end{aligned}
$$

The decay rate $\rho$ is typically 0.9 or 0.99 (PyTorch default: 0.99). Here $\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_t}$ represents the **root mean square** (RMS) of recent gradients. If gradient magnitudes hover around $\lvert g\rvert$, then $\sqrt{s} \approx \lvert g\rvert$ and parameter step sizes evaluate to $\eta\,g / \lvert g\rvert$: **roughly length $\eta$ regardless of raw gradient magnitude**. At our toy ravine start, the gradient along $w_2$ is 19 times larger than $w_1$, yet RMSProp advances both dimensions by equal step lengths.

A key property: **scaling the loss function by scalar $k$** multiplies gradients by $k$ and $\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_t}$ by $k$, leaving effective step sizes completely invariant. In standard GD, steps scale by $k$, breaking stability.

At step 1, because $\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{s}_1 = (1 - \rho)\,\mathbf{g}_1^2$, giving $\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_1} = \sqrt{1 - \rho}\,\lvert\mathbf{g}_1\rvert$. With $\rho = 0.99$, this evaluates to $0.1\,\lvert\mathbf{g}_1\rvert$, inflating the initial step length to **10 times $\eta$**. Adam addresses this start-up artifact directly.

### Adam: Combining Momentum with Adaptive Learning Rates

**Adam** (Adaptive Moment Estimation; Kingma and Ba, 2015) maintains two concurrent exponential moving averages: first moments (like Momentum) and uncentered second moments (like RMSProp):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{v}_t &= \beta_1\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\,\mathbf{g}_t \\
\mathbf{s}_t &= \beta_2\,\mathbf{s}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\,\mathbf{g}_t^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Vector $\mathbf{v}_t$ estimates the expected gradient (first moment), while $\mathbf{s}_t$ estimates the expected squared gradient (second moment). Because Adam tracks two state vectors, it requires auxiliary memory twice the parameter count. Both moments are corrected for zero-initialization bias:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t &= \frac{\mathbf{v}_t}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{s}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{s}_t}{1 - \beta_2^t} \\
\boldsymbol{\theta}_t &= \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} - \eta\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{s}}_t} + \varepsilon}
\end{aligned}
$$

The exponent $\beta^t$ denotes the power of time step $t$. Default hyperparameters across the original paper, PyTorch, and scikit-learn are: $\eta = 0.001$, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$.

**Why bias correction is essential.** Because moving averages initialize at $\mathbf{0}$, early estimates are severely biased toward zero. At step 1 on our ravine with $\mathbf{g}_1 = [-8, 150]^\top$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{v}_1 &= 0.1\,\mathbf{g}_1 = [-0.8,\ 15]^\top \\
\mathbf{s}_1 &= 0.001\,\mathbf{g}_1^2 = [0.064,\ 22.5]^\top, \qquad \sqrt{\mathbf{s}_1} \approx [0.253,\ 4.743]^\top
\end{aligned}
$$

Uncorrected $\mathbf{v}_1$ is only 10% of true gradient, while $\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_1}$ is shrunk to $\sqrt{0.001} \approx 3.2\%$ of true magnitude. Without correction, their ratio evaluates to $[-3.16, 3.16]^\top$: the denominator is suppressed far more aggressively than the numerator, blowing up the first step to $0.1 / \sqrt{0.001} \approx 3.16\,\eta$. After bias correction, $\hat{\mathbf{v}}_1 = \mathbf{g}_1$ and $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_1 = \mathbf{g}_1^2$, restoring unit ratio $[-1, 1]^\top$ so each parameter steps exactly $\eta$.

**Why dividing by $1 - \beta^t$ corrects bias.** Assuming gradient remains constant $\mathbf{g}_t = \mathbf{g}$, unrolling the recursion:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{v}_t &= (1 - \beta_1)\left(1 + \beta_1 + \dots + \beta_1^{t-1}\right)\mathbf{g} \\
&= (1 - \beta_1)\,\frac{1 - \beta_1^t}{1 - \beta_1}\,\mathbf{g} = \left(1 - \beta_1^t\right)\mathbf{g}
\end{aligned}
$$

The raw average is scaled by $(1 - \beta_1^t)$; dividing by this term recovers unbiased expectation $\mathbf{g}$. While $\beta_1 = 0.9$ dissipates its bias within tens of steps, $\beta_2 = 0.999$ recovers slowly: $1 - 0.999^{100} \approx 0.095$, meaning uncorrected $\mathbf{s}_{100}$ is only 10% of true scale after 100 steps. Without correction, early steps remain amplified for hundreds of iterations.

On our ravine, Adam ($\eta = 0.1$) converges in **193 steps**—faster than GD (369), but behind Momentum (111) and RMSProp (103). Adam is not universally the fastest optimizer; its advantage lies in exceptional stability across diverse gradient scales.

:::note[Key Takeaway]
Momentum accumulates gradients to accelerate along consistent directions. RMSProp scales steps inversely by recent gradient RMS, maintaining step lengths near $\eta$ across disparate scales. Adam combines both, scaling by $1 - \beta^t$ to counteract zero-initialization bias.
:::

### Learning Rate Schedules

Under mini-batch noise, maintaining constant $\eta$ causes parameters to bounce around the minimum. Decaying $\eta$ over time stabilizes convergence:

- **Step decay**: Multiplies $\eta$ by 0.1 at fixed epoch milestones.
- **Cosine decay**: Decays $\eta$ from $\eta_{\max}$ to $\eta_{\min}$ along a cosine half-cycle—gentle at the start and finish, steepest in the middle.
- **Warmup**: Linearly ramps $\eta$ from near zero to $\eta_{\max}$ during initial steps before decaying. This prevents unstable updates when parameter weights and optimizer moments are uncalibrated. Transformer and LLM training universally pair warmup with cosine decay.

Over total steps $T$, cosine decay follows:

$$
\eta_t = \eta_{\min} + \frac{1}{2}\left(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min}\right)\left(1 + \cos\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)
$$

At $t = 0$, $\eta_0 = \eta_{\max}$; at $t = T$, $\eta_T = \eta_{\min}$.

### Practical Recommendations

| Setting | Recommended Starting Point |
|---|---|
| Deep neural networks, new problems | Adam ($\eta = 10^{-3}$). Most forgiving baseline |
| Transformers and LLMs | AdamW with warmup and cosine decay |
| Computer vision CNNs (tuned) | SGD with Momentum 0.9 (or Nesterov) and step/cosine decay |
| Small linear models, tabular data | Standard solvers (Normal equation, L-BFGS in scikit-learn) |

**AdamW** (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) decouples weight decay from gradient updates. In standard Adam with L2 penalty, regularizing gradients are divided by $\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{s}}_t}$, inadvertently penalizing large-gradient weights less. AdamW subtracts decay term $\eta\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}$ directly outside the adaptive fraction, ensuring uniform shrinkage across all parameters.

### Limitations

- **No single optimizer dominates all benchmarks.** On simple quadratic surfaces, Momentum and RMSProp often outperform Adam. Wilson et al. (2017) observed that well-tuned SGD with momentum can yield better test generalization than adaptive methods.
- **First-order limitations.** All four methods remain strictly first-order gradient algorithms, mitigating but not eliminating condition number bottlenecks. Feature scaling remains essential.
- **Non-convex landscapes.** Complex networks feature non-convex saddle points and local minima where global convergence guarantees do not hold.

## Common Pitfalls

:::caution[Switching Optimizers Without Re-tuning the Learning Rate]
Each optimizer interprets $\eta$ differently. Under Momentum ($\beta = 0.9$), effective step length scales to $\approx 10\,\eta\,\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert$, whereas Adam maintains steps near $\eta$ regardless of gradient scale. When switching optimizers, always reset $\eta$ to standard defaults: $10^{-3}$ for Adam, and $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-1}$ for SGD with momentum.
:::

:::caution[Omitting Bias Correction or Indexing Time from Step Zero]
Omitting bias correction causes early updates to be 2 to 6.5 times larger than $\eta$ (under standard hyperparameters), frequently destabilizing training at the outset. Indexing time from $t = 0$ evaluates $1 - \beta^0 = 0$, causing division by zero. Always index steps starting at $t = 1$.
:::

:::caution[Assuming Adam Replaces Feature Standardization]
While Adam adapts learning rates per coordinate axis, it cannot untangle diagonal correlations between non-orthogonal features. Standardizing inputs as shown in [Gradient Descent](/en/ml/gradient-descent/) remains computationally inexpensive and universally recommended.
:::

## Summary and Further Reading

**Key Takeaways:**

- Standard GD crawls when condition number $\kappa$ is large because steep directions constrain $\eta < 2/L$. On our $\kappa = 100$ ravine, GD requires 369 iterations.
- Momentum maintains velocity $\mathbf{v}_t = \beta\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + \mathbf{g}_t$, accelerating consistent directions by $1 / (1 - \beta)$ while damping transversal oscillations, at the cost of inertial overshoot.
- RMSProp scales updates by the square root of exponentially decayed squared gradients, keeping step lengths near $\eta$ and conferring loss-scale invariance. AdaGrad accumulates sums indefinitely, freezing prematurely.
- Adam tracks both first and second moments, applying $1 - \beta^t$ bias correction to prevent initial step blowup. Recommended default: Adam (or AdamW) with $\eta = 10^{-3}$ and a learning rate schedule.
