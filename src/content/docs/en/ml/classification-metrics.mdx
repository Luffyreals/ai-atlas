After this lesson, you will be able to:

- Understand the precision–recall trade-off when tuning decision thresholds, identifying when to prioritize precision versus recall, and selecting thresholds on a validation set.
- Interpret precision–recall curves, ROC curves with AUC, and evaluate `macro avg` versus `weighted avg` in scikit-learn's `classification_report`.

### A Trivial Model Can Still Score 70%

The most obvious evaluation approach is counting correct predictions. By standard convention, anyone with $p \ge 0.5$ is predicted as "diseased". Patients 1 through 7 are predicted positive; the remaining 13 are predicted negative. Comparing with column $y$, the model is correct for 15 out of 20 patients, yielding **75% accuracy**.

That seems reasonable. Now consider a "dummy" model that learns nothing and **predicts healthy every time**. It correctly identifies all 14 healthy individuals, securing $14/20 = 70\%$ accuracy. The trained model is merely 5 percentage points better.

Worse yet, the trivial "always healthy" model detects **not a single sick patient**. It is entirely useless for medical screening, yet boasts a 70% score. If the disease were rarer—say, 1 in 100 people—a dummy model would trivially reach 99% accuracy.

### Not All Errors Are Created Equal

A single 75% accuracy figure conceals a critical nuance: **what types of mistakes** the model makes. The 5 misclassified patients fall into two distinct categories:

- Patients 3, 5, and 7 are healthy but flagged as sick. They undergo unnecessary confirmatory testing, incurring financial cost and anxiety.
- Patients 8 and 11 have the disease but are told they are healthy. They return home, missing timely intervention.

In medical screening, the second failure mode is vastly more dangerous. Accuracy treats both errors as completely equal.

Furthermore, the 0.5 threshold is an arbitrary design choice. Lowering it to 0.3 catches patients 8 and 11, but also misdiagnoses patients 9 and 10. Each threshold produces a different trade-off between error types.

The core problem of this lesson is: **how to evaluate classification quality so that specific error types are exposed—especially under class imbalance—and how to select thresholds aligned with real-world operating costs.**

## Theory

### From Probabilities to Labels: Decision Thresholds

Let $p^{(i)}$ be the model's estimated probability that sample $i$ belongs to the positive class. To assign a predicted label $\hat{y}^{(i)}$, we compare $p^{(i)}$ against a **decision threshold** $t$:

$$
\hat{y}^{(i)} =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } p^{(i)} \ge t \\
0 & \text{if } p^{(i)} < t
\end{cases}
$$

Scikit-learn's `predict` method applies $t = 0.5$ by default (strictly predicting positive when $p > 0.5$, differing only at exact ties), but nothing obligates using 0.5. The model learns probabilities $p$; choosing $t$ is your decision based on asymmetric error costs. All metrics discussed below (except AUC) are calculated **after** choosing $t$; shifting $t$ shifts the metrics.

### Confusion Matrix

Comparing predicted label $\hat{y}$ with true label $y$, every sample falls into one of four distinct categories:

| True Label | Predicted Positive ($\hat{y} = 1$) | Predicted Negative ($\hat{y} = 0$) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Positive ($y = 1$) | TP: True Positive | FN: False Negative |
| Negative ($y = 0$) | FP: False Positive | TN: True Negative |

- **TP** (True Positive): Has disease, predicted positive.
- **FN** (False Negative): Has disease, predicted negative. A **missed detection** (Type II error).
- **FP** (False Positive): Healthy, predicted positive. A **false alarm** (Type I error).
- **TN** (True Negative): Healthy, predicted negative.

The second letter (P or N) represents the model's **prediction**. The first letter (T or F) indicates whether that prediction is correct. This layout is the **confusion matrix**.

For our 20 patients with $t = 0.5$: TP comprises patients 1, 2, 4, 6; FP includes 3, 5, 7; FN contains 8 and 11; the remaining eleven individuals are TN.

| True Label | Predicted Positive | Predicted Negative |
|---|---:|---:|
| Positive (6 patients) | TP = 4 | FN = 2 |
| Negative (14 patients) | FP = 3 | TN = 11 |

Row sums represent actual class sizes in the dataset and remain constant regardless of threshold. Column sums represent the number of predicted positives and negatives, varying with $t$.

### Accuracy

Accuracy is the proportion of correctly classified instances across all samples—the sum of the diagonal divided by the total:

$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{TP} + \text{TN}}{\text{TP} + \text{TN} + \text{FP} + \text{FN}} = \frac{4 + 11}{20} = 0.75
$$

The "always negative" model has $\text{TP} = 0, \text{FP} = 0, \text{FN} = 6, \text{TN} = 14$, giving accuracy $14/20 = 0.70$. The vast majority of points contributing to accuracy stem from the TN quadrant—the dominant class. The more imbalanced the classes, the more TN overwhelms the score, rendering accuracy uninformative regarding the minority positive class.

### Precision and Recall

To evaluate the positive class specifically, we use two complementary metrics:

**Precision**: Among all samples **predicted positive**, what proportion is truly positive? We divide TP by the entire "predicted positive" column:

$$
\text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}} = \frac{4}{4 + 3} \approx 0.571
$$

**Recall** (also known as **sensitivity** or true positive rate): Among all samples that are **truly positive**, what proportion did the model catch? We divide TP by the entire "actual positive" row:

$$
\text{Recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}} = \frac{4}{4 + 2} \approx 0.667
$$

Both formulas share the numerator TP, differing only in the denominator. Precision is degraded by **false alarms** (FP). Recall is degraded by **missed detections** (FN).

For the negative class, an analogous metric to recall is **specificity** (true negative rate): the fraction of actual negatives correctly classified as negative:

$$
\text{Specificity} = \frac{\text{TN}}{\text{TN} + \text{FP}} = \frac{11}{11 + 3} \approx 0.786
$$

In medicine, diagnostic tests are commonly characterized by sensitivity and specificity. In machine learning, the precision–recall pair is more prevalent.

For the trivial "always negative" baseline: $\text{recall} = 0/6 = 0$, immediately unmasking what 70% accuracy disguised. Its precision evaluates to $0/0$: having zero positive predictions, it is mathematically undefined. Scikit-learn issues a warning and returns 0 by default (customizable via the `zero_division` parameter).

### F1: Combining Precision and Recall into a Single Metric

Comparing models with a single number is often convenient. The arithmetic mean $(P + R)/2$ is tempting, but flawed. Consider threshold $t = 0.9$: only patient 1 is predicted positive, yielding $P = 1/1 = 1.0$ and $R = 1/6 \approx 0.167$. Their arithmetic mean is $0.583$—higher than at $t = 0.5$, despite missing 5 out of 6 sick individuals.

The **F1 score** resolves this by computing the **harmonic mean**: averaging the reciprocals, then inverting:

$$
\frac{1}{F_1} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{P} + \frac{1}{R}\right)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P + R}
$$

The right-hand side follows by finding a common denominator $\frac{1}{P} + \frac{1}{R} = \frac{P + R}{PR}$ and taking the reciprocal.

Why does the harmonic mean prevent distortion? Small numbers have large reciprocals. When $R \approx 0.167$, $1/R = 6$ dominates $1/P = 1$, driving the average reciprocal high and compressing $F_1$ low. At $t = 0.9$:

$$
F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot 1.0 \cdot 0.167}{1.0 + 0.167} \approx 0.286
$$

F1 is strongly pulled toward the smaller of the two values. To achieve a high F1 score, both precision and recall must be high.

Expressing $P$ and $R$ directly in terms of TP, FP, and FN produces an alternative representation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{F_1} &= \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}{\text{TP}} + \frac{\text{TP} + \text{FN}}{\text{TP}}\right) \\
&= \frac{2\,\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{FN}}{2\,\text{TP}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Inverting yields:

$$
F_1 = \frac{2\,\text{TP}}{2\,\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{FN}}
$$

At $t = 0.5$: $F_1 = 8/(8 + 3 + 2) = 8/13 \approx 0.615$. Notice that the TN term is absent: a flood of true negatives cannot inflate F1 the way it inflates accuracy. Additionally, this form is computable even when positive predictions are zero: when $\text{TP} = 0$, $F_1 = 0$, whereas precision would be $0/0$.

**F-beta.** When one error type carries higher stakes, the generalized $F_\beta$ score balances them:

$$
F_\beta = \frac{(1 + \beta^2)\,P R}{\beta^2 P + R}
$$

Setting $\beta = 1$ recovers $F_1$. Setting $\beta > 1$ weights recall more heavily (e.g., $F_2$ for medical screening), whereas $\beta < 1$ favors precision (e.g., $F_{0.5}$). In scikit-learn, this is accessible via `fbeta_score`.

:::note[Key Takeaway]
$\text{Precision} = \text{TP}/(\text{TP}+\text{FP})$ measures fidelity among positive predictions. $\text{Recall} = \text{TP}/(\text{TP}+\text{FN})$ measures coverage among actual positive cases. F1 is their harmonic mean, bound closely to the weaker metric. Accuracy includes TN, creating false optimism on imbalanced negative-heavy data.
:::

### The Precision–Recall Trade-off Across Thresholds

Varying $t$ across our 20 patients demonstrates this trade-off. The second code cell prints full sweeps from 0.1 to 0.9; representative values are summarized below:

| Threshold $t$ | TP | FP | FN | TN | Accuracy | Precision | Recall | F1 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.30 | 6 | 5 | 0 | 9 | 0.750 | 0.545 | 1.000 | 0.706 |
| 0.50 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 11 | 0.750 | 0.571 | 0.667 | 0.615 |
| 0.70 | 3 | 1 | 3 | 13 | 0.800 | 0.750 | 0.500 | 0.600 |
| 0.80 | 2 | 0 | 4 | 14 | 0.800 | 1.000 | 0.333 | 0.500 |
| 0.95 | 0 | 0 | 6 | 14 | 0.700 | undefined | 0.000 | 0.000 |

Raising $t$ causes fewer samples to be flagged as positive:

- **Recall never increases.** Samples can only drop out of the positive set, so TP is non-increasing while the denominator $\text{TP} + \text{FN}$ remains fixed at 6.
- **Precision usually rises, but non-monotonically.** While FP decreases, TP can also fall. Moving from $t = 0.30$ to $0.35$, the sample eliminated is patient 11 (a true positive), causing precision to **drop** from $0.545$ to $0.500$.
- **Accuracy can rise even as model clinical utility plummets.** At $t = 0.80$, accuracy climbs to $0.800$ (higher than at $0.50$), yet recall drops to $0.333$, abandoning 4 out of 6 sick patients.

There is no universally optimal threshold. The question is: **which mistake is more costly?**

- **Prioritize recall when misses are expensive.** In clinical screening, a false alarm merely requires a follow-up test, whereas a missed diagnosis may prove fatal. Lower the threshold. Fraud detection often adopts the same stance.
- **Prioritize precision when false alarms are costly.** In spam filtering, an occasional junk email in the inbox is minor, but diverting a critical job offer to spam can be disastrous. Raise the threshold.

Selecting $t$ tunes a hyperparameter. Therefore, optimize it on the **validation set** (or via cross-validation as taught in [Overfitting and Underfitting](/en/ml/overfitting-underfitting/)), and evaluate on the test set once with the finalized threshold. Searching for the highest test F1 across thresholds commits the pitfall of "selecting models using test data" highlighted in [Introduction to Machine Learning](/en/ml/ml-overview/). Scikit-learn provides `TunedThresholdClassifierCV` to automate this procedure cleanly.

### Precision–Recall and ROC Curves

Rather than inspecting discrete thresholds, sweeping across **all possible** thresholds traces continuous evaluation curves.

The **precision–recall curve** plots recall on the horizontal axis and precision on the vertical axis. Every point corresponds to a threshold. A high-performing classifier hugs the upper-right corner (high recall with sustained high precision). A random guessing baseline forms a flat horizontal line at the dataset's positive prevalence rate ($6/20 = 0.3$).

The **ROC curve** (Receiver Operating Characteristic) plots the **false positive rate** (FPR) on the x-axis:

$$
\text{FPR} = \frac{\text{FP}}{\text{FP} + \text{TN}} = 1 - \text{Specificity}
$$

against recall (true positive rate) on the y-axis. Lowering $t$ from 1 to 0 traces a curve from $(0, 0)$ (predicting nobody positive) to $(1, 1)$ (predicting everyone positive). Random guessing follows the diagonal.

**AUC** (Area Under the Curve) measures the area under the ROC curve, ranging from 0 to 1. It carries an intuitive probabilistic interpretation: given a randomly chosen positive sample and negative sample, AUC is the probability that the model ranks the positive sample higher than the negative one. Among our 20 patients, there are $6 \times 14 = 84$ positive-negative pairs. Inspecting the table: patient 4 is ranked below 1 healthy patient (patient 3), patient 6 below 2, patient 8 below 3, patient 11 below 5. In total, 11 pairs are inverted:

$$
\text{AUC} = \frac{84 - 11}{84} \approx 0.869
$$

AUC depends solely on prediction **rank ordering**, completely independent of any chosen threshold. It is ideal for comparing model ranking capabilities prior to selecting an operating threshold. When the positive class is heavily outnumbered, the vast number of TNs keeps FPR perpetually small, making ROC curves appear deceptively optimistic. Under severe class imbalance, the precision–recall curve provides a much more faithful reflection of minority-class performance.

### Multi-class: Macro, Micro, and Weighted Averages

For $K$ classes (e.g., digit recognition from 0 to 9, covered in [Softmax Regression](/en/ml/softmax-regression/)), one calculates precision, recall, and F1 for **each individual class** by treating it as positive and all others as negative. These $K$ scores are then aggregated using one of three strategies:

- **Macro average**: The unweighted arithmetic mean across all $K$ classes, weighting each class equally. Poor performance on a rare class significantly penalizes the macro score.
- **Weighted average**: The mean weighted by the true support (sample count) of each class. Dominant classes control the score.
- **Micro average**: Aggregates total TP, FP, and FN across all classes before computing a global metric. When every sample has exactly one ground truth label, micro precision, recall, and F1 all mathematically equal overall accuracy.

To diagnose whether a model neglects minority classes, always check `macro avg`. The third code example illustrates how widely these aggregations can diverge.

### Limitations

- **Small sample sizes cause high variance in metrics.** With only 6 positive patients, catching or missing one individual shifts recall by $1/6 \approx 0.167$. Treat figures on 20 patients as educational illustrations.
- **Metrics do not incorporate dollar costs directly.** F1 treats FP and FN symmetrically; $F_\beta$ adjusts their weighting heuristically. When real business costs are known, select the threshold that minimizes expected financial loss.
- **Threshold-dependent metrics do not verify probability calibration.** A well-calibrated model ensures that among cases assigned $p = 0.9$, approximately 90% are truly positive. Calibration is an orthogonal property not captured by thresholded counts.
- **Measuring imbalance is not equivalent to remedying it.** Techniques for handling class imbalance (resampling, class-weighted loss) are detailed in [Data Preprocessing](/en/ml/data-preprocessing/).

## Common Pitfalls

:::caution[Reporting Accuracy Without a Baseline]
A test accuracy of 0.960 sounds impressive until one learns that an uninformative majority-class predictor achieves 0.945. Always contextualize accuracy against `DummyClassifier(strategy="most_frequent")`, and report precision, recall, and F1 for minority classes (`classification_report` displays all three by default).
:::

:::caution[Tuning Decision Thresholds on the Test Set]
Sweeping thresholds directly on test data to report the best obtainable F1 invalidates evaluation by treating the test set as validation data. The reported metric will be over-optimistic compared to future real-world performance. Optimize thresholds on a validation set or via cross-validation (`TunedThresholdClassifierCV`), evaluating the test set strictly once.
:::

:::caution[Reporting Precision or Recall in Isolation]
Either metric is trivial to game in isolation. Predicting positive on every sample ($t = 0$) guarantees 1.000 recall despite miserable precision. Conversely, predicting positive on only the single most confident instance ($t = 0.9$) guarantees 1.000 precision while sacrificing recall. Always report both together alongside the operating threshold, or summarize them via F1 and precision–recall curves.
:::

## Summary and Further Reading

**Key Takeaways:**

- Accuracy $= (\text{TP}+\text{TN})/m$ is easily distorted by class imbalance: a majority-class predictor matches the majority prevalence (70% on 20 patients, 94.5% in our synthetic experiment). Always compare against this baseline.
- A confusion matrix partitions errors into false alarms (FP) and missed detections (FN). $\text{Precision} = \text{TP}/(\text{TP}+\text{FP})$ penalizes false alarms, while $\text{Recall} = \text{TP}/(\text{TP}+\text{FN})$ penalizes misses. $F_1 = 2PR/(P+R) = 2\text{TP}/(2\text{TP}+\text{FP}+\text{FN})$ is their harmonic mean, tethered closely to the weaker score.
- The model outputs continuous probabilities $p$; selecting threshold $t$ is your choice. Raising $t$ cannot increase recall and typically raises precision. Prioritize recall when misses are catastrophic (medical screening); prioritize precision when false alarms are intolerable (spam filtering). Optimize $t$ on validation data.
- Precision–recall and ROC curves sweep across all thresholds. AUC represents the probability that a random positive instance is ranked above a random negative one; under severe imbalance, precision–recall curves provide more truthful feedback. For multi-class settings, macro averages treat all classes equally, weighted averages scale by support, and micro averages equate to accuracy.
