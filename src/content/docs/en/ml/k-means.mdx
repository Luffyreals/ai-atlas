## Intuition

Imagine you manage a retail fashion chain with tens of thousands of customers. For each customer, you record monthly visit frequency and average transaction spending. You want to group customers to tailor marketing promotions, but possess no predefined labels such as "VIP", "Budget", or "Bargain Hunter".

This is the domain of **unsupervised learning** and **clustering**: grouping similar observations together such that points within the same group are as close as possible, while separate groups remain well separated.

### Clustering Around Centroids

A natural way to define a cluster is to appoint a representative center—a **centroid**. Suppose you want to partition data into $k = 3$ clusters:

1. Place 3 candidate centroids at arbitrary locations across the store space.
2. Each customer observes the centroids and associates with the one closest to them, forming 3 temporary clusters.
3. Each centroid steps to the center of gravity (arithmetic mean) of all customers assigned to it.
4. As centroids relocate, some customers find another centroid now closer, switching groups accordingly.

This process alternates. After several rounds, assignments stabilize and centroids stop moving. The algorithm has converged.

The objective is: **partition $m$ unlabeled observations into $k$ clusters minimizing the total sum of squared Euclidean distances from each point to its assigned cluster centroid.**

## Theory

### Problem Formulation

Given $m$ samples $\mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \mathbf{x}^{(2)}, \dots, \mathbf{x}^{(m)} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ without target labels $y$, we partition them into $k$ disjoint subsets:

$$
C_1 \cup C_2 \cup \dots \cup C_k = \{1, 2, \dots, m\}, \quad C_i \cap C_j = \emptyset \; (\forall i \ne j)
$$

Each cluster $j \in \{1, \dots, k\}$ possesses centroid $\boldsymbol{\mu}_j \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $c^{(i)} \in \{1, \dots, k\}$ denote the cluster assigned to sample $i$.

The objective to minimize is total squared distance from points to centroids, termed **inertia** or **distortion**:

$$
J(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \sum_{i=1}^m \lVert \mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c^{(i)}} \rVert^2
$$

where:
- $\mathbf{c} = [c^{(1)}, c^{(2)}, \dots, c^{(m)}]^\top$ represents sample assignments.
- $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \{\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\}$ denotes the set of centroids.

Simultaneous optimization over both discrete assignments $\mathbf{c}$ and continuous coordinates $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is NP-hard. We turn to an alternating iterative heuristic: **Lloyd's algorithm** (commonly referred to as standard K-means).

### Lloyd's Algorithm: Two Alternating Steps

Lloyd's algorithm alternates between optimizing assignments $\mathbf{c}$ and centroids $\boldsymbol{\mu}$:

```
Initialize k candidate centroids μ_1, ..., μ_k
Repeat until convergence:
    Step 1 (Assignment): Fix μ, find optimal assignments c
    Step 2 (Update): Fix c, find optimal centroids μ
```

#### Step 1: Cluster Assignment

Holding centroids $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k$ fixed, minimizing $J$ decouples across individual samples. Each sample $\mathbf{x}^{(i)}$ is assigned to its nearest centroid:

$$
c^{(i)} = \arg\min_{j \in \{1, \dots, k\}} \lVert \mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j \rVert^2
$$

Ties are broken arbitrarily. At this step, inertia is guaranteed non-increasing: $J(\mathbf{c}^{(t+1)}, \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t)}) \le J(\mathbf{c}^{(t)}, \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t)})$.

#### Step 2: Centroid Update

Holding assignments $\mathbf{c}$ fixed, we optimize coordinates $\boldsymbol{\mu}_j$ to minimize $J$:

$$
J(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \sum_{j=1}^k \sum_{i \in C_j} \lVert \mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j \rVert^2
$$

Because clusters partition samples independently, we minimize each cluster sum separately. Differentiating with respect to centroid $\boldsymbol{\mu}_j$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_j} J &= \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_j} \sum_{i \in C_j} (\mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j)^\top (\mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j) \\
&= \sum_{i \in C_j} -2(\mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j) \\
&= -2 \left( \sum_{i \in C_j} \mathbf{x}^{(i)} - |C_j| \boldsymbol{\mu}_j \right)
\end{aligned}
$$

Setting the gradient to $\mathbf{0}$:

$$
\sum_{i \in C_j} \mathbf{x}^{(i)} - |C_j| \boldsymbol{\mu}_j = \mathbf{0} \implies \boldsymbol{\mu}_j = \frac{1}{|C_j|} \sum_{i \in C_j} \mathbf{x}^{(i)}
$$

The optimal centroid is the **arithmetic mean** (centroid) of all samples currently in that cluster. Inertia is again non-increasing: $J(\mathbf{c}^{(t+1)}, \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t+1)}) \le J(\mathbf{c}^{(t+1)}, \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t)})$.

:::note[Convergence of Lloyd's Algorithm]
Because inertia $J$ decreases monotonically at each half-step, and the finite number of distinct assignments ($k^m$) precludes infinite descent, Lloyd's algorithm **is guaranteed to converge** in a finite number of iterations. Growth halts once assignments cease changing.
:::

### Initialization and the K-means++ Algorithm

Although guaranteed to converge, Lloyd's algorithm settles at a **local minimum** dictated heavily by initial centroid placement:

- Poor initial seeds (e.g., placing two initial centroids within a single natural cluster) can split one true cluster while merging two separate clusters.
- Empty clusters ($|C_j| = 0$) can emerge if an initial centroid attracts no samples.

#### Strategy 1: Multiple Random Restarts (`n_init`)

The simplest mitigation is running Lloyd's algorithm across multiple independent random initializations (historically `n_init=10` in scikit-learn), retaining the solution achieving lowest final inertia $J$.

#### Strategy 2: The K-means++ Algorithm

Arthur & Vassilvitskii (2007) proposed **K-means++**, establishing that initial centroids should be seeded as far apart as possible.

K-means++ seeding steps:
1. Choose the first centroid $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1$ uniformly at random from the dataset.
2. For each sample $\mathbf{x}^{(i)}$, compute its distance $D(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})$ to the nearest already chosen centroid:
   $$
   D(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}) = \min_{j} \lVert \mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j \rVert
   $$
3. Select the next centroid with probability proportional to squared distance:
   $$
   P(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}) = \frac{D(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})^2}{\sum_{l=1}^m D(\mathbf{x}^{(l)})^2}
   $$
4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until $k$ centroids are chosen.

Samples remote from established centroids exhibit large $D(\mathbf{x})^2$, drastically boosting their odds of becoming subsequent centroids. This dramatically curbs poor local minima and accelerates convergence. K-means++ is the standard default across modern libraries (`init='k-means++'`).

### Choosing Number of Clusters k: Elbow and Silhouette

In real applications, ground-truth cluster count $k$ is typically unknown. Why not simply select $k$ that minimizes inertia $J$?

Because inertia strictly decreases as $k$ increases. At $k = m$, every sample is its own centroid, achieving $J = 0$ trivially. This extreme lacks practical grouping utility. Two quantitative heuristics guide selection:

#### The Elbow Method

Plot inertia $J$ across $k \in \{1, 2, \dots\}$. As $k$ advances toward the true cluster count, $J$ plunges sharply. Once past the true count, additional centroids merely subdivide natural clusters, causing $J$ to flatten.

The resulting curve exhibits an angle resembling a bent elbow. The location of this "elbow" provides an intuitive trade-off between compactness and model complexity.

#### The Silhouette Score

When the elbow curve is ambiguous, the **Silhouette score** (Rousseeuw, 1987) measures cluster quality directly per sample via intra-cluster cohesion and inter-cluster separation.

For each sample $\mathbf{x}^{(i)} \in C_I$:
1. Compute $a^{(i)}$: average distance from $\mathbf{x}^{(i)}$ to all other points within its assigned cluster $C_I$ (intra-cluster cohesion; lower is better):
   $$
   a^{(i)} = \frac{1}{|C_I| - 1} \sum_{j \in C_I, j \ne i} \lVert \mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \mathbf{x}^{(j)} \rVert
   $$
2. Compute $b^{(i)}$: smallest average distance from $\mathbf{x}^{(i)}$ to all points in any other cluster $C_J \ne C_I$ (nearest-cluster separation; higher is better):
   $$
   b^{(i)} = \min_{J \ne I} \frac{1}{|C_J|} \sum_{j \in C_J} \lVert \mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \mathbf{x}^{(j)} \rVert
   $$
3. The Silhouette coefficient of sample $i$ is:
   $$
   s^{(i)} = \frac{b^{(i)} - a^{(i)}}{\max(a^{(i)}, b^{(i)})} \in [-1, 1]
   $$

Interpretation of $s^{(i)}$:
- $s^{(i)} \approx 1$: $a^{(i)} \ll b^{(i)}$; the sample is deeply embedded within its cluster, far from neighboring clusters (ideal).
- $s^{(i)} \approx 0$: $a^{(i)} \approx b^{(i)}$; the sample sits right on the boundary between two clusters.
- $s^{(i)} < 0$: $a^{(i)} > b^{(i)}$; the sample is closer to an adjacent cluster than its assigned cluster (misclustered).

The mean Silhouette score across all samples $\bar{s} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m s^{(i)}$ peaks at the optimal cluster count $k$.

### Limitations

- **Requires predetermining $k$.** K-means cannot automatically deduce intrinsic cluster counts.
- **Assumes convex, isotropic clusters.** Because it minimizes Euclidean distance, K-means partitions space into linear Voronoi polyhedra. It fails when clusters have non-convex geometries (such as interlocking crescents or concentric circles) or drastically disparate densities.
- **Sensitive to outliers.** Squaring Euclidean distances gives distant anomalies outsized leverage, pulling centroids away from dense modes.
- **Demands feature scaling.** If one feature has vastly larger numerical magnitude than others, Euclidean distance is dominated by that single dimension.

## Common Pitfalls

:::caution[Omitting Feature Standardization Before Clustering]
Because K-means relies on Euclidean distance $\sum (x_j - \mu_j)^2$, features with large numerical magnitudes dominate cluster assignments. Always scale features to zero mean and unit variance using `StandardScaler` prior to clustering.
:::

:::caution[Expecting K-means to Segment Non-Spherical Clusters]
Voronoi partitions form linear decision boundaries. On datasets with non-convex geometries (like concentric rings or interlocking crescents), K-means cuts across natural manifolds. Use density-based algorithms such as DBSCAN or Spectral Clustering for arbitrary shapes.
:::

:::caution[Selecting k Solely by Minimizing Inertia]
Inertia $J$ decreases monotonically as $k$ increases, reaching $J = 0$ when $k = m$. Clustering aims to discover compact latent structures, not assign custom singletons. Always evaluate candidate counts via the Elbow inflection point or Silhouette peak.
:::

## Summary and Further Reading

**Key Takeaways:**

- K-means is a foundational unsupervised algorithm partitioning $m$ unlabeled samples into $k$ clusters by minimizing inertia $J = \sum_{i=1}^m \lVert \mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c^{(i)}} \rVert^2$.
- Lloyd's algorithm alternates between assigning points to closest centroids and updating centroids to cluster arithmetic means, guaranteed to reach local convergence.
- K-means++ initialization disperses initial seeds using $D(\mathbf{x})^2$ probability weighting, preventing poor local minima.
- Optimal cluster count $k$ is identified via Elbow inflection points and maximum Silhouette scores. Always scale features beforehand.
