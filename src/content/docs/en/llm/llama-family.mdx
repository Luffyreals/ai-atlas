## Intuition

### Same frame, a few swapped parts

In 2023, Meta released LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023). According to the paper, its 13-billion-parameter version outperformed GPT-3 (175 billion) on most benchmarks tested, despite being more than $10\times$ smaller and able to run on a single GPU.

The secret was not a radical new architecture. LLaMA is still the GPT architecture from earlier lessons, swapping three components for well-tested alternatives from prior papers (and adding a fourth, GQA, starting in Llama 2), and then training the model **much longer on much more data** than was previously standard. The authors' reasoning was simple: inference cost scales with model size, so training a smaller model for longer pays off over the lifetime of deployment.

| Component | GPT in earlier lessons | Llama family |
|---|---|---|
| Normalization | LayerNorm | RMSNorm: drops the mean-centering step |
| Feed-forward network | GELU, 2 matrices, hidden size $4D$ | SwiGLU, 3 matrices, hidden size roughly $\tfrac{2}{3} \cdot 4D$ |
| Position encoding | Position vector added to input embeddings | RoPE: rotates $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ inside attention at every layer |
| Attention | Separate K, V per head | GQA (from Llama 2): groups of query heads share K, V |

The first paper spelled the name "LLaMA"; later generations write "Llama".

### Position: fixed label or relative offset?

In the phrase "the cat chased the mouse", "cat" sits one token before "chased", whether the phrase appears at the very start of a document or after $1\,000$ preceding tokens. What matters is the **relative offset** between the two positions. Yet the GPT model in earlier lessons adds a distinct position vector to each absolute slot, so the pair of positions $(3, 2)$ and the pair $(1003, 1002)$ look like two completely different inputs, forcing the model to learn from scratch that they mean the same relative distance.

RoPE bakes relative offset directly into the dot product. Picture each pair of dimensions in the query and key vectors as a clock face. A token at position $m$ has its hand rotated by $m$ ticks, so two tokens separated by 2 positions always differ by 2 ticks regardless of where they sit in the text. Different dimension pairs rotate at different speeds—like second, minute, and hour hands: fast hands distinguish nearby tokens, while slow hands distinguish distant ones.

### One GPU is not enough

The 65-billion-parameter LLaMA model was trained on $2\,048$ A100 80 GB GPUs, processing roughly 380 tokens per second per GPU, and took about 21 days to get through 1.4 trillion tokens. During training, each parameter requires roughly 16 bytes of memory (we will count why in the Theory section), which comes to about $1\,040$ GB for 65 billion parameters: the combined memory of 13 80 GB GPUs, before counting any activations (intermediate layer outputs kept for the backward pass).

Work must be split up, like a kitchen preparing 100 meals: you can open multiple identical kitchens; have two chefs collaborate on the same dish; or set up an assembly line for prepping, cooking, and plating. Those correspond to the three parallelism strategies in this lesson.

The problem of this lesson is: **what does LLaMA change in GPT and why—specifically, how does RoPE make the positional dependence in attention scores a function of only the relative offset $m - n$? And when a model exceeds the memory of a single GPU, how can we partition training across many GPUs?**

## Theory

### The LLaMA Transformer block

Each LLaMA block performs two steps, each wrapped in a residual connection just like GPT:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{H} &\leftarrow \mathbf{H} + \mathrm{Attn}_{\mathrm{RoPE}}\big(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(\mathbf{H})\big) \\
\mathbf{H} &\leftarrow \mathbf{H} + \mathrm{FFN}_{\mathrm{SwiGLU}}\big(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(\mathbf{H})\big)
\end{aligned}
$$

Normalization sits **before** each sub-layer (pre-norm) just as in GPT-2; the LLaMA paper notes this choice improves training stability. The input to the first block is simply the token embeddings, with no added positional embeddings.

### RMSNorm: divide only, do not subtract

Standard LayerNorm subtracts the mean of the components of $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^D$, divides by the standard deviation, and then scales and shifts using two learnable vectors. Zhang and Sennrich (2019) hypothesized that what actually stabilizes training is **rescaling** by the magnitude, while mean-centering is unnecessary. Dropping both the mean subtraction and the learnable shift vector gives RMSNorm:

$$
\mathrm{RMS}(\mathbf{x}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D} x_i^2 + \epsilon},
\qquad
\mathrm{RMSNorm}(\mathbf{x})_i = \frac{x_i}{\mathrm{RMS}(\mathbf{x})}\, g_i
$$

Here $\mathrm{RMS}$ is the root mean square, $\epsilon$ is a tiny constant to prevent division by zero, and $\mathbf{g} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is a learnable gain vector.

**Why is it still scale-invariant?** Replace $\mathbf{x}$ with $c\,\mathbf{x}$ for any constant $c > 0$, ignoring $\epsilon$:

$$
\mathrm{RMS}(c\,\mathbf{x}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D} c^2 x_i^2} = \sqrt{c^2}\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D} x_i^2} = c\,\mathrm{RMS}(\mathbf{x})
$$

The first equality factors $c^2$ out of the sum, and the second uses $\sqrt{c^2} = c$ for $c > 0$. Consequently, $\frac{c\,x_i}{c\,\mathrm{RMS}(\mathbf{x})} = \frac{x_i}{\mathrm{RMS}(\mathbf{x})}$: scaling the input up or down by any positive factor leaves the normalized output unchanged. Before multiplying by $\mathbf{g}$, the output vector always has an RMS of 1. Fewer reductions also mean faster execution.

### SwiGLU: adding a gate

The feed-forward network (FFN; the MLP block in earlier lessons) of GPT uses two matrices, $\mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times 4D}$ and $\mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{4D \times D}$: $\mathrm{FFN}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathrm{GELU}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}_1)\,\mathbf{W}_2$, where $\mathbf{x}$ is a row vector. SwiGLU (Shazeer, 2020) introduces a third projection and multiplies two branches element-wise ($\odot$):

$$
\mathrm{FFN}_{\mathrm{SwiGLU}}(\mathbf{x}) = \big(\mathrm{Swish}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}_1) \odot \mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}_3\big)\,\mathbf{W}_2,
\qquad
\mathrm{Swish}(u) = u\,\sigma(u)
$$

Here $\mathbf{W}_1, \mathbf{W}_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times d_{\text{ff}}}$, $\mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}} \times D}$, and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. The Swish branch acts as a **gate**: for each coordinate, it controls how much of the linear branch $\mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}_3$ passes through.

**Why is the hidden dimension set to $\tfrac{2}{3} \cdot 4D$?** Three matrices have more parameters than two. To keep the parameter count matched to a standard $4D$ FFN, set the two counts equal:

$$
\underbrace{2 \cdot D \cdot 4D}_{\text{FFN}} = \underbrace{3 \cdot D \cdot d_{\text{ff}}}_{\text{SwiGLU}}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
d_{\text{ff}} = \frac{8D}{3} = \frac{2}{3} \cdot 4D
$$

Llama 3 deviates slightly from this exact ratio: the 8B model has $D = 4\,096$ and an FFN hidden dimension of $14\,336$, which is $3.5D$. In Shazeer's experiments, SwiGLU consistently achieved among the best perplexities; the author candidly noted that we have no theoretical explanation for why these gated variants work so well, only empirical evidence.

### RoPE: rotate instead of add

**The old way.** GPT adds positional vectors to token embeddings before projecting to queries and keys. Expanding the dot product yields cross-terms that mix token content with isolated positions $m$ or $n$, making the score depend on absolute positions (as the NumPy cell in Code examples demonstrates numerically).

**The idea of RoPE** (Su et al., 2021). Keep input embeddings free of positional information. Compute $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ from content as usual, and then **rotate** $\mathbf{q}$ by an angle proportional to its position $m$, and rotate $\mathbf{k}$ by an angle proportional to its position $n$. Note that in this lesson $m$ and $n$ denote token positions (following the RoFormer paper), rather than sample count and feature count.

**The two-dimensional case.** The rotation matrix for an angle $\alpha$ is:

$$
\mathbf{R}(\alpha) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha \\ \sin\alpha & \cos\alpha \end{pmatrix}
$$

It rotates a 2D vector counterclockwise by an angle $\alpha$ without changing its length. We need two basic properties of 2D rotations:

1. **$\mathbf{R}(\alpha)^\top = \mathbf{R}(-\alpha)$.** Transposing swaps the two $\sin$ entries. Because $\cos(-\alpha) = \cos\alpha$ and $\sin(-\alpha) = -\sin\alpha$, the transpose equals $\mathbf{R}(-\alpha)$: rotating backward by $\alpha$.
2. **$\mathbf{R}(\alpha)\,\mathbf{R}(\beta) = \mathbf{R}(\alpha + \beta)$.** Rotating by $\beta$ and then by $\alpha$ is the same as a single rotation by $\alpha + \beta$. The top-left entry of the product is $\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta = \cos(\alpha + \beta)$ by the angle-addition identity, and the other three entries follow similarly.

Let $\theta$ be the base angle per position step. A query at position $m$ becomes $\mathbf{R}(m\theta)\mathbf{q}$, and a key at position $n$ becomes $\mathbf{R}(n\theta)\mathbf{k}$. Their dot-product score is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\big(\mathbf{R}(m\theta)\,\mathbf{q}\big)^\top \big(\mathbf{R}(n\theta)\,\mathbf{k}\big)
&= \mathbf{q}^\top \mathbf{R}(m\theta)^\top \mathbf{R}(n\theta)\,\mathbf{k} \\
&= \mathbf{q}^\top \mathbf{R}(-m\theta)\, \mathbf{R}(n\theta)\,\mathbf{k} \\
&= \mathbf{q}^\top \mathbf{R}\big((n - m)\theta\big)\,\mathbf{k}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line uses the transpose of a product, $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b})^\top = \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{A}^\top$. The second line uses Property 1. The third line uses Property 2 with $\alpha = -m\theta$ and $\beta = n\theta$.

The result depends on the content vectors ($\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{k}$) and **depends on position only through the relative offset $n - m$**. Shifting both tokens by any constant $c$ gives $(n + c) - (m + c) = n - m$, leaving the score completely unchanged. Order still matters: $(m, n) = (5, 2)$ uses $\mathbf{R}(-3\theta)$ while $(2, 5)$ uses $\mathbf{R}(3\theta)$, which generally produce different scores.

**The $d$-dimensional case.** Each attention head has dimension $d$, where $d$ is even. Group the coordinates into $d/2$ adjacent pairs $(x_1, x_2), (x_3, x_4), \dots$; pair $i$ rotates by angle $m\theta_i$ with frequency:

$$
\theta_i = 10\,000^{-2(i-1)/d}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, d/2
$$

The $d$-dimensional dot product is the sum of the $d/2$ pair dot products. By the 2D derivation above, each pair's contribution depends on position only through $n - m$, so their sum does too.

These are the clock hands from our intuition. The first pair has $\theta_1 = 1$ radian per token, completing a full revolution every $\approx 6$ tokens. For $d = 128$—the head dimension of Llama 3 8B ($4\,096 / 32$)—the last pair has $\theta_{64} = 10\,000^{-126/128} \approx 1.15 \times 10^{-4}$, taking roughly $54\,000$ tokens for a single revolution.

**Implementation.** You never build a full rotation matrix. For each pair and angle $\varphi = m\theta_i$:

$$
x'_{2i-1} = x_{2i-1}\cos\varphi - x_{2i}\sin\varphi,
\qquad
x'_{2i} = x_{2i-1}\sin\varphi + x_{2i}\cos\varphi
$$

In 0-indexed Python, the pairs are `x[0::2]` and `x[1::2]`. Some libraries pair coordinate $j$ with coordinate $j + d/2$ (first half with second half): that is just a permutation of coordinate indices and expresses the exact same math.

RoPE is applied to $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ at **every layer**, but never to $\mathbf{v}$: attention scores come from $\mathbf{q}^\top\mathbf{k}$, which needs relative position, whereas $\mathbf{v}$ is the content payload being aggregated. The RoFormer paper also derives an **upper bound** on the attention score magnitude and shows that it decays as $|m - n|$ grows. Keep in mind that this is an upper bound, not a strict monotonic decrease for every individual pair of vectors.

### GQA: sharing keys and values

**KV cache.** During autoregressive generation, each step needs the keys and values of all preceding tokens at every layer. Because of the causal mask, past keys and values never change when a new token arrives, so we compute them once and store them in the **KV cache**. The cache memory required per token is:

$$
2 \cdot N_{\text{layer}} \cdot H_{\text{kv}} \cdot d \cdot s
$$

The factor of 2 accounts for both K and V; $N_{\text{layer}}$ is the number of layers, $H_{\text{kv}}$ is the number of K/V heads, $d$ is the head dimension, and $s$ is the number of bytes per number. Llama 3 8B has $N_{\text{layer}} = 32$, $H_{\text{kv}} = 8$, and $d = 4\,096/32 = 128$, stored in BF16 ($s = 2$): each token takes $2 \cdot 32 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 131\,072$ bytes (128 KiB), so an $8\,192$-token context takes exactly 1 GiB. If every query head had its own K and V ($H_{\text{kv}} = 32$), that single sequence would take 4 GiB, multiplied by the number of concurrent requests being served.

**Three ways to organize heads.** Standard multi-head attention (MHA) sets $H_{\text{kv}} = H$. Multi-query attention (MQA; Shazeer, 2019) shares a single K, V head across all query heads ($H_{\text{kv}} = 1$): minimal cache, but can hurt quality. **GQA** (Ainslie et al., 2023) sits in between: it partitions the $H$ query heads into $H_{\text{kv}}$ groups, with each group sharing one K, V head. Llama 3 8B has 32 query heads and 8 K/V heads, so every 4 query heads share one K/V pair. The GQA paper reports accuracy close to MHA with inference speed close to MQA. The number of query heads stays at $H$, and total model parameters drop only slightly because only the K and V projection matrices shrink.

### Parallel training

**Where does the memory go?** When training with Adam in mixed precision, the ZeRO paper (Rajbhandari et al., 2020) counts the memory per parameter: 2 bytes for the FP16 weight, 2 bytes for the FP16 gradient, and 12 bytes for the optimizer states (an FP32 master copy of the weight, plus FP32 momentum and variance). That totals 16 bytes per parameter. A model with $\Psi$ parameters requires $16\Psi$ bytes before activations; for $\Psi = 7.5$ billion, that is already 120 GB.

**Data parallelism.** Every GPU holds a **complete copy of the model** and processes a different slice of the batch. After the backward pass, GPUs average their gradients via an **all-reduce** communication step so every replica updates identically. This increases throughput, but saves zero model memory per GPU.

**Tensor parallelism.** Megatron-LM (Shoeybi et al., 2019) splits **individual weight matrices inside each layer**. Take the standard MLP block $\mathbf{Y} = \mathrm{GELU}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{B}$ split across two GPUs:

1. Split $\mathbf{A}$ by columns, $\mathbf{A} = [\mathbf{A}_1, \mathbf{A}_2]$, so $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A} = [\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}_1, \mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}_2]$: each GPU computes half the columns.
2. Because GELU acts element-wise, $\mathrm{GELU}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}) = [\mathbf{Z}_1, \mathbf{Z}_2]$ where $\mathbf{Z}_j = \mathrm{GELU}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{A}_j)$. No communication is needed yet.
3. Split $\mathbf{B}$ by rows into $\mathbf{B}_1$ (top half) and $\mathbf{B}_2$ (bottom half). By block matrix multiplication, $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{Z}_1\mathbf{B}_1 + \mathbf{Z}_2\mathbf{B}_2$. Each GPU computes one term, and a single all-reduce sums them.

For SwiGLU, split both $\mathbf{W}_1$ and $\mathbf{W}_3$ column-wise and $\mathbf{W}_2$ row-wise. For attention, each GPU handles a subset of heads. Because every single layer requires an all-reduce, tensor parallelism demands ultra-fast interconnects—typically NVLink among GPUs inside the same node.

**Pipeline parallelism.** Split the model **depth-wise**: GPU 1 holds the first few layers, GPU 2 the next few, and so on. Each group of layers is a pipeline **stage**. Feeding an entire batch at once leaves GPU 2 waiting while GPU 1 works, so GPipe (Huang et al., 2019) slices the batch into $M$ smaller **micro-batches** that stream through the stages back-to-back: as soon as Stage 1 hands off micro-batch 1, it starts on micro-batch 2.

Even so, a **pipeline bubble** (idle time) remains at the start and end of each batch. Counting in time steps—where one step is the time for a stage to process one micro-batch—across $P$ stages: the final micro-batch enters Stage 1 at step $M$ and finishes Stage $P$ at step $M + P - 1$. Thus the forward pass spans $M + P - 1$ steps, during which each stage is busy for $M$ steps and idle for $P - 1$ steps:

$$
\text{bubble} = \frac{P - 1}{M + P - 1}
$$

The backward pass has the exact same ratio. For $P = 4$: $M = 4$ gives an idle fraction of $3/7 \approx 43\%$, while $M = 16$ drops it to $3/19 \approx 16\%$. GPipe notes that bubble overhead becomes negligible when $M \ge 4P$. Because stages only pass boundary activations, pipeline parallelism tolerates slower interconnects than tensor parallelism.

**ZeRO and FSDP.** Standard data parallelism is wasteful: $N_d$ GPUs store $N_d$ identical copies of the model state. ZeRO **shards** those states across the data-parallel GPUs in three progressive stages. Using the paper's example with $\Psi = 7.5$ billion and $N_d = 64$:

| Strategy | Bytes per GPU | Example ($\Psi = 7.5\text{B}, N_d = 64$) |
|---|---|---|
| Standard data parallelism | $(2 + 2 + 12)\Psi$ | 120 GB |
| ZeRO stage 1: shard optimizer states | $\left(2 + 2 + \tfrac{12}{N_d}\right)\Psi$ | 31.4 GB |
| ZeRO stage 2: also shard gradients | $\left(2 + \tfrac{2 + 12}{N_d}\right)\Psi$ | 16.6 GB |
| ZeRO stage 3: also shard parameters | $\tfrac{(2 + 2 + 12)\Psi}{N_d}$ | 1.9 GB |

In Stage 3, whenever a GPU needs a layer's weights, it gathers the shards from peers, runs the layer, and immediately frees the non-local shards; according to the paper, this increases communication volume by only about $1.5\times$ compared to standard data parallelism. Because each GPU still processes its own data batch, this remains a form of data parallelism. PyTorch's native implementation of this idea is **FSDP** (Fully Sharded Data Parallel).

**Putting it all together: Llama 3.** Training the 405B model combined four dimensions of parallelism simultaneously: tensor parallelism, **context parallelism** (splitting very long input sequences across GPUs), pipeline parallelism, and data parallelism (FSDP), nested from innermost to outermost in that order. Tensor parallelism sits on the innermost ring over the fastest intra-node links, while FSDP sits on the outermost ring where latency tolerance is highest.

:::note[Key takeaway]
RoPE rotates $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ by position, so the attention score $\mathbf{q}^\top \mathbf{R}((n - m)\theta)\,\mathbf{k}$ depends on position only through the relative offset. GQA shrinks the KV cache. Data parallelism splits the batch, tensor parallelism splits matrices within a layer, pipeline parallelism splits layers across stages, and ZeRO/FSDP shards model states across data-parallel workers.
:::

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Thinking RoPE adds position to embeddings, or rotates values too]
RoPE never touches the input token embeddings. It rotates $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ inside every attention layer while leaving $\mathbf{v}$ untouched. Nor does the attention score "depend only on distance": it still depends on the content of $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$, with only the positional interaction being relative. When implementing RoPE yourself, the per-head dimension $d$ must be even, and the coordinate pairing convention must match the checkpoint you load.
:::

:::caution[Thinking RoPE automatically extrapolates to arbitrary context lengths]
Although the formula can compute rotation angles for any position index, a model only learns to handle the relative offsets it actually saw during training; on sequences much longer than the training window, quality degrades rapidly. Llama 3 set its RoPE base frequency to $500\,000$ from the start and still needed a six-stage continued pre-training schedule to extend context from 8K to 128K tokens.
:::

:::caution[Assuming more GPUs automatically fit the model and scale speed linearly]
Standard data parallelism does not help a giant model fit in memory, because every GPU holds a full copy of the model; you must shard the model itself (tensor or pipeline parallelism) or shard the states (ZeRO/FSDP). Doubling the GPU count does not double training speed either: according to Table 4 of the Llama 3 paper, scaling from $8\,192$ to $16\,384$ GPUs dropped per-GPU compute throughput from 430 to 400 TFLOPs, yielding a $1.86\times$ speedup rather than $2\times$. Even at $8\,192$ GPUs, over half of theoretical peak FLOPs go unused due to communication overhead, pipeline bubbles, and non-matmul operations.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- The Llama family refines GPT with four changes: LLaMA 1 introduced RMSNorm, SwiGLU (hidden size roughly $\tfrac{2}{3} \cdot 4D$), and RoPE; Llama 2 added GQA. From Llama 1 to Llama 3, the core block architecture barely changed—progress came primarily from data quality and training scale.
- RoPE rotates the $i$-th coordinate pair of $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ by angle $m\theta_i$. Because $\mathbf{R}(m\theta)^\top \mathbf{R}(n\theta) = \mathbf{R}((n - m)\theta)$, attention scores depend on position only through the relative offset.
- The KV cache per token costs $2 \cdot N_{\text{layer}} \cdot H_{\text{kv}} \cdot d \cdot s$ bytes. GQA reduces $H_{\text{kv}}$ while keeping the full number of query heads: Llama 3 8B needs 1 GiB for $8\,192$ tokens instead of 4 GiB.
- Data parallelism splits the batch, tensor parallelism splits matrices within a layer (requiring fast intra-node links), pipeline parallelism splits layers across stages (with bubble fraction $\frac{P-1}{M+P-1}$), and ZeRO/FSDP shards model states across data-parallel GPUs.
