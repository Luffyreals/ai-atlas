## Intuition

### Who, where, and which organization

Read the following Vietnamese sentence:

> Ông Nguyễn Văn An đến Hà Nội làm việc cho Vietcombank từ tháng 3/2024.
> *(Mr. Nguyễn Văn An came to Hanoi to work for Vietcombank starting in March 2024.)*

You can spot the key pieces right away: `"Nguyễn Văn An"` is a **person**, `"Hà Nội"` is a **location**, `"Vietcombank"` is an **organization**, and `"tháng 3/2024"` is a **date**. Phrases that name specific real-world objects like these are called **named entities**. Finding them—with the exact boundaries and the right category—is the job of NER.

This task sits underneath a huge range of applications. News sites tag people and companies in every article. Banks extract applicant names and addresses from forms. Question-answering systems use entity types to know that a *"who"* question expects a person as its answer.

### Is looking up a name dictionary enough?

The first idea everyone thinks of is to compile lists of person names, place names, and company names, then match them against the text. Such a list is called a **gazetteer**. Gazetteers are useful, but they break down in three ways:

- **Same string, different entity type.** In `"đến Hà Nội"` ("came to Hanoi"), `"Hà Nội"` is a location. In `"Chị Mai chuyển từ Bia Hà Nội sang Vinamilk"` ("Ms. Mai moved from Hanoi Beer to Vinamilk"), `"Hà Nội"` is part of the company name `"Bia Hà Nội"`. A static lookup list ignores context.
- **Names that collide with common words.** Many Vietnamese given names are also everyday words: *Mai* ("tomorrow"), *Lan* ("orchid"), *Hoa* ("flower"), *An* ("peace"), *Hải* ("sea"). `"Chị Mai"` ("Ms. Mai") is a person, whereas `"sáng mai"` ("tomorrow morning") is not. `"Ông An"` ("Mr. An") is a person, whereas `"bình an"` ("peaceful") is not.
- **New names appear every day.** New startups, newly famous people, new product lines—a static dictionary is always out of date.

The real clue lies in the **surrounding context**: honorifics like `"Ông"` or `"Chị"` right before a name, verbs like `"làm việc cho"` ("works for"), or prepositions like `"đến"` ("to"). A model needs to read the whole sentence to decide for each word.

### One tag per word

If we attach a tag to every word indicating which entity it belongs to, we can read the entities right back out. However, an entity can span multiple words, and two entities of the same type can sit right next to each other—as in the sports headline `"Trận Việt Nam Thái Lan"` ("The Vietnam–Thailand match"). Our tags must carry enough information to tell **where each entity begins**.

The problem of this lesson is: **assign a tag to every word in a sentence so that we can recover all entities with their exact boundaries and types, and then measure how many entities the model truly found.**

## Theory

### Entity types

Every NER dataset defines its own inventory of entity types. The most widely used English benchmark is **CoNLL-2003** (Tjong Kim Sang and De Meulder, 2003), built from Reuters news stories with four types:

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| `PER` | Person | Nguyễn Văn An, Angela Merkel |
| `LOC` | Location | Hà Nội, Mekong River |
| `ORG` | Organization | Vietcombank, United Nations |
| `MISC` | Miscellaneous proper name | World Cup, nationalities such as German |

The English portion of CoNLL-2003 contains roughly 203,000 training tokens and 23,500 annotated entities. For Vietnamese, two landmark shared tasks established standard benchmarks (Nguyễn Thị Minh Huyền et al., 2018): **VLSP 2016** (CoNLL-style column format with pre-segmented words) and **VLSP 2018** (raw text with XML tags, including nested entities). Both use `PER`, `LOC`, and `ORG` as their core types alongside a smaller `MISC` category.

What about `"tháng 3/2024"`? The four CoNLL types do not cover dates or numbers. Other corpora, such as OntoNotes, add `DATE`, `TIME`, `MONEY`, and more. Choosing an entity inventory is a design decision driven by your application; this lesson uses `PER`, `LOC`, and `ORG` across all examples.

### NER as sequence labeling

Let a sentence be a sequence of words $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_L)$ of length $L$. NER assigns a tag $y_i$ to each word $x_i$, producing a tag sequence $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_L)$ of the exact same length. This setup is called **sequence labeling**: the output has the same length as the input and aligns with it one-to-one. Part-of-speech tagging (noun, verb, adjective, …) has the exact same structure, unlike machine translation in [Seq2Seq](/en/llm/seq2seq/), where input and output lengths and word orders differ.

### BIO tagging

The most naive tagging scheme labels each word directly with its entity type: `PER`, `LOC`, or `O` (*outside* any entity). Try that on our sports headline:

| Word | Trận | Việt | Nam | Thái | Lan |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Type only | O | LOC | LOC | LOC | LOC |
| BIO | O | B-LOC | I-LOC | B-LOC | I-LOC |

Here country names are labeled as locations (some annotation guidelines label national sports teams as `ORG`; either way, the two adjacent names share the same type). The "Type only" row cannot distinguish two separate entities `"Việt Nam"` + `"Thái Lan"` from a single four-word entity `"Việt Nam Thái Lan"`. What was lost is **where each entity starts**.

**BIO tagging** fixes this by pairing a prefix with the entity type:

- `B-X` (*begin*): the first word of an entity of type `X`.
- `I-X` (*inside*): a subsequent word of the currently open entity of type `X`.
- `O` (*outside*): not part of any entity.

With $K$ entity types, the tag set has $2K + 1$ tags. CoNLL-2003 has $K = 4$, giving $2(4) + 1 = 9$ tags. The scheme originated in base noun-phrase chunking (Ramshaw and Marcus, 1995). Two historical variants are worth distinguishing: in **IOB1** (used in the original CoNLL-2003 data files), every word inside an entity takes `I-`, and `B-` is used *only* on the first word of the second entity when two same-type entities touch; in **IOB2** (used in this lesson and almost all modern libraries), every entity starts with `B-`. Today, "BIO" almost always means IOB2.

A more expressive variant is **BIOES** (also called IOBES), which adds `E-X` for the *end* word of a multi-word entity and `S-X` for a *single-word* entity: `"Nguyễn Văn An"` becomes `B-PER I-PER E-PER`, and `"Vietcombank"` becomes `S-ORG`. Lample et al. (2016) used BIOES but noted that it did not significantly outperform BIO in their experiments.

**Decoding tags back into spans:** scan the tag sequence from left to right. A `B-X` tag opens a new entity of type `X`. An `I-X` tag extends the currently open entity if its type is also `X`. An `O` tag—or an `I-` tag of a different type—closes the open entity. One edge case occurs in model predictions even though it never appears in valid IOB2 gold data: an `I-X` tag that does not immediately follow `B-X` or `I-X` (for example, right at the start of a sentence, after `O`, or after a different type). We call this an **orphan `I-`**. This lesson follows the convention of the official CoNLL evaluation script `conlleval` and the default mode of the Python library `seqeval`: an orphan `I-X` **opens a new entity of type `X`**. By contrast, `seqeval`'s strict mode rejects orphan `I-` tags. Because the two rules can produce different F1 scores, always state which convention you use.

### From hand-crafted features to CRFs

Before deep learning, a typical NER system described each word with hand-engineered **features**: is the word capitalized, does it contain digits, does it appear in a gazetteer, what is the previous word (`"Ông"`, `"tại"`, `"công ty"`), what is the next word? A statistical model then learned which features signal which tags.

If you classify **each word independently**, the model can output illegal tag transitions such as `O I-PER` or `B-PER I-LOC` because it has no visibility into neighboring tag decisions. Earlier sequence models such as HMMs and MEMMs scored whole tag sequences, but HMMs required generative independence assumptions while MEMMs suffered from the label bias problem. The linear-chain **conditional random field** (CRF) of Lafferty, McCallum, and Pereira (2001) scores the **entire tag sequence** jointly while avoiding both pitfalls. For NER, the score of a tag sequence $\mathbf{y}$ given input $\mathbf{x}$ is the sum of two terms:

$$
s(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \sum_{i=1}^{L} \psi_i(y_i) + \sum_{i=2}^{L} A_{y_{i-1}, y_i}
$$

- $\psi_i(y_i)$ is the **emission (local) score**: how compatible word $i$ (and its surrounding input context) is with tag $y_i$.
- $A_{y_{i-1}, y_i}$ is the **transition score** from a learned matrix $\mathbf{A}$, with one entry per `(previous tag, current tag)` pair. When trained on IOB2 data, the entry for `(O, I-PER)` learns a strongly negative value because `I-PER` never follows `O` in the training set.

The sequence score is converted into a probability via a softmax over **all** $K'^L$ possible tag sequences $\mathbf{y}'$:

$$
p(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\exp s(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})}{\sum_{\mathbf{y}'} \exp s(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}')}
$$

Although the number of candidate tag sequences grows exponentially with sentence length $L$ ($9^{20}$ sequences for a 20-word sentence with 9 tags), the Markov structure—where transitions depend only on adjacent tags $(y_{i-1}, y_i)$—allows exact dynamic programming in $O(L K'^2)$ time. During training, the denominator is computed with the **forward algorithm**; at inference time, the highest-scoring sequence is found with the **Viterbi algorithm**. To forbid a transition outright, you can set its entry in $\mathbf{A}$ to $-\infty$ so that $\exp s = 0$ for any path using it.

In 2016, Lample et al. replaced hand-crafted features with a **BiLSTM** (a bidirectional recurrent network reading the sentence in both directions) to compute $\psi_i$, while keeping the CRF layer on top. Their **BiLSTM-CRF** model reached 90.94 F1 on English CoNLL-2003 without any gazetteers.

### Token classification with BERT

With [BERT](/en/nlp/bert/), the architecture becomes simpler still. Feed the sentence into BERT and you get a contextual vector $\mathbf{h}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$ for **every** token $i$, where $D$ is the hidden size and self-attention has already mixed information from the entire sentence in both directions. (Note: in this section, the index $i$ runs over BERT's subword tokens rather than whitespace words; the next section explains how to bridge the two.) On top of $\mathbf{h}_i$, place a single shared linear layer:

$$
\mathbf{z}_i = \mathbf{W} \mathbf{h}_i + \mathbf{b}, \qquad
p(y_i = k \mid \mathbf{x}) = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_i)_k
$$

Here $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{K' \times D}$ and $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{K'}$ are the newly added parameters for $K' = 2K + 1$ BIO tags, producing a logit vector $\mathbf{z}_i$ at each position. This setup—an independent classifier on top of each token vector—is called **token classification**. During fine-tuning, both BERT and the linear head are updated together by minimizing the average cross-entropy over the set of labeled positions $\mathcal{M}$:

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \ln p(y_i \mid \mathbf{x})
$$

Devlin et al. (2019) used this exact linear head **without** a CRF and achieved 92.4 F1 (BERT-base) and 92.8 F1 (BERT-large) on the CoNLL-2003 test set. Why is a CRF less critical here? Because each $\mathbf{h}_i$ already attends to the entire sentence, indirectly informing position $i$ about what tags its neighbors are likely to take and making `O I-PER` mistakes much rarer—rarer, though not impossible, as our second code cell will show.

### Aligning labels with subwords

NER datasets annotate labels at the **word** level, whereas BERT operates on **subwords**. As covered in [Tokenization](/en/nlp/tokenization/), WordPiece splits unfamiliar words into multiple subword pieces, prefixing continuation pieces with `##`. Here is how the tokenizer of the BERT model in our code cell splits `"Nguyen Thi Lan works for Vietcombank"` (taken from an actual run):

| Token | `[CLS]` | `Ng` | `##uy` | `##en` | `T` | `##hi` | `La` | `##n` | `works` | `for` | `V` | `##iet` | `##comb` | `##an` | `##k` | `[SEP]` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Word index | – | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | – |
| Label | -100 | B-PER | -100 | -100 | I-PER | -100 | I-PER | -100 | O | O | B-ORG | -100 | -100 | -100 | -100 | -100 |

Six words become 16 tokens. Following original BERT and the standard Hugging Face token-classification recipe, **only the first subword of each word receives the word's BIO tag**. All continuation subwords (`##...`) and special tokens (`[CLS]`, `[SEP]`) are assigned `-100`. Why `-100`? Because PyTorch's `CrossEntropyLoss` has an `ignore_index` parameter whose default value is `-100`: any position carrying `-100` is excluded from $\mathcal{M}$ and contributes zero to the loss and gradients.

Why not copy the word's label onto all of its subwords?

- **Copying `B-ORG` verbatim** onto all 5 pieces of `"Vietcombank"` means every piece starts a new entity at decoding time, turning one company name into five separate entities.
- **Copying and converting continuation pieces to `I-ORG`** produces valid BIO sequences, but a 5-piece word now contributes 5 terms to the loss while `"for"` contributes only 1. Rare proper nouns—the exact words NER cares about most—get chopped into the most pieces, skewing the training loss relative to word-level evaluation.
- **Evaluation is word-level.** We need exactly one tag prediction per word, and the first subword is the natural place to read it.

Keep one consequence in mind: if a model was trained with `-100` on continuation pieces, its predictions on `##` subwords **were never supervised during training**. At inference time, read the tag from the first subword of each word.

:::note[Key takeaway]
NER assigns a BIO tag to each word. BERT does this with a shared linear layer on top of each token's contextual vector. Place each word's label on its first subword, and set continuation subwords and special tokens to `-100` so the cross-entropy loss ignores them.
:::

### Entity-level evaluation

A predicted entity counts as correct only on an **exact match** with a gold entity: identical start word, identical end word, and identical entity type. This is the official scoring rule of CoNLL-2003 and `seqeval`. Let $N_{\text{pred}}$ be the number of predicted entities, $N_{\text{gold}}$ the number of gold entities, and $N_{\text{ok}}$ the number of exact matches:

$$
P = \frac{N_{\text{ok}}}{N_{\text{pred}}}, \qquad
R = \frac{N_{\text{ok}}}{N_{\text{gold}}}, \qquad
F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P + R}
$$

**Precision** $P$ asks: among the entities the model predicted, what fraction is completely right? **Recall** $R$ asks: among the true entities in the text, what fraction did the model find? $F_1$ is their harmonic mean. Substituting $P$ and $R$ and canceling $N_{\text{ok}}$ gives a handy shortcut formula:

$$
F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \frac{N_{\text{ok}}}{N_{\text{pred}}} \cdot \frac{N_{\text{ok}}}{N_{\text{gold}}}}{\frac{N_{\text{ok}}}{N_{\text{pred}}} + \frac{N_{\text{ok}}}{N_{\text{gold}}}}
= \frac{2 N_{\text{ok}}}{N_{\text{pred}} + N_{\text{gold}}}
$$

(Multiply numerator and denominator by $N_{\text{pred}} N_{\text{gold}} / N_{\text{ok}}$: the numerator becomes $2 N_{\text{ok}}$ and the denominator becomes $N_{\text{gold}} + N_{\text{pred}}$.)

Exact-match scoring is unforgiving. If the model predicts `"Văn An"` instead of `"Nguyễn Văn An"`, it is penalized **twice**: once as a false-positive prediction (hurting $P$) and once as a missed gold entity (hurting $R$). At the token level, that same mistake is only 1 or 2 wrong tags out of 14. Worse still, most tokens in running text are `O`, so a dummy model that predicts `O` everywhere achieves high token accuracy while finding zero entities. Our first code cell computes both metrics side by side.

### Challenges in Vietnamese

- **Syllables vs. words.** Written Vietnamese puts spaces between syllables: `"Hà Nội"` is two syllables forming one word. Many Vietnamese datasets and models (such as PhoBERT by Nguyen and Nguyen, 2020) operate on **word-segmented** text where `"Hà_Nội"` is joined by an underscore. Any segmentation error cascades directly into an entity boundary error. For simplicity, the examples in this lesson tag at the syllable level.
- **Capitalization is less reliable.** Vietnamese capitalizes the first syllable of a sentence, honorifics and titles (`"Ông"`, `"Chủ tịch"`), and many non-entities. Conversely, social media text often omits both capitalization and diacritics (`"di ha noi voi lan"`), causing models trained on formal news text to drop sharply in recall.
- **Ambiguity with common words**, as seen in the Intuition section (`"Mai"`, `"An"`), which becomes even harder when diacritics are stripped.

### Limitations

- **Nested entities.** `"Đại học Quốc gia Hà Nội"` ("Vietnam National University, Hanoi") is an `ORG` containing a `LOC` (`"Hà Nội"`). Flat BIO tagging can only represent one layer, so CoNLL-style corpora annotate only the outermost span.
- **Domain shift.** A model trained on Reuters news does not know drug names or electronic part numbers. Moving to a new domain requires new labeled data; see the third caution box under Common pitfalls.
- **Recognition is not linking.** NER spots the string `"Vietcombank"`, but does not link it to a unique identifier in a knowledge base. Resolving mentions to canonical entities is a separate task called **entity linking**.

- **Sentence 4** (missing `B-`): all three gold entities are missed (the two adjacent country names merge into `"Việt Nam Thái Lan"`, and `"Mỹ Đình"` is missed entirely).

Across all four sentences there are 11 gold entities, 9 predicted entities, and 5 exact matches. Thus $P = 5/9 \approx 0.556$, $R = 5/11 \approx 0.455$, and $F_1 = 2 \cdot 5 / (9 + 11) = 0.500$, matching the `entity` line. Meanwhile, token-level accuracy is $34/43 \approx 0.791$: it sounds as if the model is nearly 80% accurate, even though it misses more than half the entities. The final line shows a dummy model that predicts `O` for every token: it still gets `0.488` token accuracy while finding zero entities. These four toy sentences are packed with names; in typical news or web text where `O` tokens make up 85–90% of the corpus, the gap between token accuracy and entity F1 is even wider.

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Reporting token accuracy instead of entity-level F1]
Most tokens in natural text are `O`, and a boundary error only flips one or two tokens. In our first code cell, token accuracy is 0.791 while entity F1 is only 0.500, and predicting `O` everywhere still scores 0.488 accuracy. Always evaluate NER with exact-match entity-level precision, recall, and F1 using `seqeval` or `conlleval`, and state whether orphan `I-` tags use default (`conlleval`) or strict mode.
:::

:::caution[Reading predictions on every subword or using `simple` aggregation]
When a model is trained with `-100` on continuation subwords, the tags it emits on `##` pieces are unsupervised noise. Our BERT example showed `##en` receiving `B-PER`, causing `simple` aggregation to slice `"Nguyen Thi Lan"` in half. In Hugging Face pipelines, pass `aggregation_strategy="first"` (or align predictions yourself using `word_ids()` from the fast tokenizer). Always match your inference aggregation rule to how subword labels were aligned during training.
:::

:::caution[Using an English or formal-news NER model on Vietnamese or noisy social text]
`dslim/bert-base-NER` was trained on English Reuters news: it does not know Vietnamese honorifics like `"Ông"` or `"Chị"`, and its English WordPiece vocabulary shatters accented Vietnamese words into tiny pieces. Even a Vietnamese NER model trained on formal newswire will degrade sharply on lowercase, unaccented chat messages. Pick a backbone and training set that match your target language and domain (such as VLSP benchmarks for Vietnamese), and measure entity F1 on a small hand-labeled sample from your own traffic before deploying.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- NER locates phrases naming people, places, organizations, and more with exact boundaries and types. It is formulated as sequence labeling: one tag per word. Gazetteers alone fail because entity types depend on context and names collide with common words.
- BIO tagging uses `B-X`, `I-X`, and `O` ($2K + 1$ tags); the `B-` prefix separates adjacent entities of the same type. Decoding requires choosing a convention for orphan `I-` tags.
- Linear-chain CRFs score the entire tag sequence using emission scores plus a transition matrix, preventing illegal tag transitions; BiLSTM-CRF replaces manual features with a bidirectional recurrent encoder. BERT needs only a linear layer on top of each token vector, placing each word's label on its first subword and `-100` on continuation pieces.
- Evaluate NER with exact-match entity-level F1, $F_1 = 2 N_{\text{ok}} / (N_{\text{pred}} + N_{\text{gold}})$. Token-level accuracy is inflated by the overwhelming majority of `O` tokens.
