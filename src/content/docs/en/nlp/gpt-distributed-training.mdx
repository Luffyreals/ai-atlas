## Intuition

### One model, one job: continue the text

Cover the last word of the line "Trăm năm trong cõi người ..." ("A hundred years in this human ...") and guess what comes next. That is the entire pre-training task of GPT: given the preceding text, predict the next token. No human annotator labels the data. The text serves as its own label, so as much text as you can collect is as much training data as you have.

The surprise of 2018–2020 was how far you could get simply by doing that **at a much larger scale**: more parameters, more text. GPT-2 had 1.5 billion parameters. GPT-3 had 175 billion, trained on 300 billion tokens.

### A single GPU is not enough

Let us do the arithmetic for the largest GPT-2 model, at 1.5 billion parameters. When training with Adam and mixed precision, each parameter takes 16 bytes (we will count this in the Theory section). Parameters, gradients, and optimizer states alone therefore require $16 \times 1.5 \times 10^9 = 24 \times 10^9$ bytes, or 24 GB. A 24 GB GPU is completely full before storing a single activation (the intermediate layer outputs kept around for the backward pass).

For GPT-3, the paper reports a total training compute of roughly $3.14 \times 10^{23}$ floating-point operations. An NVIDIA A100 GPU peaks at $312 \times 10^{12}$ operations per second in 16-bit precision. Running non-stop at that theoretical peak, a single A100 would take about $10^9$ seconds—**nearly 32 years**. In practice it would take even longer, since no GPU sustains 100% peak utilization.

### Splitting the work like splitting a customer survey

Suppose you want the average satisfaction score of 100 customers. Having one person interview all 100 takes too long. Instead, four people each interview 25 customers, compute the average of their own 25, and then meet to average their four numbers. The result **is identical** to the average across all 100 customers—because everyone interviewed the exact same number of people. If one person interviewed 70 customers while the other three interviewed 10 each, a plain average of the four numbers would be skewed: a 10-customer group would carry the same weight as the 70-customer group.

Data parallelism does the exact same thing with gradients. Every GPU holds a copy of the model, computes the gradient on its own slice of the batch, and then the GPUs "meet" to average their gradients. The engineering question is how to average those gradients quickly when each gradient vector weighs gigabytes.

The problem of this lesson is: **what does GPT learn, and why does a single pass learn at every position at once? And when the model and data outgrow a single GPU, how do we split training across many GPUs while keeping the math identical to training on one machine?**

## Theory

### GPT: causal language modeling

Let a text passage be a sequence of tokens $x_1, \dots, x_L$. By the chain rule of probability, $p(x_1, \dots, x_L) = \prod_{t=1}^{L} p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$. GPT models each factor $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ with a stack of Transformer decoder blocks. Because each prediction depends only on the past, this is called a **causal** language model.

The training objective is the mean cross-entropy loss across all positions:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\frac{1}{L} \sum_{t=1}^{L} \ln p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x_{t+1} \mid x_1, \dots, x_t)
$$

Given a chunk of $L + 1$ tokens, the input is $x_1, \dots, x_L$ and the target is $x_2, \dots, x_{L+1}$—the input **shifted left by one position**. The output at position $t$ predicts token $t + 1$.

Why does a single forward pass yield all $L$ loss terms at once? The causal mask guarantees that the hidden representation at position $t$ depends only on $x_1, \dots, x_t$. Position $t$ cannot peek at the answer $x_{t+1}$, so all $L$ predictions are valid training examples simultaneously. A 1,024-token passage provides 1,024 learning signals from one forward pass and one backward pass. (Generating text at inference time is different: only the logits at the final position are used to sample the next token, which is then appended for the next step. See [Building Your First LLM](/en/llm/build-first-llm/) for a line-by-line implementation.)

### From GPT-1 to GPT-3

| Model | Year | Size | Training data | Key shift |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-1 (Radford et al.) | 2018 | 12 blocks, $D = 768$, context 512 | BooksCorpus, >7,000 books | Pre-train, then fine-tune separately per task |
| GPT-2 (Radford et al.) | 2019 | up to 1.5B params, context 1,024 | WebText, ~40 GB | Express tasks as text prompts; evaluate zero-shot (no examples) |
| GPT-3 (Brown et al.) | 2020 | 175B params, context 2,048 | 300B tokens | In-context learning: zero-shot, one-shot, few-shot with no weight updates |

The architecture barely changed across the three generations. What changed was scale—and how the model is used: from task-specific fine-tuning to specifying the task directly inside the prompt. [The GPT Family and Multi-Task Fine-Tuning](/en/llm/gpt-family-finetuning/) tells the full story of this progression and the instruction-tuning stage that followed. For GPT-3 specifically, the paper notes (Section 2.3) that to fit the model in memory, training combined model parallelism within each matrix multiply and model parallelism across the network's layers—the two model-parallel strategies we will meet at the end of this section.

**Scaling laws.** Kaplan et al. (2020) trained models across many orders of magnitude and showed that cross-entropy loss decreases as a smooth **power law** with model size, dataset size, and compute budget. They also popularized the standard rule of thumb: training costs roughly $6\Psi$ FLOPs per token, where $\Psi$ is the number of parameters. Checking this on GPT-3 gives $6 \times 175 \times 10^9 \times 300 \times 10^9 \approx 3.15 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs, matching the paper's $3.14 \times 10^{23}$.

Hoffmann et al. (2022) asked the follow-up question: given a fixed compute budget, should you spend it on a larger model or on more training tokens? Their answer was to scale **both in equal proportion**. Their 70B-parameter Chinchilla model trained on 1.4 trillion tokens—roughly 20 tokens per parameter—and outperformed the 280B Gopher model trained with the same compute budget. By that yardstick, GPT-3 (at roughly 1.7 tokens per parameter) was heavily oversized for the amount of data it saw.

**GPT vs. BERT.** BERT ([BERT](/en/nlp/bert/)) is an encoder-only Transformer with bidirectional attention trained to predict masked tokens. Because every position sees both sides, its representations are richer for understanding tasks like classification, sequence labeling, and extractive QA. In exchange, BERT only gets a loss signal from the ~15% of positions that are masked, and cannot naturally generate text from left to right. GPT gets a loss signal at 100% of positions, and left-to-right generation is the exact task it was trained on. A full comparison across all three Transformer families awaits in [Advanced Transformers](/en/nlp/advanced-transformers/).

### Where the memory goes

We counted this table in [The LLaMA Family](/en/llm/llama-family/) and recap it here. Following the accounting in the ZeRO paper (Rajbhandari et al., 2020), training with Adam and mixed precision requires per parameter:

| Stored quantity | Bytes per parameter |
|---|---:|
| 16-bit parameter | 2 |
| 16-bit gradient | 2 |
| FP32 master copy of parameter | 4 |
| Adam first moment / momentum (FP32) | 4 |
| Adam second moment / variance (FP32) | 4 |
| **Total** | **16** |

The three FP32 entries make up the **optimizer state**. A model with $\Psi$ parameters therefore needs $16\Psi$ bytes just for model states, before any activations. For 1.5B GPT-2 that is 24 GB; for 175B GPT-3 it is roughly 2.8 TB—requiring at least thirty-five 80 GB GPUs just to hold the weights and optimizer.

Notice there are two distinct bottlenecks: **training is too slow** (one GPU cannot crunch the tokens fast enough) and **the model does not fit** (one GPU cannot hold the bytes). Data parallelism solves the first problem. The second requires sharding the model itself, covered at the end of this section.

### Data parallelism: the mean of shard gradients equals the full-batch gradient

Suppose we have $N$ GPUs, which we call $N$ workers indexed by $k = 1, \dots, N$. Every worker holds **a complete, identical copy** of the parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. A batch of $m$ samples is partitioned into $N$ equal **shards** $S_1, \dots, S_N$, each containing $B = m/N$ samples. (For GPT, one sample is one text chunk.)

Let $\ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ be the loss on sample $i$. The full-batch loss is the average:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})
$$

Worker $k$ only sees its own shard $S_k$. It computes the mean loss on that shard and the corresponding gradient:

$$
\mathcal{L}_k = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i \in S_k} \ell_i, \qquad \mathbf{g}_k = \nabla \mathcal{L}_k = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i \in S_k} \nabla \ell_i
$$

We want to show that the average of the worker gradients $\mathbf{g}_k$ equals the full-batch gradient $\nabla \mathcal{L}$.

**Step 1: the gradient of a sum is the sum of the gradients.** By linearity of differentiation:

$$
\nabla \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \nabla \ell_i
$$

**Step 2: group the sum by shard.** Because every sample belongs to exactly one shard, the sum over all $m$ samples splits into $N$ partial sums:

$$
\nabla \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \sum_{i \in S_k} \nabla \ell_i
$$

**Step 3: substitute each worker's gradient.** From the definition of $\mathbf{g}_k$, the inner sum $\sum_{i \in S_k} \nabla \ell_i$ equals $B\,\mathbf{g}_k$. Substituting this in and using $B/m = 1/N$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla \mathcal{L} &= \frac{1}{m} \sum_{k=1}^{N} B\,\mathbf{g}_k \\
&= \frac{B}{m} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbf{g}_k \\
&= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbf{g}_k
\end{aligned}
$$

So the workers only need to communicate so that every worker obtains the **average** of the $N$ gradients $\mathbf{g}_k$. Then every worker runs the exact same Adam step starting from the exact same parameters with the exact same gradient, keeping all $N$ copies in lockstep. Up to floating-point rounding, the update is identical to a single machine processing all $m$ samples at once.

**When the shards are uneven**, let $B_k$ be the number of samples on worker $k$. Step 3 gives $\sum_{i \in S_k} \nabla \ell_i = B_k\,\mathbf{g}_k$, so:

$$
\nabla \mathcal{L} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{B_k}{m}\,\mathbf{g}_k
$$

This is a **weighted** average, weighted by each shard's share of the batch. The plain average $\frac{1}{N}\sum_k \mathbf{g}_k$ is only exact when all $B_k$ are equal, just like the customer survey in the Intuition section. PyTorch's `DistributedSampler` pads the dataset with a few repeated indices by default so every worker gets the exact same number of samples (and runs the exact same number of steps). With language models there is a further subtlety: if each sample's loss is averaged over its non-padding tokens, and different shards contain different numbers of real tokens, then "equal size" must be weighted by token count rather than sequence count.

### All-reduce and the ring algorithm

The collective operation where every worker ends up with the sum (or average) of all workers' vectors is called **all-reduce**. The naive approach sends every worker's gradient of size $G$ to a central parameter server, sums them there, and broadcasts the result back. The central server must receive $(N-1)G$ bytes and send back $(N-1)G$ bytes: the more workers you add, the worse that single node bottlenecks.

**Ring all-reduce** arranges the $N$ workers in a logical ring where each worker only sends to its right neighbor. Each worker's gradient vector is sliced into $N$ equal chunks of size $G/N$.

1. **Reduce-scatter**, $N - 1$ steps. In each step, every worker simultaneously sends one chunk to its right neighbor, and the receiver adds the incoming chunk into its own matching chunk. As chunks travel around the ring, partial sums accumulate. After $N - 1$ steps, each worker holds the **complete sum** of one distinct chunk.
2. **All-gather**, another $N - 1$ steps. Each worker forwards its fully summed chunk around the ring, and receivers overwrite their local chunk with the incoming one. After $N - 1$ steps, every worker holds the complete sum for all $N$ chunks.

With 3 GPUs and three chunks A, B, C—writing A0 for GPU 0's chunk A, A01 for A0 + A1, and A012 for the full sum:

| Step | GPU 0 sends | GPU 1 sends | GPU 2 sends | State after this step |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reduce-scatter 1 | A0 | B1 | C2 | GPU 1 has A01, GPU 2 has B12, GPU 0 has C20 |
| Reduce-scatter 2 | C20 | A01 | B12 | GPU 0 has B012, GPU 1 has C012, GPU 2 has A012 |
| All-gather 1 | B012 | C012 | A012 | Every GPU has two fully summed chunks |
| All-gather 2 | A012 | B012 | C012 | Every GPU has all three fully summed chunks |

**Counting the bytes sent.** There are $2(N-1)$ steps in total, and in each step a worker sends one chunk of size $G/N$. The total data sent by each worker is:

$$
2(N-1) \cdot \frac{G}{N} = \frac{2(N-1)}{N}\,G
$$

This is always strictly less than $2G$ and **hardly grows at all** as you add more workers: $1.5\,G$ at $N = 4$, and $1.97\,G$ at $N = 64$. Every link in the ring carries traffic simultaneously at every step, with no central bottleneck. Patarasuk and Yuan (2009) proved that this volume is bandwidth-optimal for large buffers. Baidu (Gibiansky, 2017) brought ring all-reduce to deep learning, and Horovod (Sergeev and Del Balso, 2018) popularized it across TensorFlow and PyTorch.

Try the numbers on 1.5B GPT-2, where 16-bit gradients weigh 3 GB. Across 64 GPUs connected by 100 Gb/s links (12.5 GB/s), each GPU sends about $1.97 \times 3 \approx 5.9$ GB, taking roughly 0.47 seconds per step. A central server would have to receive $63 \times 3 = 189$ GB over its link—about 15 seconds (ignoring latency). Because the number of steps $2(N-1)$ does grow linearly with $N$, per-step network latency eventually matters at very large GPU counts; libraries like NCCL switch to hybrid ring/tree algorithms in that regime.

PyTorch's `DistributedDataParallel` (DDP) goes one step further and **hides** communication behind computation: it groups parameters into buckets and launches a bucket's all-reduce as soon as the backward pass finishes computing that bucket's gradients, while earlier layers are still backpropagating (Li et al., 2020).

### Gradient accumulation

A large batch may not fit in a single GPU's memory because activation memory scales linearly with batch size. Our shard derivation above gives an immediate escape hatch without buying more GPUs: split the batch into $A$ **micro-batches** run **sequentially** on the same GPU, accumulate $\frac{1}{A}\mathbf{g}_a$ from each micro-batch, and only step the optimizer once at the end. Mathematically, it is the exact same average as data parallelism, replacing "$N$ workers in parallel" with "$A$ micro-batches in series." The trade-off is wall-clock time: $A$ sequential passes instead of one.

Combining both techniques, the **effective batch size** of a single optimizer step is:

$$
\text{effective batch size} = (\text{micro-batch per GPU}) \times A \times N
$$

In PyTorch, `loss.backward()` **adds** into `.grad` rather than overwriting it, so gradient accumulation is simply calling `backward()` $A$ times (with each micro-batch loss divided by $A$) before calling `optimizer.step()` and `zero_grad()`. When combined with DDP, wrap the first $A - 1$ micro-batches in `with model.no_sync():` so DDP only runs all-reduce on the final micro-batch.

### Mixed precision

Computing in 16-bit floating point runs much faster and halves activation memory on GPUs with Tensor Cores. However, 16-bit numbers have two pitfalls:

- **FP16** (5 exponent bits, 10 mantissa bits) can only represent positive numbers between roughly $6 \times 10^{-8}$ and $65\,504$. A gradient of $10^{-8}$ underflows to 0.
- The gap between 1 and the next representable FP16 number is about $0.001$. Adding a weight update of $10^{-4}$ to a weight of 1.0 rounds right back to 1.0: the update vanishes completely.

Micikevicius et al. (2017; ICLR 2018) showed how to run the forward and backward passes in 16-bit while preserving full-precision accuracy using three techniques that are now standard:

1. **FP32 master weights:** optimizer updates are applied to a 32-bit master copy of the weights so tiny updates are never lost. The 16-bit weights used in the forward and backward passes are cast from the master copy after each step.
2. **Loss scaling:** multiply the loss by a scale factor $s$ (say $s = 1024$) before calling `backward()`. By the chain rule, every gradient is multiplied by $s$ as well, shifting values like $10^{-8}$ up to $\sim 10^{-5}$ where FP16 can represent them cleanly. Before stepping the optimizer, divide the gradients back by $s$ in FP32.
3. **FP32 accumulation:** products of FP16 numbers inside matrix multiplications and dot products are accumulated into an FP32 register before rounding the result back to 16-bit.

While the original paper tested a fixed scale $s$ (noting in its conclusion that $s$ could be adjusted automatically), modern frameworks use dynamic loss scaling: if any gradient overflows (`inf` or `nan`), skip the optimizer step and halve $s$; if many steps pass without overflow, double $s$.

**BF16** (8 exponent bits, 7 mantissa bits) has the exact same exponent range as FP32, so it rarely underflows or overflows and generally needs no loss scaling. In exchange, it has only 2–3 decimal digits of precision, so FP32 master weights are still essential. In PyTorch, 16-bit forward computation is enabled with `torch.autocast`, and dynamic loss scaling for FP16 is handled by `torch.amp.GradScaler`.

### When the model does not fit on a single GPU

Data parallelism copies the **entire** model onto every GPU, so it cannot help when $16\Psi$ bytes exceeds a single GPU's memory. At that point you must partition the model itself or its training states. The three strategies below are covered with full equations and worked examples in [The LLaMA Family](/en/llm/llama-family/); here we summarize the core idea of each:

- **Tensor parallelism** (Megatron-LM; Shoeybi et al., 2019): split **individual weight matrices inside each layer** across GPUs—for instance, each GPU holds half the columns of the first FFN matrix. Because GPUs must synchronize activations inside every single layer, tensor parallelism requires ultra-fast interconnects (typically NVLink within a single node).
- **Pipeline parallelism** (GPipe; Huang et al., 2019): slice the model **by depth**, assigning each GPU a contiguous stage of layers. A batch is split into micro-batches that stream through the stages assembly-line style, leaving a small idle fraction called the **pipeline bubble** while later stages wait at the start and earlier stages wait at the end.
- **ZeRO** (Rajbhandari et al., 2020) and **FSDP** (Fully Sharded Data Parallel in PyTorch): keep the data-parallel execution model, but instead of storing $N$ redundant copies, shard the optimizer states (Stage 1), gradients (Stage 2), and parameters themselves (Stage 3) across the $N$ GPUs. At Stage 3, each GPU holds only $16\Psi/N$ bytes; before a layer runs, GPUs all-gather that layer's parameter shards on the fly and free them right after.

Large training runs combine all three ("3D parallelism"): tensor parallelism within a node, pipeline parallelism across nodes, and data parallelism / FSDP on the outside.

:::note[Key takeaway]
GPT learns $p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$; its causal mask yields a valid loss at all $L$ positions in a single pass, with targets equal to the input shifted by one position. In data parallelism, $\nabla\mathcal{L} = \sum_k \frac{B_k}{m}\mathbf{g}_k$, which simplifies to $\frac{1}{N}\sum_k \mathbf{g}_k$ when shards are equal. Ring all-reduce sends $\frac{2(N-1)}{N}G$ per worker—nearly independent of $N$. Gradient accumulation computes the same average sequentially. Mixed precision computes in 16-bit, keeps FP32 master weights, and scales the loss by $s$ for FP16. When a model outgrows one GPU, shard matrices (tensor), layers (pipeline), or states (ZeRO/FSDP).
:::

### Limitations

- **Bigger batches are not automatically better.** Data parallelism preserves the exact gradient of the combined batch, but adding GPUs increases the effective batch size. Very large batches typically require retuning the learning rate and using **learning-rate warmup**—gradually ramping $\eta$ up over initial steps (see the first box under Common pitfalls).
- **The code example runs 2 processes on CPU.** It demonstrates the exact mechanics of DDP, not multi-GPU throughput. Serving large models across multiple GPUs at inference time is covered in [Deploying LLMs: Multi-GPU and Distributed Serving](/en/llm/llm-deployment/).
- **Architectural improvements after GPT-3** (RoPE, efficient attention, KV caching) are covered in [Advanced Transformers](/en/nlp/advanced-transformers/) and [The LLaMA Family](/en/llm/llama-family/).

| Data parallelism (DDP) | Full model, $1/N$ of the batch | All-reduce gradients, once per optimizer step | Training speed |
| Gradient accumulation | Full model, one micro-batch at a time | Nothing (runs on 1 GPU) | Batch activations exceed memory |
| ZeRO / FSDP | $1/N$ of parameters, gradients, optimizer state | All-gather parameters per layer, reduce-scatter gradients | Model states exceed 1 GPU, while still splitting the batch |
| Tensor parallelism | A slice of every layer's weight matrices | Layer activations, inside every layer | Individual layers too wide |
| Pipeline parallelism | A contiguous group of layers | Boundary activations and gradients between stages | Model too deep for 1 GPU |

### DDP with two processes

The script below runs **real distributed data parallelism** using `torch.distributed` on CPU, with no GPU required:

- `mp.spawn` launches 2 processes, each acting as a worker running `worker(rank)` with `rank` 0 or 1. The `mp.spawn` call must sit inside `if __name__ == "__main__":` because each child process re-imports the script from scratch; without that guard, child processes would recursively spawn more processes and raise a `RuntimeError`.
- `init_process_group("gloo", init_method="tcp://127.0.0.1:29500", ...)` connects the two processes over localhost port 29500. The `gloo` backend runs on CPU across Linux, macOS, and Windows; on NVIDIA GPUs you would use `nccl`.
- The dataset is the opening 4 lines of *The Tale of Kieu* (130 characters), sliced into 114 overlapping windows of 17 characters. The model is a tiny character-level GPT: token + position embeddings, one causally masked Transformer block, and a linear output head. `forward` takes a 17-character window, uses the first 16 characters as input and the last 16 characters as shifted targets, and returns the cross-entropy loss.
- `DistributedSampler` splits the 114 windows into two halves of 57 windows per process, reshuffling differently each epoch via `set_epoch`.
- Each process **deliberately** initializes with a different random seed (`torch.manual_seed(rank)`). When `DDP(...)` wraps the model, it broadcasts rank 0's initial parameters to all other ranks so both start identically. During `loss.backward()`, DDP all-reduces and averages the gradients.
- After 30 epochs, `all_gather` collects both processes' flattened parameter vectors to verify they stayed identical.

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Adding GPUs without adjusting the learning rate]
With DDP, adding GPUs multiplies the effective batch size: 8 GPUs with 32 samples each process 256 samples per step, not 32. Each epoch therefore runs only $1/N$ as many optimizer steps as on a single GPU, so keeping the learning rate $\eta$ unchanged slows down progress per sample seen. Because a larger batch has lower gradient variance, you can take larger steps—i.e., increase $\eta$. Goyal et al. (2017) popularized the linear scaling rule (scaling $\eta$ proportionally to effective batch size, paired with several epochs of warmup) to train ResNet-50 with a batch size of 8,192 images, though linear scaling eventually breaks down at extreme batch sizes. Always log your effective batch size ($\text{micro-batch} \times A \times N$) alongside $\eta$ when comparing runs.
:::

:::caution[Accumulating gradients without dividing the loss]
`loss.backward()` adds into `.grad`. If you accumulate across 4 micro-batches without dividing each micro-batch loss by 4, the accumulated gradient is $4\times$ too large—silently multiplying your learning rate by 4 (or worse, scaling the gradient norm before clipping). Likewise, when micro-batches contain different numbers of non-padding tokens, averaging the per-micro-batch mean losses falls into the "uneven shards" trap shown in the first NumPy cell. The exact fix is to sum the token losses across all micro-batches and divide by the total number of non-padding tokens in the effective batch.
:::

:::caution[Expecting standard data parallelism to make a large model fit in memory]
Standard data parallelism replicates the full model parameters, gradients, and optimizer state on **every** GPU. A model that needs 112 GB of state on 1 GPU still needs 112 GB on each of 8 GPUs. To reduce per-GPU state memory, use ZeRO/FSDP, tensor parallelism, or pipeline parallelism. Also remember to call `sampler.set_epoch(epoch)` at the start of every epoch: without it, `DistributedSampler` uses the exact same shuffle order on every epoch.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- GPT is a decoder-only Transformer trained to model $p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$. Targets are the input shifted by one position, and the causal mask yields a valid loss at every position in a single pass. GPT-1, GPT-2, and GPT-3 kept the same architecture while scaling up; scaling laws show loss follows a smooth power law, with Chinchilla recommending roughly 20 training tokens per parameter.
- Training with Adam and mixed precision requires $16\Psi$ bytes of model state before activations, creating two distinct challenges: training speed and memory capacity.
- In data parallelism, every GPU holds a copy of the model and processes one shard of the batch; $\nabla\mathcal{L} = \sum_k \frac{B_k}{m}\mathbf{g}_k$, which equals the plain average when shards are equal. Ring all-reduce sends $\frac{2(N-1)}{N}G$ bytes per GPU for gradient size $G$. Gradient accumulation computes the same average sequentially on one GPU.
- Mixed precision runs forward/backward passes in 16-bit, updates FP32 master weights, and scales the loss by $s$ for FP16. When the model itself exceeds single-GPU memory, shard weight matrices (tensor parallelism), layer stages (pipeline parallelism), or model states (ZeRO/FSDP).
