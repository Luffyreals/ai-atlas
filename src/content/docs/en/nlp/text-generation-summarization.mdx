## Intuition

### A highlighter pen or rewriting from scratch

Suppose you read a seven-sentence morning news brief: Da Nang launches its first electric bus route, how long the route is, the ticket price, free rides during the first month, how far each bus travels per charge, what residents say about it, and how many more routes are planned for next year. Your manager asks you to condense it into two sentences. You have two ways to do it.

**Method 1: use a highlighter pen.** Highlight the two most important sentences and copy them verbatim. You can never invent a false claim that the article did not make, because every word comes straight from the article. However, two sentences lifted out of context can feel choppy: if you copy `"Tuyến dài 18 km"` ("The route is 18 km long") on its own, the reader has no idea which route "the route" refers to.

**Method 2: rewrite in your own words.** *"Da Nang has opened its first electric bus route linking the airport to My Khe beach. Rides are free for the first month, with three more routes planned next year."* That is tighter and smoother. But now you are **writing**, which means you can introduce mistakes: accidentally turning *"first"* into *"second"*, or slipping in a detail the article never mentioned.

A neural model performs Method 2 by **generating text** one token at a time. At every step, the model outputs a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary, and we must **choose** one token. Should we always pick the single most likely token, or draw at random for variety? When summarizing a contract, we want accuracy and stability. When writing a story, we want surprise.

One more question: when you have a thousand machine-generated summaries to evaluate and not enough human readers, how do you score them automatically?

The problem of this lesson is: **how to choose tokens when generating text, whether to select existing sentences or rewrite from scratch when summarizing, and how to score a summary automatically without being fooled.**

## Theory

### Text generation as an autoregressive loop

As seen in [GPT and Distributed Training](/en/nlp/gpt-distributed-training/), a causal language model learns $p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$: the probability distribution of the next token given all preceding tokens. An encoder–decoder model such as T5 learns $p(y_i \mid y_{<i}, \mathbf{x})$, conditioning on a source text $\mathbf{x}$ as well ([Sequence-to-Sequence Models](/en/llm/seq2seq/)). Both generate text through the exact same **autoregressive** loop:

1. Feed the tokens generated so far into the model to obtain a logit $z_i$ for every token $i$ in the vocabulary.
2. Convert the logits into a probability distribution and **select** one token according to a decoding rule.
3. Append the chosen token to the sequence and repeat from Step 1 until `<EOS>` is produced or the maximum token limit is reached.

The neural network only performs Step 1. A **decoding strategy** is the rule used in Step 2. With the exact same model weights, changing the decoding rule can completely change the generated text.

### Decoding strategies side by side

The table below compares the main decoding strategies. The exact formulas and hand calculations for temperature, top-k, and top-p were introduced in [Using Text APIs](/en/llm/text-api/), and beam search with length penalty was covered in [Sequence-to-Sequence Models](/en/llm/seq2seq/). Here we put them side by side.

| Strategy | How the next token is chosen | `generate` parameters | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Greedy decoding | Pick the single highest-probability token | `num_beams=1`, `do_sample=False` | Fast, deterministic | Cannot revise early choices; prone to repetition in long text |
| Beam search | Maintain the $B$ partial sequences with the highest total log-probability | `num_beams`, `length_penalty` | Finds higher-probability sequences than greedy | Bland, repetitive in open-ended generation; costs $B\times$ compute |
| Pure sampling | Draw a token directly from the model's full distribution | `do_sample=True`, `top_k=0` | Maximum diversity | Occasionally samples nonsensical tokens from the long tail |
| Temperature $T$ | Divide logits by $T$ before softmax, then sample | `temperature` | Single knob trading off sharpness vs. diversity | Does not truncate the tail on its own |
| Top-k sampling | Sample only from the $k$ most likely tokens | `top_k` | Cuts off the long tail | Fixed $k$ cannot adapt when the distribution is very peaked or very flat |
| Top-p (nucleus) | Sample from the smallest set of top tokens whose cumulative probability is $\ge p$ | `top_p` | Candidate pool expands and shrinks dynamically with model uncertainty | Still stochastic, harder to reproduce across hardware |

Top-k sampling was popularized for story generation by Fan, Lewis, and Dauphin (2018), who sampled from the top $k = 10$ tokens at each step and found it far more engaging than beam search, which tended to repeat generic phrases. Top-p (nucleus) sampling was proposed by Holtzman et al. (2020) for the reason explained next.

### Neural text degeneration

If a well-trained language model assigns high probability to good text, shouldn't picking the sequence with the highest probability produce the best text? Surprisingly, no. Holtzman et al. (2020) prompted GPT-2 Large to continue 5,000 passages (up to 200 tokens each) and measured what percentage of continuations got stuck in a repetition loop (a phrase repeated at least three times at the end):

| Decoding method | Repetition rate (%) |
|---|---:|
| Human text | 0.28 |
| Greedy decoding | 73.66 |
| Beam search, $B = 16$ | 28.94 |
| Pure sampling | 0.22 |
| Top-p sampling, $p = 0.95$ | 0.36 |

Why does greedy decoding repeat so badly? Holtzman et al. showed that the model's assigned probability for a repeated phrase **increases** with each repetition: once a phrase repeats once, repeating it a third and fourth time becomes even more "probable", creating a positive feedback loop. Human writing does the opposite: it continually branches into lower-probability, higher-information tokens.

Pure sampling virtually eliminates repetition loops, but fails in the opposite direction: the text wanders incoherently and sometimes invents bizarre wording. The culprit is the **unreliable tail** of the softmax distribution: tens of thousands of unlikely tokens whose individual probabilities are tiny, but whose combined probability mass is substantial. Top-p sampling slices off that tail. While pure sampling has an even lower raw repetition rate in the table, top-p produces text closest to human writing across perplexity and HUSE (a combined human-and-statistical evaluation metric).

Holtzman et al. coined the term **neural text degeneration** for both failure modes: dull, repetitive loops when maximizing likelihood, and incoherent rambling when sampling from the unpruned tail.

### Repetition penalty

A direct way to discourage repetition is to penalize tokens that have already appeared. In their paper introducing the CTRL model, Keskar et al. (2019) proposed **penalized sampling**. Let $g$ be the set of tokens already generated. The probability of token $i$ at the next step is:

$$
p_i = \frac{\exp\big(z_i / (T \cdot I(i \in g))\big)}{\sum_j \exp\big(z_j / (T \cdot I(j \in g))\big)},
\qquad
I(c) = \begin{cases} \theta & \text{if } c \text{ is true} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

Reading the formula:

- If token $i$ has **not** appeared yet: $I = 1$, and its logit is simply divided by temperature $T$ as usual.
- If token $i$ **has** already appeared: its logit is divided by an extra factor $\theta$. For $\theta > 1$ and a positive logit $z_i > 0$, this shrinks the logit and lowers the token's probability.
- $\theta = 1$ disables the penalty. Keskar et al. found that near-greedy decoding with $\theta \approx 1.2$ struck a good balance between coherence and non-repetition.

**A trap with negative logits.** Dividing a negative number by $\theta > 1$ moves it **closer to 0**, making it **larger**: $-1.0 / 1.2 \approx -0.83 > -1.0$. That would accidentally *boost* a repeated token whenever its raw logit happened to be negative! To fix this, `repetition_penalty` in Hugging Face `transformers` divides positive logits by $\theta$ but **multiplies** negative logits by $\theta$. With $\theta = 1.2$, a logit of $2.0$ becomes $2.0 / 1.2 \approx 1.67$, while a logit of $-1.0$ becomes $-1.0 \times 1.2 = -1.2$. Both move downward as intended.

A harder constraint is `no_repeat_ngram_size=n`, which sets the probability of any previously seen $n$-gram to zero. Keep in mind that both penalties also suppress **legitimate** repetition: a summary about electric buses may genuinely need to mention `"xe buýt điện"` ("electric bus") twice, which `no_repeat_ngram_size=3` forbids.

### Which strategy to use when

Holtzman et al. divide text generation tasks into two broad regimes:

- **Directed generation:** the output is tightly constrained by the input, as in machine translation, summarization, or data-to-text generation. Beam search (or greedy decoding) is the standard choice. Repetition loops are much less common here because the source text anchors the content, while stability and faithfulness to the input are paramount.
- **Open-ended generation:** story continuation, creative writing, chitchat, brainstorming. Truncated sampling (top-p, or top-k with temperature) produces much more natural and varied prose.

Treat this as a starting point rather than a rigid law. For instance, the model configuration file of `t5-small` stores task-specific summarization defaults inside `config.task_specific_params`: beam search with $B = 4$ and `no_repeat_ngram_size=3`. Note that calling `model.generate` in recent versions of `transformers` does not load those task-specific parameters automatically; calling `generate` without extra arguments runs greedy decoding, as shown in the `greedy` row of our T5 code cell below.

:::note[Key takeaway]
The model produces a probability distribution; the decoding strategy chooses the token. Likelihood-maximizing decoding (greedy, beam search) is stable but prone to repetition in long open-ended text. Pure sampling is diverse but wanders into the unreliable tail. Top-p dynamically truncates that tail based on model certainty. Repetition penalty divides positive logits (and multiplies negative logits) of already-seen tokens by $\theta$. Use beam or greedy for summarization and translation; use truncated sampling for creative writing.
:::

### Extractive summarization: picking sentences

Extractive summarization scores each sentence in the source document and selects the top few sentences, **preserving their original order** so the resulting passage reads coherently. Here are three classic ways to score sentences, from simplest to richest:

**Lead baseline (first $k$ sentences).** News articles are traditionally written in an "inverted pyramid" style, placing the most important facts in the opening sentences. Simply taking the first $k$ sentences is therefore a famously tough baseline on news benchmarks—though it fails on genres with a different structure, such as scientific papers, emails, or meeting transcripts.

**TF-IDF centroid similarity.** Treat the $N$ sentences of the document as a mini-corpus. Represent each sentence $j$ by its TF-IDF vector $\mathbf{s}_j$ ([Vector Semantics and Embeddings](/en/nlp/vector-semantics-embeddings/)): the count of term $t$ in the sentence multiplied by $\mathrm{idf}_t = \ln\frac{1 + N}{1 + \mathrm{df}_t} + 1$, where $\mathrm{df}_t$ is the number of sentences containing $t$. Sum all sentence vectors to form a **centroid** vector $\mathbf{c}$ representing the document as a whole, and score each sentence by its cosine similarity to $\mathbf{c}$:

$$
\mathbf{c} = \sum_{j=1}^{N} \mathbf{s}_j, \qquad
\mathrm{score}_j = \cos(\mathbf{s}_j, \mathbf{c}) = \frac{\mathbf{s}_j^\top \mathbf{c}}{\lVert \mathbf{s}_j \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{c} \rVert}
$$

A high-scoring sentence is one that best resembles the document as a whole: it contains topical terms that recur across several sentences without appearing in every single one. Dividing by $\lVert \mathbf{s}_j \rVert$ prevents long sentences from winning automatically.

**TextRank.** Mihalcea and Tarau (2004) turn the document into a graph: each sentence is a node, and two sentences are connected by an edge weighted by the number of words they share, divided by $\log |S_i| + \log |S_j|$ (where $|S_i|$ is the length of sentence $i$) to normalize for length. They then run PageRank (damping factor $d = 0.85$) over this graph. The intuition is **voting**: a sentence is important if it is similar to many other sentences, especially other important ones. On 567 news articles from DUC 2002 (100-word summaries), unsupervised TextRank competed closely with the best supervised systems in the shared task.

Extractive summarization **never invents new sentences** because every sentence is copied verbatim from the source, although lifting a sentence out of context can still mislead a reader. Its downside is inflexibility: it cannot merge facts from two sentences, trim irrelevant clauses from a long sentence, or fix dangling pronouns like `"Tuyến dài 18 km"`.

### Abstractive summarization: rewriting

Abstractive summarization uses a sequence-to-sequence model: an encoder reads the source document, and a decoder generates the summary token by token using one of the decoding strategies above. Training such a model from scratch would require millions of `(article, summary)` pairs; in practice, modern systems pretrain an encoder–decoder with a self-supervised denoising objective on unlabeled text, then fine-tune on summarization datasets:

- **BART** (Lewis et al., 2020) and **T5** (Raffel et al., 2020) are denoising encoder–decoder Transformers covered in [Advanced Transformers](/en/nlp/advanced-transformers/). T5 specifies the task via a text prefix such as `"summarize: "`. Because the released T5 checkpoints were multi-task trained on a mixture that already included CNN/Daily Mail summarization, prepending `"summarize: "` lets `t5-small` summarize English news out of the box.
- **PEGASUS** (Zhang et al., 2020) designed a pretraining objective **tailored specifically for summarization**, called **Gap Sentences Generation** (GSG). During pretraining, the authors remove whole **important sentences** from a document, replace each removed sentence with a mask token `[MASK1]`, and train the decoder to generate the missing sentences from the remaining text. Importance is measured by computing the ROUGE-1 F1 of each sentence against the rest of the document (masking the top 30% of sentences in their main ablation). Because this pretraining task closely mirrors summarization itself, the largest PEGASUS model set new state-of-the-art results across all 12 summarization benchmarks tested, and surpassed prior state-of-the-art on 6 benchmarks with just 1,000 fine-tuning examples.
- **Prompting an instruction-tuned LLM.** A decoder-only LLM can summarize zero-shot when prompted with *"Summarize the following article in two sentences"* ([Prompt Engineering](/en/llm/prompt-engineering/)). This requires no fine-tuning and lets you control length, style, and format in natural language, though compute cost and hallucination risk remain.

For Vietnamese, **ViT5** (Phan et al., 2022) pretrains the T5 architecture on Vietnamese text, achieving strong abstractive summarization results on the Vietnews and WikiLingua benchmarks.

| Criterion | Extractive | Abstractive |
|---|---|---|
| Output | Verbatim sentences from the source | Newly generated sentences; may use words outside the source |
| Factual fabrication | Cannot invent new sentences, though out-of-context quotes can mislead | Can hallucinate distorted or unsupported facts |
| Fluency | Can feel disjointed or leave dangling references | Smooth, compact, able to fuse ideas across sentences |
| Requirements | Unsupervised (lead, TF-IDF centroid, TextRank) | Pretrained encoder–decoder or LLM |

### Scoring summaries with ROUGE

Lin (2004) introduced **ROUGE** (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation), which scores a candidate summary $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ produced by a system against one or more human-written **reference** summaries $\mathbf{y}$ by counting overlapping lexical units.

**ROUGE-N** counts overlapping $n$-grams (contiguous sequences of $n$ words). For each distinct $n$-gram $g$, let $\mathrm{cnt}_{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}(g)$ and $\mathrm{cnt}_{\mathbf{y}}(g)$ be its occurrence counts in the candidate and reference. The clipped overlap count is:

$$
\mathrm{hit}_n = \sum_{g} \min\big(\mathrm{cnt}_{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}(g),\ \mathrm{cnt}_{\mathbf{y}}(g)\big)
$$

Taking the $\min$ clips each $n$-gram's count so that repeating a word ten times in the candidate summary cannot earn more credit than the number of times it actually appears in the reference. From $\mathrm{hit}_n$ we compute precision, recall, and F1:

$$
P_n = \frac{\mathrm{hit}_n}{\text{number of } n\text{-grams in } \hat{\mathbf{y}}}, \qquad
R_n = \frac{\mathrm{hit}_n}{\text{number of } n\text{-grams in } \mathbf{y}}, \qquad
F_n = \frac{2 P_n R_n}{P_n + R_n}
$$

- **Precision** $P_n$: what fraction of the system's $n$-grams appear in the reference? Long, padded summaries get penalized on precision.
- **Recall** $R_n$: what fraction of the reference's $n$-grams did the system recover? Overly brief summaries get penalized on recall.
- **F1** $F_n$: the harmonic mean of $P_n$ and $R_n$, which is high only when both precision and recall are high.

Lin's original 2004 paper defined ROUGE primarily as a **recall** metric (hence "Recall-Oriented" in the name); modern papers almost always report **F1**. Whenever you read a ROUGE table, check whether the numbers are recall or F1.

**ROUGE-L** replaces contiguous $n$-grams with the **longest common subsequence** (LCS): the longest sequence of words that appear in both $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ **in the same relative order**, without needing to be adjacent. Writing $\mathrm{LCS}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}, \mathbf{y})$ for its length, we have $P = \mathrm{LCS} / |\hat{\mathbf{y}}|$ and $R = \mathrm{LCS} / |\mathbf{y}|$, combined into F1 as above. ROUGE-L rewards preserving word order without requiring exact adjacency. (Lin's original paper weighted precision and recall with a parameter $\beta$ set very large in DUC evaluations, effectively reducing to recall, and defined a sentence-level union LCS for multi-sentence summaries; our code cell below treats the whole summary as a single sequence for simplicity.)

**Hand calculation.** Reference: `"trời hôm nay rất đẹp"` ("the weather today is very nice", 5 syllables). Candidate: `"hôm nay trời đẹp"` ("today the weather is nice", 4 syllables).

- **ROUGE-1.** Four unigrams match (`trời`, `hôm`, `nay`, `đẹp`): $P_1 = 4/4 = 1$, $R_1 = 4/5 = 0.8$, $F_1 = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 0.8 / 1.8 \approx 0.889$.
- **ROUGE-2.** The reference has 4 bigrams (`trời hôm`, `hôm nay`, `nay rất`, `rất đẹp`) and the candidate has 3 (`hôm nay`, `nay trời`, `trời đẹp`). Only `hôm nay` matches: $P_2 = 1/3$, $R_2 = 1/4$, $F_2 = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{12} / \frac{7}{12} = 2/7 \approx 0.286$.
- **ROUGE-L.** The longest common subsequence is `"hôm nay đẹp"` of length 3 (`"trời"` sits before `"hôm nay"` in the reference but after it in the candidate, so it cannot join the same increasing subsequence): $P = 3/4$, $R = 3/5$, F1 $= 2 \cdot 0.45 / 1.35 \approx 0.667$.

Notice how swapping word order causes ROUGE-2 and ROUGE-L to drop sharply while leaving ROUGE-1 untouched.

### What ROUGE misses

- **Inverted roles.** Reference `"mèo đuổi chuột"` ("cat chases mouse") vs. candidate `"chuột đuổi mèo"` ("mouse chases cat"): ROUGE-1 is 1.000 even though the meaning is reversed (ROUGE-2 is 0, ROUGE-L is $1/3$).
- **Synonyms and paraphrases.** `"khai trương"` ("inaugurate") and `"mở"` ("open") mean the same thing here, but share zero characters and receive zero credit.
- **Factual accuracy.** Changing `"đầu tiên"` ("first") to `"thứ hai"` ("second") alters only two syllables; a factually wrong summary can easily outscore a faithful paraphrase on ROUGE, as the table in the Illustration section shows.
- **Tokenization sensitivity.** On Vietnamese, counting syllables (`"xe"`, `"buýt"`) vs. segmented compound words (`"xe_buýt"`) produces different numbers ([Tokenization](/en/nlp/tokenization/)). Never compare ROUGE scores computed with different tokenizers.

ROUGE is valuable for comparing systems quickly on the same benchmark under the same tokenizer—Lin (2004) showed that it correlates well with human rankings at the system level on DUC data—but it cannot replace human checks when factual faithfulness matters.

### Hallucination and faithfulness

A summary is **faithful** to its source document if every claim it makes is entailed by the source. Any unsupported or contradicted claim is a **hallucination**. Maynez et al. (2020) divide hallucinations into two categories:

- **Intrinsic hallucination:** the model manipulates or recombines information **present** in the source in a way that distorts the facts—for example, attaching a title to the wrong person, or turning `"first"` into `"second"`.
- **Extrinsic hallucination:** the model introduces information **absent** from the source—for example, adding a year or an organization that the article never mentioned.

Maynez et al. had human annotators judge one-sentence summaries generated for 500 articles from the XSum dataset. Over 70% of summaries across all neural systems contained hallucinations—mostly extrinsic—and over 90% of those extrinsic hallucinations were **factually wrong** rather than helpful background knowledge. Even more strikingly, 76.9% of XSum's *human reference summaries* contained hallucinations (73.1% extrinsic), because XSum was constructed by stripping the opening sentence off BBC articles and asking models to predict that sentence from the body; journalists often put background facts in the lead sentence that were never repeated in the body. Models trained on such pairs learn to fabricate extra details. Two encouraging findings from the same study: pretrained encoder–decoder models achieved both higher ROUGE and higher human faithfulness than non-pretrained seq2seq models, and automated **textual entailment** (NLI) classifiers correlated much better with human faithfulness judgments than ROUGE did.

Practical ways to reduce hallucination in summarization systems:

- **Cleaner training data:** filter out training pairs whose reference summary contains entities or facts absent from the source article.
- **Conservative decoding:** use beam search or greedy decoding rather than high-temperature sampling when factual precision matters.
- **Post-generation verification:** run a natural language inference (entailment) model or check that every number and named entity in the summary appears in the source.
- **Hybrid extract-then-abstract pipelines:** extract key sentences first and rewrite only from those sentences, or require an LLM to cite the source sentence for each claim.

### Limitations

- **Very long documents:** standard encoder–decoder models have a fixed context window; book-length inputs require chunk-by-chunk hierarchical summarization or long-context architectures.
- **Multi-document summarization:** synthesizing conflicting reports across dozens of articles introduces cross-document coreference and contradiction challenges beyond single-document summarization.
- **Evaluation:** ROUGE misses faithfulness and readability, and model-based evaluators (NLI classifiers or LLM-as-a-judge) bring their own biases; see the second caution box under Common pitfalls.

## Common pitfalls

:::caution[Using the same decoding strategy for every task]
Running high-temperature sampling to summarize a legal contract yields a different summary on every run and risks altering numbers or obligations. Conversely, running beam search for creative story writing produces flat, repetitive prose (28.94% of beam-16 continuations got stuck in repetition loops in Holtzman et al.'s experiments). Match the strategy to the regime: beam search or greedy decoding for directed generation (summarization, translation, extraction), and top-p or top-k sampling for open-ended generation. Always inspect a few dozen outputs before locking in parameters.
:::

:::caution[Assuming a high ROUGE score means the summary is factually true]
In our Illustration table, `"Đà Nẵng đóng tuyến xe buýt điện đầu tiên"` ("Da Nang closes its first electric bus route") scored 0.889 ROUGE-1—higher than a faithful paraphrase. ROUGE counts surface word overlap, not factual entailment. Use ROUGE to compare models on the same dataset with the same tokenizer, and pair it with entity/number verification, NLI entailment checks, and human spot checks whenever factual accuracy matters.
:::

:::caution[Setting the repetition penalty too high]
An overly aggressive `repetition_penalty` or a small `no_repeat_ngram_size` bans legitimate repetition. A summary about a company needs to repeat the company's name; a summary about electric buses may need `"xe buýt điện"` twice, which `no_repeat_ngram_size=3` forbids. Forced to avoid those tokens, the decoder may resort to awkward circumlocutions or substitute an incorrect term. Start near $\theta \approx 1.2$ (as in Keskar et al.) and inspect the output rather than cranking the penalty up blindly.
:::

## Summary and further reading

**Key takeaways:**

- Text generation runs an autoregressive loop: the model outputs next-token logits, and the decoding strategy selects a token. Greedy and beam search are stable but prone to repetition in open-ended text (neural text degeneration); pure sampling is diverse but wanders into the unreliable tail; top-k and top-p truncate that tail, with top-p adapting dynamically to model uncertainty. Repetition penalty divides positive logits (and multiplies negative logits) of already-seen tokens by $\theta$.
- Extractive summarization selects verbatim source sentences (lead-$k$, TF-IDF centroid cosine, TextRank): it cannot invent new sentences, but can feel disjointed. Abstractive summarization rewrites via pretrained encoder–decoder models (BART, T5, PEGASUS with Gap Sentences Generation) or instruction-tuned LLMs: fluent and compact, but vulnerable to hallucination.
- ROUGE-N counts clipped $n$-gram overlap ($\min$ of counts) and ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence; precision divides by candidate length, recall by reference length, and F1 is their harmonic mean. ROUGE is blind to synonyms and factual inversions.
- Intrinsic hallucinations distort facts present in the source; extrinsic hallucinations add unsupported facts. Maynez et al. found hallucinations in over 70% of one-sentence neural summaries on XSum. Mitigate them with cleaner training data, conservative decoding, NLI verification, and extract-then-abstract pipelines.
